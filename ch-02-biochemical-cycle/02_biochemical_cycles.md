# **1. Basic Concepts**

**Biogeochemical cycles** are natural processes through which chemical elements and compounds move between **biotic components** (living organisms) and **abiotic components** (air, water, soil and rocks).

Major cycles include the **carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur and water (hydrological) cycles**. The water cycle is predominantly physical, although living organisms also contribute to it.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reservoir** | A location or system in which a substance is stored |
| **Stock** | Quantity of a substance stored in a reservoir at a given time |
| **Flux** | Rate at which a substance moves between reservoirs |
| **Sink** | Reservoir that absorbs more of a substance than it releases over a specified period |
| **Source** | Reservoir or process that releases more than it absorbs over a specified period |

**Important distinction:** Nutrients and elements are recycled through biogeochemical cycles; energy flows through ecosystems and is ultimately dissipated as heat.

# **2. Carbon Cycle**

The **carbon cycle** is the movement of carbon among the **atmosphere, oceans, living organisms, soils and geological formations**. Carbon is a constituent of carbohydrates, proteins, fats and nucleic acids.

---

## **2.1 Major Carbon Reservoirs**

| Reservoir | Principal forms of carbon |
|---|---|
| **Atmosphere** | Carbon dioxide (CO₂); smaller amounts of methane (CH₄) |
| **Oceans** | Dissolved CO₂, bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻) and organic carbon |
| **Living organisms** | Organic compounds in biomass |
| **Soils** | Organic matter and inorganic carbon |
| **Fossil fuels** | Carbon in coal, petroleum and natural gas |
| **Sedimentary rocks** | Carbonates and buried organic carbon; major long-term stores |

---

## **2.2 Major Carbon-Transfer Processes**

The carbon cycle is a **network of simultaneous pathways**, not a single fixed sequence.

| Process | Movement of carbon | Important point |
|---|---|---|
| **Photosynthesis** | Inorganic CO₂ → organic carbon in producers | Plants, algae and some bacteria fix carbon using light |
| **Food-chain transfer** | Producers → consumers | Carbon passes through food as organic compounds |
| **Respiration** | Organic carbon → CO₂ | Plants, animals and microorganisms respire |
| **Decomposition** | Dead organic matter → CO₂, soil/water carbon and sometimes CH₄ | Bacteria and fungi recycle organic matter; methane may form under oxygen-deficient conditions |
| **Combustion** | Biomass/fossil carbon → atmospheric CO₂ | Fossil-fuel burning transfers geological carbon into the active cycle |
| **Ocean–atmosphere exchange** | CO₂ moves between air and seawater in both directions | Oceans can absorb CO₂ or release it by outgassing |
| **Sedimentation and burial** | Carbon in particles → sediments and rocks | Provides long-term storage |
| **Volcanic activity** | Geological carbon → atmospheric CO₂ | Releases some geological carbon |

**Photosynthesis (simplified):**

$$
6CO_2+6H_2O\xrightarrow{\text{light}}C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2
$$

**Aerobic respiration (simplified):**

$$
C_6H_{12}O_6+6O_2\rightarrow6CO_2+6H_2O+\text{energy}
$$

**Important distinctions:**

- Plants carry out **both photosynthesis and respiration**.
- Photosynthesis transfers carbon from inorganic CO₂ to organic compounds; it does not destroy carbon.
- **Anaerobic decomposition** may produce methane (CH₄), particularly through methanogenic microorganisms.
- Carbon buried in sedimentary rocks can remain stored over geological timescales.

---

## **2.3 Oceanic Carbon Uptake and Storage**

Oceans exchange CO₂ with the atmosphere in both directions but currently act as a **net sink for human-generated (anthropogenic) CO₂**. Two important mechanisms transfer carbon from surface waters to the ocean interior.

### **2.3.1 Solubility Pump**

1. Atmospheric CO₂ dissolves in surface seawater.
2. Dissolved CO₂ participates in carbonate chemistry, forming bicarbonate and carbonate ions.
3. Ocean circulation transports some dissolved inorganic carbon into deeper waters.

**Prelims fact:** Cold seawater generally dissolves more CO₂ than warm seawater, other conditions being equal.

![Solubility Pump](images/26_solubility_pump.png)

![Biological Carbon Pump](images/26_biological_carbon_pump.png)

### **2.3.2 Biological Carbon Pump**

1. **Phytoplankton** fix dissolved inorganic carbon through photosynthesis.
2. Organic carbon enters marine food chains.
3. Dead organisms, organic particles and faecal material sink.
4. Some carbon remains in deep waters or becomes buried in seabed sediments; much is decomposed and recycled before burial.

### **2.3.3 Solubility Pump vs Biological Carbon Pump**

| Feature | Solubility pump | Biological carbon pump |
|---|---|---|
| **Main mechanism** | Dissolution and ocean circulation | Photosynthesis, food-chain transfer and sinking particles |
| **Main transported form** | Dissolved inorganic carbon | Predominantly organic carbon |
| **Role of phytoplankton** | Not essential | Central |
| **Where carbon is stored** | Deep seawater | Deep seawater and some seabed sediments |

**Related concept — Upwelling:** Nutrient-rich, sometimes CO₂-rich deep water rises towards the surface. It can stimulate phytoplankton production but may also promote CO₂ outgassing.

---

## **2.4 Carbon Fixation and Sequestration**

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Carbon fixation** | Conversion of inorganic carbon into organic compounds, for example through photosynthesis |
| **Carbon sequestration** | Capture and storage of carbon in biological, oceanic or geological reservoirs; storage duration varies |

**Key distinction:** Carbon fixation does **not** automatically imply long-term sequestration. Some fixed carbon returns rapidly to the environment through respiration and decomposition.

**Examples of carbon storage:** Forest vegetation and soils, coastal sediments, deep ocean water and carbonate rocks. Sedimentary rocks are among Earth's largest long-term carbon reservoirs.

## **2.4.1 Blue Carbon and Other Carbon Terms**

| Term | Meaning / examples |
|---|---|
| **Blue carbon** | Carbon captured and stored by coastal and marine ecosystems, especially **mangroves, seagrass meadows and tidal salt marshes** |
| **Green carbon** | Carbon stored in terrestrial vegetation and associated soils, such as forests and grasslands |
| **Black carbon** | Light-absorbing carbonaceous particles from incomplete combustion, including diesel exhaust and biomass burning |
| **Brown carbon** | Light-absorbing organic aerosol particles, including those from biomass burning |
| **Grey carbon** | Context-dependent term sometimes associated with industrial carbon or emissions; **no single universally accepted definition** |

**Important:** Black carbon is an **air pollutant and climate-warming agent**, not a type of ecosystem carbon sequestration. Coastal blue-carbon ecosystems can store substantial carbon in waterlogged soils and sediments.

---

## **2.5 Carbon Fertilization Effect**

The **carbon fertilization effect** is the stimulation of plant photosynthesis and potential growth by increased atmospheric CO₂ concentrations.

Its actual effect depends on **water, nutrients, temperature and plant type**. Higher atmospheric CO₂ does **not** guarantee increased plant growth.

### **2.5.1 C₃ and C₄ Plants**

| Feature | C₃ plants | C₄ plants |
|---|---|---|
| **First stable carbon-fixation product** | 3-phosphoglycerate (three-carbon compound) | Oxaloacetate (four-carbon compound) |
| **Initial CO₂-fixing enzyme** | RuBisCO | PEP carboxylase |
| **Photorespiration** | Can be substantial under hot, dry conditions | Usually very low |
| **Direct photosynthetic response to elevated CO₂** | Generally stronger | Generally weaker |
| **Examples** | Rice, wheat, soybean | Maize, sugarcane, sorghum |

**Why?** C₄ plants already have a CO₂-concentrating mechanism. Elevated CO₂ may nevertheless improve their water-use efficiency.

**Photorespiration:** RuBisCO reacts with O₂ instead of CO₂, initiating reactions that release CO₂ and reduce the efficiency of carbon fixation.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### **2.5.2 Distinguishing Three CO₂-Related Effects**

| Effect | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Carbon fertilization** | Potential stimulation of photosynthesis and plant growth |
| **Global warming** | Rise in Earth's average temperature associated with enhanced greenhouse forcing |
| **Ocean acidification** | Decrease in ocean pH caused by absorption of atmospheric CO₂ |

# **3. Oxygen Cycle**

## **3.1 Basic Concepts**

The **oxygen cycle** is the movement of oxygen among the **atmosphere, water, living organisms, soils and rocks**. It involves both molecular oxygen and oxygen chemically combined in other substances.

- **Molecular oxygen (O₂):** About **21% of Earth's dry atmosphere by volume**; also dissolved in water.
- **Ozone (O₃):** A form of oxygen especially important in the stratospheric ozone layer.
- **Combined oxygen:** Oxygen present in water (H₂O), carbon dioxide (CO₂), organic compounds and minerals.

---

## **3.2 Major Processes of the Oxygen Cycle**

| Process | What happens to oxygen? | Important point |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxygenic photosynthesis** | Releases O₂ formed from water | Plants, algae and cyanobacteria |
| **Aerobic respiration** | Consumes O₂ | Plants, animals and many microorganisms |
| **Aerobic decomposition** | Consumes O₂ | Microorganisms use oxygen while breaking down dead organic matter |
| **Combustion** | Consumes O₂ | Burning fuels and biomass produces oxygen-containing compounds |
| **Chemical oxidation** | Incorporates oxygen into compounds | Example: oxidation of iron |
| **Atmosphere–water exchange** | Transfers O₂ in both directions | Dissolution into water and release into air |
| **Ozone formation and breakdown** | Interconverts O₂, O₃ and atomic oxygen | Stratospheric ozone absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation |

**Key facts:**

- The O₂ released by **oxygenic photosynthesis originates from the splitting of water (H₂O)**, not directly from CO₂.
- Plants **photosynthesize and respire**. Photosynthesis requires light; respiration occurs both day and night.
- Decomposition can also occur **anaerobically**, without consuming molecular oxygen.
- The photosynthesis and aerobic respiration equations are given in **Section 2.2**; they are not repeated here.

**Other reactions:**

- **Complete combustion of carbon:** $C+O_2\rightarrow CO_2$.
- **Oxidation of iron (simplified):** $4Fe+3O_2\rightarrow 2Fe_2O_3$.
- **Ozone transformations:** UV radiation can split O₂ into oxygen atoms; an oxygen atom can combine with O₂ to form O₃, which also undergoes photochemical breakdown. These reactions involve additional collision partners in the atmosphere.

![Oxygen Cycle](images/20_oxygen_cycle.png)

---

## **3.3 Origin and Production of Atmospheric Oxygen**

Earth's oxygen-rich atmosphere developed primarily through **oxygenic photosynthesis**, including that performed by ancient **cyanobacteria**. The **Great Oxidation Event**, approximately **2.4 billion years ago**, marked a major increase in atmospheric oxygen.

- Marine phytoplankton and other marine photosynthetic microorganisms contribute **approximately half of global gross photosynthetic oxygen production**.
- Terrestrial vegetation produces much of the remainder.
- Much of the oxygen produced is subsequently consumed through respiration, decomposition and chemical oxidation.
- **Gross oxygen production is not the same as net atmospheric oxygen accumulation.**
- Without sustained biological oxygen production over geological timescales, atmospheric O₂ would decline substantially.

**Prelims distinction:** Tropical rainforests are productive ecosystems, but do **not** produce more oxygen than the world's oceans collectively.

---

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

## **3.4 Dissolved Oxygen and Aquatic Life**

**Dissolved oxygen (DO)** is molecular oxygen dissolved in water. Fish and many other aquatic organisms use it for aerobic respiration.

**Sources of DO:**

1. **Atmospheric exchange:** O₂ dissolves from air into water.
2. **Aquatic photosynthesis:** Aquatic plants, algae and cyanobacteria release O₂ into water.

| Factor | Effect on dissolved oxygen |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Cold water generally holds more O₂ than warm water, other conditions being equal |
| **Salinity** | Increasing salinity generally reduces O₂ solubility |
| **Photosynthesis** | Can increase DO during daylight |
| **Respiration and decomposition** | Consume DO; excessive decomposition may cause **hypoxia** (low DO) |
| **Aeration and mixing** | Can replenish DO |

**Day and night:** During daylight, photosynthesis and respiration can occur simultaneously; if photosynthesis exceeds respiration, there is net O₂ release. In darkness, photosynthesis stops but respiration continues.

**Air vs water:** Air contains approximately **21% O₂ by volume**, whereas well-oxygenated surface seawater commonly contains around **5–9 mg O₂/L**, depending on conditions. These figures use different units; **water contains much less oxygen per unit volume than air**.

---

## **3.5 Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) and Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)**

BOD and COD are **indicators of oxygen demand and water pollution**, not direct measurements of the oxygen already dissolved in water. Both are commonly expressed as **mg O₂/L**.

| Feature | BOD | COD |
|---|---|---|
| **Full form** | Biochemical Oxygen Demand | Chemical Oxygen Demand |
| **Meaning** | Oxygen consumed by aerobic microorganisms while decomposing biodegradable organic matter under specified test conditions | Oxygen equivalent required to chemically oxidize substances in a water sample |
| **Method** | Biological decomposition | Strong chemical oxidizing agent |
| **Main indication** | Biodegradable organic pollution | Chemically oxidizable pollution, including many non-biodegradable substances |
| **Typical test** | **BOD₅:** 5 days at 20°C | Usually completed within a few hours |
| **Typical relationship** | Generally lower than COD | Generally higher than BOD |

**Examples:** Untreated sewage commonly has high BOD because microorganisms consume oxygen while decomposing organic wastes. Industrial wastewater may have high COD even when some pollutants are not readily biodegradable.

**DO vs BOD vs COD:**

- **DO:** Oxygen **present** in water.
- **BOD:** Oxygen **consumed in biological decomposition** under the test conditions.
- **COD:** Oxygen **equivalent required for chemical oxidation**.

Water heavily polluted with biodegradable organic matter commonly has **high BOD and reduced DO**. However, DO also depends on temperature, mixing, photosynthesis and other conditions. **High BOD does not establish an exact DO concentration.**

**Classification note:** BOD and COD are primarily water-quality and pollution indicators. They are included here because of their close relationship with dissolved oxygen; they can be cross-referenced, rather than fully repeated, in the Environmental Pollution chapter.

# **4. Nitrogen Cycle**

The **nitrogen cycle** is the movement and chemical transformation of nitrogen among the **atmosphere, soil, water and living organisms**. Nitrogen is an essential component of **amino acids, proteins, DNA and RNA**.

- **Atmospheric reservoir:** Nitrogen gas (N₂) makes up approximately **78% of Earth's dry atmosphere by volume**.
- **Availability:** Most plants and animals cannot use atmospheric N₂ directly because of its strong triple bond.
- **Plant uptake:** Plants generally absorb nitrogen from soil as **ammonium (NH₄⁺)** or **nitrate (NO₃⁻)**.
- **Animal uptake:** Animals obtain nitrogen through food.

**Important:** The nitrogen cycle consists of interconnected pathways, **not one compulsory sequence**.

---

## **4.1 Important Forms of Nitrogen**

| Form | Formula | Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Molecular nitrogen** | N₂ | Principal atmospheric nitrogen reservoir |
| **Ammonia** | NH₃ | Produced during biological fixation and ammonification |
| **Ammonium** | NH₄⁺ | Plant-available inorganic nitrogen |
| **Nitrite** | NO₂⁻ | Intermediate in nitrification and denitrification |
| **Nitrate** | NO₃⁻ | Plant-available nitrogen; can undergo leaching or denitrification |
| **Organic nitrogen** | In proteins, nucleic acids, etc. | Nitrogen incorporated into organisms and organic matter |
| **Nitrous oxide** | N₂O | Potent greenhouse gas associated with microbial nitrogen transformations |

**Ammonia and ammonium are not identical.** Ammonia can accept a hydrogen ion to form ammonium:

$$
\mathrm{NH_3+H^+\rightleftharpoons NH_4^+}
$$

In most ordinary soils, the dissolved ammonia–ammonium pool is predominantly present as ammonium.

---

## **4.2 Major Processes of the Nitrogen Cycle**

### **4.2.1 Nitrogen Fixation — Atmospheric N₂ to Usable Nitrogen**

**Nitrogen fixation** converts atmospheric N₂ into chemically usable nitrogen compounds. Its three main routes are **biological, atmospheric and industrial fixation**.

#### **A. Biological Nitrogen Fixation**

Certain microorganisms use the enzyme **nitrogenase** to convert atmospheric N₂ into ammonia (NH₃). The process requires energy. **Nitrogenase is oxygen-sensitive**, so nitrogen-fixing organisms have mechanisms to protect it.

##### **Nitrogen-Fixing Microorganisms**

| Association | Examples | Important point |
|---|---|---|
| **Symbiotic rhizobia** | *Rhizobium* and related bacteria | Form nitrogen-fixing root nodules in many legumes |
| **Free-living bacteria** | *Azotobacter*, *Clostridium* | Fix nitrogen without forming legume root nodules |
| **Cyanobacteria** | *Anabaena*, *Nostoc* | Some species fix N₂, either free-living or in associations |
| **Actinorhizal symbiosis** | *Frankia* | Associates with certain non-leguminous plants |

##### **Nitrogen-Fixing Plants and Plant Associations**

**The microorganism fixes atmospheric nitrogen; the plant provides nutrients and a suitable environment.** The association must be effective: not every individual plant fixes nitrogen under every condition.

| Plant group | Important examples | Nitrogen-fixing partner |
|---|---|---|
| **Legumes (Fabaceae)** | Alfalfa (lucerne), clover, chickpea (gram), pea, soybean, groundnut, lentil, pigeon pea (arhar), green gram (moong), black gram (urad), cowpea (lobia), *Sesbania* (dhaincha) | Rhizobia, usually in root nodules |
| **Non-leguminous actinorhizal plants** | Alder (*Alnus*), *Casuarina*, sea buckthorn (*Hippophae*), sweet fern (*Comptonia*) | *Frankia* |
| **Aquatic fern** | *Azolla* | Nitrogen-fixing cyanobacterium traditionally called *Anabaena azollae* (also classified as *Trichormus azollae*) |
| **Gymnosperm** | *Cycas* | Cyanobacteria, including *Nostoc*, in coralloid roots |

**Important Prelims distinctions:**

- **Azolla is an aquatic fern**, not a cyanobacterium. Its cyanobacterial symbiont fixes nitrogen; Azolla is used as a **biofertilizer in paddy cultivation**.
- **Cycas is a gymnosperm** with **coralloid roots** that host nitrogen-fixing cyanobacteria.
- **Not all nitrogen-fixing plant associations involve legumes**: *Frankia* forms associations with certain non-leguminous plants.
- **Amaranth, spinach and purslane (kulfa)** are not ordinarily classified as symbiotic nitrogen-fixing plants.

#### **B. Atmospheric Nitrogen Fixation**

**Lightning** supplies energy for atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen to react, forming nitrogen oxides. Subsequent atmospheric reactions produce nitrate-containing compounds that reach land and water through precipitation.

$$
\mathrm{N_2+O_2\rightarrow 2NO}
$$

#### **C. Industrial Nitrogen Fixation**

The **Haber–Bosch process** combines nitrogen and hydrogen to produce ammonia, widely used in fertilizer manufacture.

$$
\mathrm{N_2+3H_2\rightleftharpoons 2NH_3}
$$

### **4.2.2 Nitrification — Ammonia/Ammonium to Nitrate**

**Nitrification** is the microbial **oxidation** of ammonia/ammonium first to nitrite and then to nitrate. It generally occurs under **aerobic (oxygen-available) conditions**.

| Stage | Transformation | Representative microorganisms |
|---|---|---|
| **Ammonia oxidation** | NH₃/NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ | *Nitrosomonas*; ammonia-oxidizing archaea also contribute |
| **Nitrite oxidation** | NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ | *Nitrobacter*, *Nitrospira* |

**Overall ionic reaction:**

$$
\mathrm{NH_4^++2O_2\rightarrow NO_3^-+2H^++H_2O}
$$

- Nitrification produces **nitrate**, which plants can absorb.
- It releases **H⁺**, which can contribute to **soil acidification**.
- **Nitrification is not required before plant uptake**: plants can absorb ammonium directly.

### **4.2.3 Assimilation — Inorganic Nitrogen to Organic Nitrogen**

**Assimilation** is the incorporation of inorganic nitrogen into **organic compounds**, including amino acids, proteins and nucleic acids.

1. Plants absorb **NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻** from soil.
2. Absorbed nitrate is reduced within plants before its nitrogen is incorporated into organic compounds.
3. Animals obtain organic nitrogen by eating plants or other animals.

**Example:** Soil NH₄⁺/NO₃⁻ → Grass → Deer → Tiger.

**Distinction:** Nitrification produces nitrate from ammonium; **assimilation incorporates inorganic nitrogen into biomass**.

### **4.2.4 Ammonification — Organic Nitrogen to Ammonia/Ammonium**

**Ammonification** is the microbial conversion of organic nitrogen from **dead organisms and wastes** into **ammonia/ammonium** during decomposition.

- Decomposers include **bacteria and fungi**.
- Released NH₄⁺ may be **absorbed by plants**, **nitrified** or used in other microbial processes.
- There is **no universal equation** for ammonification because organic nitrogen occurs in many different compounds.

**Example — urea hydrolysis:**

$$
\mathrm{CO(NH_2)_2+H_2O\rightarrow 2NH_3+CO_2}
$$

This is an example of ammonia production from organic nitrogen, **not a universal equation for all ammonification**.

### **4.2.5 Denitrification — Nitrate/Nitrite to Gaseous Nitrogen**

**Denitrification** is the microbial **reduction** of nitrate or nitrite into gaseous nitrogen compounds. **Complete denitrification produces N₂**, returning fixed nitrogen to the atmosphere.

$$
\mathrm{NO_3^-\rightarrow NO_2^-\rightarrow NO\rightarrow N_2O\rightarrow N_2}
$$

*This represents successive transformations, not a balanced chemical equation.*

- **Conditions:** Commonly occurs where oxygen is scarce, such as **waterlogged soils** and **oxygen-poor sediments**.
- **Microorganisms:** Certain species of *Pseudomonas* and *Paracoccus*.
- **Effect:** Reduces nitrate available in soil and can return nitrogen to the atmosphere.
- **Climate relevance:** Denitrification can release **nitrous oxide (N₂O)**, a potent greenhouse gas, particularly when reduction to N₂ is incomplete.

**Important:** Plant assimilation and microbial denitrification are **alternative fates of nitrate**, not compulsory consecutive steps.

### **4.2.6 Anammox — Anaerobic Ammonium Oxidation**

**Anammox** is a microbial process in which **ammonium (NH₄⁺)** is oxidized using **nitrite (NO₂⁻)** under **anaerobic (oxygen-free) conditions**, producing nitrogen gas.

**Simplified overall reaction:**

$$
\mathrm{NH_4^++NO_2^-\rightarrow N_2+2H_2O}
$$

- Performed by specialized **anammox bacteria**.
- Occurs in **oxygen-free aquatic sediments**, anaerobic zones of water bodies and some wastewater-treatment systems.
- Provides another pathway for returning fixed nitrogen to the atmosphere.

**Denitrification versus anammox:**

| Feature | Denitrification | Anammox |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting nitrogen forms** | NO₃⁻ or NO₂⁻ | NH₄⁺ and NO₂⁻ together |
| **Main transformation** | Reduction of nitrate/nitrite | Oxidation of ammonium coupled with reduction of nitrite |
| **Final product** | N₂ when complete | N₂ |
| **Conditions** | Commonly oxygen-deficient | Anaerobic |

## **4.3 Comparison of Major Nitrogen-Cycle Processes**

| Process | Starting nitrogen form | Main result | Key distinction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen fixation** | Atmospheric N₂ | NH₃ or other fixed nitrogen compounds | Makes atmospheric nitrogen chemically usable |
| **Nitrification** | NH₃/NH₄⁺ | NO₃⁻ through NO₂⁻ | Generally **aerobic oxidation** |
| **Assimilation** | NH₄⁺/NO₃⁻ | Organic nitrogen | Incorporates nitrogen into biomass |
| **Ammonification** | Organic nitrogen | NH₃/NH₄⁺ | Recycles nitrogen from dead matter and wastes |
| **Denitrification** | NO₃⁻/NO₂⁻ | N₂ when complete | Generally favoured by **low oxygen** |
| **Anammox** | NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ | N₂ | **Anaerobic** ammonium oxidation |

**How the pathways connect:** Fixation introduces atmospheric nitrogen into usable forms. Plants absorb ammonium directly or nitrate produced through nitrification; animals obtain nitrogen through feeding. Ammonification returns organic nitrogen to the ammonia–ammonium pool. Denitrification and anammox return some fixed nitrogen to atmospheric N₂. **The processes operate simultaneously; there is no compulsory linear sequence.**

# **5. Phosphorus Cycle**

The **phosphorus cycle** is the movement of phosphorus among **rocks, soil, water and living organisms**. It is primarily a **sedimentary biogeochemical cycle**, with **no major atmospheric gaseous phase**.

Phosphorus is essential for:

- **DNA and RNA:** Genetic material.
- **ATP (adenosine triphosphate):** Cellular energy transfer.
- **Phospholipids:** Components of cell membranes.
- **Bones and teeth:** Contain calcium phosphate minerals.

---

## **5.1 Major Reservoirs and Available Forms**

| Feature | Important fact |
|---|---|
| Major long-term reservoirs | Phosphate-bearing rocks and sediments |
| Forms absorbed by plants | Inorganic phosphate ions, mainly **H₂PO₄⁻** and **HPO₄²⁻** |
| Atmospheric phase | **No major gaseous phase** in the natural cycle |
| Important ecological feature | Phosphorus availability can limit biological productivity, particularly in many freshwater ecosystems |

---

## **5.2 Major Processes of the Phosphorus Cycle**

### **5.2.1 Weathering — Release of Phosphate from Rocks**

- Phosphorus occurs in rocks and sediments, commonly in phosphate minerals.
- Physical and chemical **weathering** releases phosphorus into soil and water.
- Only a portion of the released phosphorus remains dissolved and readily available to organisms; some becomes bound to soil particles or minerals.

**Distinction:** Weathering releases phosphate from exposed rocks; geological uplift merely brings buried rocks closer to, or onto, Earth's surface.

### **5.2.2 Plant Uptake — Entry into Living Organisms**

- Plant roots absorb **inorganic phosphate**, mainly **H₂PO₄⁻** and **HPO₄²⁻**, from soil water.
- Plants incorporate phosphorus into DNA, RNA, ATP and phospholipids.

### **5.2.3 Food-Chain Transfer — Movement to Animals**

- Herbivores obtain phosphorus by consuming plants; carnivores obtain it by consuming other animals.
- Phosphorus becomes part of animal tissues, including bones and teeth.

**Example:** Phosphate in soil → grass → deer → tiger.

### **5.2.4 Decomposition and Mineralization — Return to Soil and Water**

- Dead organisms and phosphorus-containing wastes supply **organic phosphorus** to soil and water.
- Decomposers, including bacteria and fungi, break down organic matter.
- **Mineralization** converts organic phosphorus into **inorganic phosphate**, which can be taken up again by organisms.

### **5.2.5 Runoff and Sedimentation — Transfer to Aquatic Sediments**

- Runoff transports dissolved phosphate and phosphorus attached to soil particles into rivers, lakes and oceans.
- Aquatic organisms use some of this phosphorus; some is deposited and buried in sediments.
- Over geological timescales, buried phosphorus-containing sediments may become sedimentary rocks.

**Important:** Not all phosphorus entering water bodies is buried; some continues to circulate within aquatic ecosystems.

### **5.2.6 Geological Uplift — Exposure of Phosphorus-Bearing Rocks**

- Tectonic processes may uplift phosphorus-bearing sedimentary rocks over geological timescales.
- Once exposed, these rocks undergo weathering, releasing phosphate into soil and water.

**Important:** Uplift itself does **not** directly make phosphorus available to plants; subsequent weathering is required.

---

## **5.3 Biological and Geological Pathways**

| Pathway | Main movement | Typical timescale |
|---|---|---|
| **Biological (short-term)** | Soil/water phosphate → plants → animals → decomposers → soil/water phosphate | Generally much faster |
| **Geological (long-term)** | Weathering → transport to water bodies → sediment burial → rock formation → uplift → weathering | Generally much slower; often geological timescales |

![Phosphorus Cycle](images/21_phosphorous_cycle.png)

---

## **5.4 Important UPSC Prelims Distinctions**

- The phosphorus cycle is primarily **sedimentary**, unlike cycles with substantial atmospheric gaseous reservoirs, such as carbon and nitrogen.
- **Rocks and sediments** are the principal long-term phosphorus reservoirs.
- Plants take up **phosphate ions**, not atmospheric phosphorus gas.
- **Mineralization** converts organic phosphorus into inorganic phosphate; **plant uptake** incorporates inorganic phosphate into biomass.
- Phosphorus is often a **limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems**; excessive phosphorus inputs can cause **eutrophication**, including algal blooms and subsequent dissolved-oxygen depletion.
- **Geological uplift followed by weathering**, rather than uplift alone, helps return phosphorus from sedimentary rocks to the biologically active environment.

---

# **6. Sulphur Cycle**

The sulphur cycle is the circulation and chemical transformation of sulphur among rocks, soil, water, the atmosphere and living organisms.

Sulphur is an essential component of the amino acids cysteine and methionine, which are required for protein synthesis.

Unlike the phosphorus cycle, the sulphur cycle has a significant atmospheric component.

The sulphur cycle consists of interconnected biological, microbial, atmospheric and geological pathways rather than one compulsory sequence.

---

## **6.1 Important Forms of Sulphur**

| Form              | Chemical formula                    | Significance                                                                      |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sulphate          | SO₄²⁻                               | Main form absorbed by plants; abundant in seawater                                |
| Hydrogen sulphide | H₂S                                 | Found in oxygen-deficient soils and sediments; also released by volcanic activity |
| Sulphur dioxide   | SO₂                                 | Released by volcanoes and combustion of sulphur-containing fuels                  |
| Elemental sulphur | S                                   | Occurs in natural deposits and certain microbial environments                     |
| Organic sulphur   | Present in amino acids and proteins | Incorporated into living organisms and organic matter                             |

Major reservoirs: Sulphur occurs in rocks, minerals, sediments and oceans. Seawater contains a large reservoir of dissolved sulphate.

---

## **6.2 Major Processes of the Sulphur Cycle**

### **6.2.1 Weathering — Release of Sulphur from Rocks**

- Sulphur is stored in rocks as sulphide and sulphate minerals.
- Weathering breaks down or chemically alters these minerals, releasing sulphur compounds into soil and water.
- Some of the released sulphur becomes available as sulphate (SO₄²⁻), which plants can absorb.

Important: Sulphide minerals may require oxidation before their sulphur becomes available as sulphate.

### **6.2.2 Assimilation and Food-Chain Transfer — Inorganic to Organic Sulphur**

Assimilation is the incorporation of inorganic sulphur into organic compounds within living organisms.

1. Plants absorb sulphur mainly as sulphate (SO₄²⁻) through their roots.
2. They incorporate sulphur into organic compounds, including cysteine and methionine.
3. Animals obtain sulphur by consuming plants or other animals.

Example: Soil sulphate → Grass → Deer → Tiger.

Distinction: Assimilation incorporates sulphur into biomass, whereas food-chain transfer moves organic sulphur between organisms.

### **6.2.3 Decomposition and Mineralization — Organic to Inorganic Sulphur**

Decomposition breaks down dead organisms and sulphur-containing wastes, while mineralization converts organic sulphur into inorganic forms.

- Decomposer microorganisms include bacteria and fungi.
- Depending on environmental conditions, inorganic sulphur may be released as sulphate or reduced sulphur compounds.
- The released sulphur may undergo further microbial transformations or become available for plant uptake.

### **6.2.4 Sulphate Reduction — Sulphate to Reduced Sulphur**

Sulphate reduction is the microbial conversion of sulphate into reduced sulphur compounds, commonly hydrogen sulphide.

Simplified transformation:

$$
\mathrm{SO_4^{2-}\longrightarrow H_2S}
$$

This represents the transformation, not a balanced chemical equation.

- Conditions: Generally anaerobic (oxygen-free).
- Locations: Waterlogged soils, oxygen-free aquatic sediments and other anaerobic environments.
- Microorganisms: Sulphate-reducing bacteria, such as Desulfovibrio.

Certain microorganisms use sulphate as an electron acceptor during anaerobic respiration, producing reduced sulphur compounds.

### **6.2.5 Sulphur Oxidation — Reduced Sulphur to Oxidized Sulphur**

Sulphur oxidation converts reduced sulphur compounds, such as hydrogen sulphide or elemental sulphur, into more oxidized forms, often sulphate.

Simplified transformation:

$$
\mathrm{H_2S\\;/\\;S\longrightarrow SO_4^{2-}}
$$

This represents the transformation, not a balanced chemical equation.

- Conditions: Commonly aerobic, although some microorganisms can oxidize sulphur under anaerobic conditions using alternative electron acceptors.
- Microorganisms: Certain species of Thiobacillus and Acidithiobacillus.
- Importance: Produces sulphate that may again become available for plant uptake.

Sulphate reduction vs sulphur oxidation:

| Feature               | Sulphate reduction          | Sulphur oxidation                   |
| --------------------- | --------------------------- | ----------------------------------- |
| Starting form         | SO₄²⁻                       | H₂S or elemental sulphur            |
| Main transformation   | Sulphate to reduced sulphur | Reduced sulphur to oxidized sulphur |
| Typical product       | H₂S                         | SO₄²⁻                               |
| Conditions            | Generally anaerobic         | Commonly aerobic                    |
| Example microorganism | Desulfovibrio               | Thiobacillus, Acidithiobacillus     |

### **6.2.6 Atmospheric Emissions — Sulphur Enters the Atmosphere**

Sulphur enters the atmosphere through natural processes and human activities.

| Source                    | Important sulphur compounds                    |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |
| Volcanic activity         | SO₂ and H₂S                                    |
| Biological processes      | H₂S and dimethyl sulphide (DMS)                |
| Fossil-fuel combustion    | SO₂ from sulphur-containing coal and petroleum |
| Smelting of sulphide ores | SO₂                                            |

Important: Marine biological processes release dimethyl sulphide (DMS), an important natural source of atmospheric sulphur.

### **6.2.7 Atmospheric Reactions and Deposition — Sulphur Returns to Earth's Surface**

- Atmospheric SO₂ undergoes chemical reactions, producing sulphuric acid and sulphate-containing particles.
- These compounds return to land and water through wet deposition and dry deposition.

| Type           | Meaning                                                                           |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Wet deposition | Removal of atmospheric sulphur compounds through rain, snow or fog                |
| Dry deposition | Direct deposition or uptake of sulphur-containing gases and particles by surfaces |

Acid rain: Atmospheric SO₂ can undergo oxidation and subsequent reactions that form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), contributing to acid deposition.

---

## **6.3 Summary of Major Sulphur-Cycle Processes**

| Process                          | Starting form                 | Main result                         | Key distinction                       |
| -------------------------------- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Weathering                       | Sulphur-bearing rocks         | Sulphur compounds in soil and water | Releases sulphur from rocks           |
| Assimilation                     | SO₄²⁻                         | Organic sulphur                     | Incorporates sulphur into biomass     |
| Decomposition and mineralization | Organic sulphur               | Inorganic sulphur compounds         | Recycles sulphur from organic matter  |
| Sulphate reduction               | SO₄²⁻                         | Reduced sulphur, commonly H₂S       | Generally anaerobic                   |
| Sulphur oxidation                | H₂S or elemental sulphur      | Oxidized sulphur, often SO₄²⁻       | Commonly aerobic                      |
| Atmospheric emissions            | Sulphur-containing materials  | SO₂, H₂S, DMS and other gases       | Transfers sulphur into the atmosphere |
| Atmospheric deposition           | Atmospheric sulphur compounds | Sulphur deposited on land and water | Returns sulphur to Earth's surface    |

How the pathways connect: Weathering releases sulphur into soil and water. Plants assimilate sulphate, and animals obtain sulphur through food chains. Decomposition returns sulphur to inorganic forms. Microorganisms carry out sulphate reduction and sulphur oxidation, while atmospheric emissions and deposition transfer sulphur between the Earth's surface and atmosphere.

Sulphur also reaches oceans through runoff, and some becomes buried in sediments over geological timescales. These pathways operate simultaneously; no single sequence is mandatory.

---

## **6.4 UPSC Prelims: Key Distinctions**

- Cysteine and methionine are sulphur-containing amino acids.
- Sulphate (SO₄²⁻) is the principal form of sulphur absorbed by plants.
- The sulphur cycle has a significant atmospheric component, unlike the phosphorus cycle.
- Sulphate reduction is generally anaerobic; sulphur oxidation is commonly aerobic.
- Desulfovibrio is associated with sulphate reduction; certain Thiobacillus and Acidithiobacillus species are associated with sulphur oxidation.
- Volcanoes, fossil-fuel combustion and sulphide-ore smelting can release atmospheric SO₂.
- Dimethyl sulphide (DMS) is an important sulphur-containing gas released through marine biological processes.
- Atmospheric SO₂ contributes to acid deposition through reactions that form sulphuric acid.
- Oceans contain a large reservoir of dissolved sulphate.
- Sulphur occurs in multiple oxidation states, allowing microbial oxidation and reduction.

# **7. Water Cycle (Hydrological Cycle)**

The water cycle, also called the hydrological cycle, is the continuous circulation of water among the oceans, land, atmosphere and living organisms.

Unlike the carbon, nitrogen and sulphur cycles, the water cycle primarily involves physical movement and changes in the state of water, rather than its conversion into different chemical compounds.

- Major reservoir: Oceans contain approximately 97% of Earth's water.
- Principal energy source: Solar energy drives evaporation.
- Role of gravity: Drives precipitation, surface runoff and much of the downward movement of water.

The water cycle consists of interconnected atmospheric, surface and underground pathways rather than a single compulsory sequence.

---

## **7.1 Important Forms and Reservoirs of Water**

| Reservoir                  | Form of water                                  | Significance                                       |
| -------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Oceans                     | Liquid water                                   | Largest reservoir of Earth's water                 |
| Glaciers and ice caps      | Ice                                            | Largest reservoir of Earth's freshwater            |
| Groundwater                | Liquid water                                   | Major freshwater reservoir beneath Earth's surface |
| Rivers, lakes and wetlands | Liquid water                                   | Surface freshwater storage                         |
| Atmosphere                 | Water vapour, liquid droplets and ice crystals | Involved in cloud formation and precipitation      |
| Soil and living organisms  | Soil moisture and biological water             | Supports plant growth and biological processes     |

Important: Most of Earth's freshwater is stored in glaciers, ice caps and groundwater. Only a small fraction occurs in rivers and lakes.

---

## **7.2 Major Processes of the Water Cycle**

### **7.2.1 Evaporation — Liquid Water to Water Vapour**

Evaporation is the conversion of liquid water into water vapour.

- Solar energy causes water to evaporate from oceans, lakes, rivers and other water bodies.
- Evaporation also occurs from moist soil and wet surfaces.
- Water vapour enters the atmosphere and contributes to atmospheric moisture.

Important: Solar energy is the principal source of energy driving evaporation.

### **7.2.2 Transpiration — Release of Water Vapour by Plants**

Transpiration is the release of water vapour from plants, mainly through stomata (tiny pores on leaves).

1. Roots absorb water from the soil.
2. Water moves through the plant to its leaves.
3. Water evaporates from internal leaf surfaces and escapes through stomata.

Evapotranspiration is the combined transfer of water to the atmosphere through evaporation and plant transpiration.

Distinction: Evaporation occurs from water bodies, soil and other wet surfaces, whereas transpiration involves water loss from plants.

### **7.2.3 Condensation — Water Vapour to Liquid Water**

Condensation is the conversion of water vapour into liquid water.

- Moist air rising through the atmosphere generally cools as it expands.
- When sufficiently cooled, water vapour condenses onto tiny atmospheric particles called cloud condensation nuclei.
- The resulting droplets can contribute to cloud formation.

Important: Clouds may contain liquid water droplets, ice crystals or both.

### **7.2.4 Precipitation — Water Returns to Earth's Surface**

Precipitation is the transfer of water from the atmosphere to Earth's surface in liquid or solid form.

It occurs when cloud droplets or ice particles grow sufficiently large to fall under gravity.

Major forms:

- Rain
- Snow
- Sleet
- Hail

Precipitation returns atmospheric water to oceans, rivers, lakes and land.

### **7.2.5 Infiltration — Water Enters the Soil**

Infiltration is the process by which water at the ground surface enters the soil.

- Some precipitation enters the soil through pores and openings.
- Part of the infiltrated water remains as soil moisture, available for plant uptake.
- Some water moves deeper into the ground.

Factors affecting infiltration: Soil texture and structure, vegetation, rainfall intensity, existing soil moisture and land cover.

### **7.2.6 Percolation — Downward Movement of Water**

Percolation is the movement of water through soil and permeable geological materials, particularly its downward movement into deeper layers.

- Some infiltrated water moves downward under gravity.
- Water reaching the saturated zone may replenish groundwater.

This replenishment is called groundwater recharge.

Infiltration vs Percolation:

| Feature      | Infiltration                              | Percolation                                            |
| ------------ | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Meaning      | Entry of water into soil from the surface | Movement of water through soil and permeable materials |
| Location     | At the ground surface                     | Within soil and underlying materials                   |
| Significance | Supplies soil moisture                    | Can contribute to groundwater recharge                 |

Important: Not all infiltrated water reaches groundwater. Some remains in the soil, is absorbed by plants or returns to the atmosphere through evaporation.

### **7.2.7 Surface Runoff — Water Flows Over Land**

Surface runoff is the flow of water over the land surface.

- Runoff can occur when rainfall exceeds the soil's infiltration capacity or when the soil becomes saturated.
- Water flows downslope into streams, rivers, lakes and eventually oceans.

Factors affecting runoff: Rainfall intensity, slope, vegetation, soil permeability and land use.

Important: Impervious surfaces, such as roads and buildings, generally reduce infiltration and increase surface runoff.

### **7.2.8 Groundwater Flow — Underground Movement of Water**

Groundwater is water present in the saturated pores and fractures of soil and rocks.

- Groundwater moves through permeable geological formations in response to differences in hydraulic head.
- It may discharge into springs, rivers, lakes or oceans.
- Groundwater is also extracted through wells.

Baseflow: The contribution of groundwater to stream or river flow, helping maintain river discharge during periods without rainfall.

Important: Groundwater movement is generally much slower than surface runoff, although its speed varies with geological conditions.

---

## **7.3 Summary of Major Water-Cycle Processes**

| Process          | Main transformation or movement                      | Key distinction                               |
| ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| Evaporation      | Liquid water → Water vapour                          | Driven mainly by solar energy                 |
| Transpiration    | Water from plants → Water vapour                     | Occurs mainly through stomata                 |
| Condensation     | Water vapour → Liquid droplets                       | Important in cloud formation                  |
| Precipitation    | Atmospheric water → Earth's surface                  | Includes rain, snow, sleet and hail           |
| Infiltration     | Surface water → Soil                                 | Entry of water into the ground                |
| Percolation      | Water moves through soil and permeable materials     | Can contribute to groundwater recharge        |
| Surface runoff   | Water flows over land                                | Transfers water into streams and water bodies |
| Groundwater flow | Water moves through saturated underground formations | Can sustain river baseflow                    |

How the pathways connect: Evaporation and transpiration transfer water into the atmosphere. Condensation contributes to cloud formation, and precipitation returns water to Earth's surface. Some precipitation becomes surface runoff, while some infiltrates the soil. Infiltrated water may be taken up by plants or percolate deeper to recharge groundwater, which can eventually discharge into rivers, lakes or oceans.

These pathways operate simultaneously; water does not have to pass through every process in a fixed order.

---

## **7.4 UPSC Prelims: Key Distinctions**

- Solar energy is the principal driver of evaporation, while gravity drives precipitation, surface runoff and much of the downward movement of water.
- Evapotranspiration combines evaporation and transpiration.
- Stomata are the principal openings through which plants release water vapour during transpiration.
- Condensation converts water vapour into liquid droplets; clouds may also contain ice crystals.
- Infiltration is the entry of water into soil, whereas percolation is its movement through soil and permeable materials.
- Groundwater recharge occurs when water reaches and replenishes the groundwater system.
- Baseflow is the contribution of groundwater to river or stream flow.
- Oceans are Earth's largest water reservoir; glaciers and ice caps contain most of Earth's freshwater.
- Vegetation influences the water cycle through transpiration, interception of precipitation and effects on infiltration and runoff.
- Urbanization generally increases surface runoff and reduces infiltration where natural surfaces are replaced by impermeable surfaces.
- The hydrological cycle is predominantly a physical cycle, involving water movement and changes of state.

## Water Cycle


**Q25. Which one of the following best distinguishes infiltration from percolation?**

**A. Infiltration is groundwater discharge; percolation is precipitation.**  
**B. Infiltration is cloud formation; percolation is surface runoff.**  
**C. Infiltration is evaporation from soil; percolation is transpiration.**  
**D. Infiltration is entry of water into soil; percolation is movement through soil and permeable materials.**  

**Answer: D**

> **Explanation:** Infiltration describes entry across the ground surface, while percolation describes movement within soil and permeable layers.


**Q26. Consider the following statements about the hydrological cycle:**


1. Evapotranspiration includes evaporation and plant transpiration.
2. Transpiration commonly releases water vapour through leaf stomata.
3. Condensation is the conversion of liquid water into water vapour.

Which of the statements given above are correct?

**A. 1 and 2 only**  
**B. 1 and 3 only**  
**C. 2 and 3 only**  
**D. 1, 2 and 3**  

**Answer: A**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Condensation is vapour to liquid; evaporation is liquid to vapour.


**Q27. Which of the following statements about groundwater is correct?**

**A. Groundwater can move only vertically downward.**  
**B. Groundwater discharge can sustain river baseflow during dry periods.**  
**C. All infiltrated rainfall necessarily recharges groundwater.**  
**D. Groundwater is confined exclusively to underground rivers.**  

**Answer: B**

> **Explanation:** Groundwater may discharge into rivers and sustain baseflow. Not all infiltration becomes recharge, and groundwater moves through saturated pores and fractures.


**Q28. Consider the following statements:**


1. Oceans constitute Earth’s largest reservoir of water.
2. Glaciers and ice caps contain most of Earth’s freshwater.
3. Replacing permeable land with impervious surfaces generally increases infiltration.

**A. 1 and 3 only**  
**B. 2 and 3 only**  
**C. 1 and 2 only**  
**D. 1, 2 and 3**  

**Answer: C**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 2 are correct. Impervious surfaces generally reduce infiltration and increase surface runoff.
