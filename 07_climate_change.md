# Chapter 7: Climate Change and Global Warming
## UPSC Civil Services Preliminary Examination – Study Notes

## 7.1 Climate Change: Basic Concepts

### Important Definitions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Weather | Atmospheric conditions at a particular place and time |
| Climate | Long-term statistical characteristics of weather, commonly assessed over 30-year periods |
| Global warming | Long-term increase in Earth's average surface temperature |
| Climate change | Long-term changes in temperature, precipitation and other characteristics of the climate system |
| Greenhouse effect | Warming caused by greenhouse gases absorbing and emitting infrared radiation |
| Global warming potential (GWP) | Measure of a greenhouse gas's heat-trapping effect relative to CO₂ over a specified period |
| Radiative forcing | Change in Earth's energy balance caused by a forcing agent |
| Carbon footprint | Greenhouse gas emissions attributable to an activity, product, individual or organization, generally expressed in CO₂-equivalent |

### Global Warming vs Climate Change

- **Global warming:** Refers specifically to the increase in global average surface temperature.
- **Climate change:** Includes warming and associated changes in rainfall, ocean conditions, glaciers, sea level and extreme weather.

**Important:** Global warming is one component of climate change.

---

## 7.2 Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is a natural process that maintains Earth's surface at a temperature suitable for life.

### Mechanism

1. Incoming shortwave solar radiation reaches Earth.
2. Earth's surface absorbs energy and warms.
3. The surface emits longwave infrared radiation.
4. Greenhouse gases absorb and emit part of this infrared radiation.
5. This reduces the rate at which energy escapes to space and warms the surface and lower atmosphere.

### Natural vs Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

| Natural Greenhouse Effect | Enhanced Greenhouse Effect |
|---|---|
| Occurs naturally | Results from increased greenhouse gas concentrations |
| Maintains Earth's habitable temperature | Causes additional warming |
| Essential for life | Primarily driven by human activities in recent decades |

### Important Fact

Without the natural greenhouse effect, Earth's average surface temperature would be approximately **−18°C**, compared with about **+15°C** under present-day conditions.

## 7.3 Important Greenhouse Gases

| Gas | Chemical Formula | Important Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | Fossil fuel combustion, deforestation, cement production |
| Methane | CH₄ | Livestock, rice cultivation, landfills, fossil fuel extraction |
| Nitrous oxide | N₂O | Fertilizers, agricultural soils, industrial activities |
| Water vapour | H₂O | Evaporation and transpiration |
| Ozone | O₃ | Formed through atmospheric chemical reactions |
| Hydrofluorocarbons | HFCs | Refrigeration, air conditioning and industrial applications |
| Perfluorocarbons | PFCs | Industrial processes |
| Sulphur hexafluoride | SF₆ | Electrical equipment and industrial applications |
| Nitrogen trifluoride | NF₃ | Electronics and semiconductor manufacturing |

### Important Distinctions

- **Water vapour:** The most abundant greenhouse gas, primarily acting as a climate feedback rather than the principal direct driver of current human-caused warming.
- **CO₂:** The largest contributor to long-term anthropogenic warming.
- **Methane:** A powerful greenhouse gas with a shorter atmospheric lifetime than CO₂.
- **Nitrous oxide:** A long-lived greenhouse gas associated particularly with agricultural activities.
- **Ozone:** A greenhouse gas whose effects depend on its atmospheric location.

### Greenhouse Gases vs Non-Greenhouse Gases

| Greenhouse Gases | Not Significant Greenhouse Gases |
|---|---|
| CO₂ | N₂ |
| CH₄ | O₂ |
| N₂O | Ar |
| H₂O | — |
| O₃ | — |

**UPSC Trap:** Nitrogen and oxygen are major constituents of the atmosphere but are not significant greenhouse gases.

## 7.4 Global Warming Potential (GWP)

GWP compares the integrated warming effect of a greenhouse gas with that of CO₂ over a specified time horizon.

### Important Facts

- CO₂ is assigned a GWP of **1**.
- GWP is commonly calculated over **20 years** or **100 years**.
- A gas's GWP depends on its radiative efficiency and atmospheric lifetime.

### Approximate 100-Year GWP Values

Using IPCC AR6 values:

| Greenhouse Gas | GWP₁₀₀ |
|---|---:|
| Carbon dioxide | 1 |
| Methane (non-fossil origin) | 27.0 |
| Methane (fossil origin) | 29.8 |
| Nitrous oxide | 273 |
| Sulphur hexafluoride | 25,200 |

**Important:** GWP values vary between IPCC assessment reports and according to the calculation convention.

### UPSC Prelims Distinctions

- **GWP** measures relative warming impact.
- **Atmospheric lifetime** measures how long a gas persists or is removed through atmospheric processes.
- A gas with a higher GWP does not necessarily have a higher atmospheric concentration.

## 7.5 Sources of Greenhouse Gas Emissions

### Major Anthropogenic Sources

| Sector | Important Greenhouse Gases |
|---|---|
| Electricity generation | CO₂ |
| Transport | CO₂ |
| Industry | CO₂, N₂O, fluorinated gases |
| Agriculture | CH₄, N₂O |
| Livestock | CH₄ |
| Rice cultivation | CH₄ |
| Landfills | CH₄ |
| Deforestation | CO₂ |
| Refrigeration | HFCs and other refrigerants |
| Fertilizer use | N₂O |

### Important Facts

**Methane**
- Produced by anaerobic decomposition.
- Important sources include wetlands, rice paddies, ruminants and landfills.
- Natural wetlands are a major natural methane source.

**Nitrous Oxide**
- Released through microbial processes in soils.
- Agricultural nitrogen inputs are an important anthropogenic source.

**Carbon Dioxide**
- Fossil fuel combustion is a major anthropogenic source.
- Deforestation contributes through carbon release and reduction of carbon storage.

## 7.6 Carbon Cycle and Climate Change

Carbon moves among the atmosphere, oceans, vegetation, soils and geological reservoirs.

### Major Carbon Reservoirs

1. Atmosphere.
2. Oceans.
3. Terrestrial vegetation.
4. Soils.
5. Fossil fuels and sedimentary rocks.

### Carbon Sinks

A carbon sink absorbs more carbon than it releases over a specified period.

**Examples:**
- Growing forests.
- Oceans.
- Certain soils and wetlands.

### Carbon Sources

A carbon source releases more carbon than it absorbs.

**Examples:**
- Fossil fuel combustion.
- Deforestation.
- Decomposition and respiration, when considered as individual processes.

### Important Distinction

**Carbon stock** is the quantity stored, whereas a **carbon sink** describes net uptake over time.

## 7.7 Climate Feedback Mechanisms

A climate feedback amplifies or reduces an initial climatic change.

| Feedback | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Water vapour feedback | Positive | Warming increases atmospheric water vapour, enhancing warming |
| Ice–albedo feedback | Positive | Ice loss exposes darker surfaces, increasing solar absorption |
| Permafrost carbon feedback | Positive | Thawing releases CO₂ and methane |
| Planck response | Negative | A warmer Earth emits more infrared radiation to space |
| Cloud feedback | Variable | Depends on cloud type, altitude and other characteristics |

### Positive vs Negative Feedback

- **Positive feedback:** Amplifies the initial change.
- **Negative feedback:** Opposes the initial change.

**Prelims Trap:** Positive feedback does not mean environmentally beneficial feedback.

## 7.8 Albedo

**Albedo** is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface.

### Important Facts

| Surface | General Albedo Characteristic |
|---|---|
| Fresh snow | High |
| Sea ice | Relatively high |
| Desert sand | Often relatively high |
| Forest | Generally lower |
| Open ocean | Generally low, varying with solar angle |

### Ice–Albedo Feedback

1. Rising temperature melts snow and ice.
2. Darker land or ocean surfaces become exposed.
3. More solar radiation is absorbed.
4. Additional warming occurs.

**Important:** This is a positive climate feedback.

---

## 7.9 Aerosols and Climate

Aerosols are small solid or liquid particles suspended in the atmosphere.

### Important Aerosols

| Aerosol | Important Climate Effect |
|---|---|
| Sulphate aerosols | Generally scatter sunlight and produce cooling |
| Black carbon | Absorbs sunlight and contributes to warming |
| Organic aerosols | Effects depend on composition |
| Mineral dust | Can absorb and scatter radiation |
| Sea salt | Generally scatters sunlight and influences clouds |

### Black Carbon

- Produced by incomplete combustion.
- Important sources include diesel engines, biomass burning and solid-fuel combustion.
- Absorbs solar radiation.
- Deposition on snow and ice reduces surface albedo and can accelerate melting.

### Black Carbon vs Carbon Dioxide

| Black Carbon | Carbon Dioxide |
|---|---|
| Particulate aerosol | Gas |
| Short atmospheric residence time | Long-lasting climate influence |
| Absorbs sunlight | Absorbs and emits infrared radiation |
| Also affects air quality | Principal long-lived anthropogenic greenhouse gas |

**UPSC Trap:** Black carbon is not a greenhouse gas in the conventional sense; it is a climate-warming aerosol.

## 7.10 Ozone and Climate Change

Ozone occurs primarily in two atmospheric regions.

| Stratospheric Ozone | Tropospheric Ozone |
|---|---|
| Protects against harmful ultraviolet radiation | Harmful air pollutant |
| Forms the ozone layer | Formed through photochemical reactions |
| Important for life on Earth | Damages vegetation and human health |
| Also influences atmospheric radiation | Acts as a greenhouse gas |

### Important Distinctions

- Ozone depletion and global warming are different environmental problems.
- Some ozone-depleting substances are also powerful greenhouse gases.
- The Montreal Protocol primarily addresses ozone-depleting substances.
- The Kigali Amendment addresses HFCs, which are greenhouse gases but generally do not deplete stratospheric ozone.

## 7.11 Global Warming and Oceans

Oceans absorb much of the excess heat associated with global warming.

### Important Consequences

1. Ocean warming.
2. Thermal expansion of seawater.
3. Sea-level rise.
4. Marine heatwaves.
5. Coral bleaching.
6. Changes in marine ecosystems.
7. Ocean deoxygenation.

### Causes of Sea-Level Rise

| Cause | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Thermal expansion | Warming causes seawater to expand |
| Melting mountain glaciers | Adds water to oceans |
| Melting Greenland ice sheet | Adds water to oceans |
| Melting Antarctic land ice | Adds water to oceans |

### Important Distinction

**Melting floating sea ice** does not directly produce substantial sea-level rise because it already displaces seawater.

**Melting land-based ice** contributes directly to sea-level rise.

## 7.12 Ocean Acidification

Ocean acidification refers to the long-term decline in average ocean pH caused mainly by the absorption of atmospheric CO₂.

### Chemical Process

\[
CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3
\]

\[
H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-
\]

The increase in hydrogen-ion concentration reduces seawater pH.

### Important Effects

- Reduced carbonate-ion availability.
- Difficulties for some calcifying organisms.
- Potential impacts on corals, shellfish and plankton.
- Changes in marine ecosystems.

### Important Facts

- Ocean acidification does not mean that seawater has necessarily become acidic (pH below 7).
- Average seawater remains alkaline but is becoming less alkaline.
- Ocean acidification and ocean warming are distinct processes.

---

## 7.13 Coral Bleaching and Climate Change

Coral bleaching occurs when corals lose their symbiotic algae or associated pigments.

### Major Causes

- Elevated sea surface temperature.
- Marine heatwaves.
- Other environmental stressors.

### Important Facts

- Bleached corals are not necessarily dead.
- Prolonged bleaching can cause coral mortality.
- Climate change increases the risk of mass bleaching events.
- Ocean acidification can separately impair coral calcification.

---

## 7.14 Climate Change and the Cryosphere

The **cryosphere** includes Earth's frozen-water components.

### Components

- Glaciers.
- Ice sheets.
- Sea ice.
- Snow cover.
- Permafrost.
- Frozen lakes and rivers.

### Important Distinctions

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Glacier | Persistent mass of land ice formed from accumulated snow |
| Ice sheet | Large land-based ice mass covering extensive areas |
| Sea ice | Frozen seawater floating on the ocean |
| Permafrost | Ground remaining at or below 0°C for at least two consecutive years |
| Glacial lake | Lake formed in association with glacial processes |
| GLOF | Glacial Lake Outburst Flood |

### Climate Change Impacts

- Glacier retreat.
- Permafrost thaw.
- Reduced snow cover in many regions.
- Changes in seasonal river flow.
- Increased risk from some glacial lakes.

---

## 7.15 Climate Change and Extreme Weather

Climate change influences the frequency, intensity, duration or geographical distribution of certain extreme weather events.

### Important Events

| Event | Climate Change Relationship |
|---|---|
| Heatwaves | Increasing frequency and intensity in many regions |
| Heavy rainfall | Warmer air can hold more moisture, increasing potential rainfall intensity |
| Drought | Changes in precipitation and evaporation affect drought risk |
| Tropical cyclones | Warmer oceans can increase potential intensity and rainfall; regional responses vary |
| Wildfires | Heat and drought can increase fire-weather risk |
| Marine heatwaves | Increasing occurrence and intensity |

### Important Facts

- Individual weather events cannot automatically be attributed entirely to climate change.
- Event-attribution studies assess how climate change alters the probability or intensity of particular events.
- Climate change does not imply that every location becomes uniformly warmer or wetter at all times.

## 7.16 Climate Change and Biodiversity

### Important Impacts

- Shifts in species distribution towards higher latitudes or elevations.
- Changes in flowering, breeding and migration periods.
- Coral bleaching and reef degradation.
- Increased extinction risks for climate-sensitive species.
- Changes in forest composition.
- Habitat loss in polar and alpine ecosystems.

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Phenology | Timing of recurring biological events, such as flowering and migration |
| Range shift | Change in geographical distribution of a species |
| Climate refuge | Area providing relatively favourable conditions during climatic change |
| Ecosystem resilience | Capacity of an ecosystem to withstand or recover from disturbances |

**Important:** Climate change can alter interactions between species, including predator–prey and plant–pollinator relationships.

# 7.17 Climate Change Mitigation and Adaptation

## A. Mitigation

**Mitigation** refers to actions that reduce greenhouse gas emissions or enhance greenhouse gas removals.

### Examples

- Renewable energy.
- Energy efficiency.
- Electrification of transport.
- Methane emission reduction.
- Forest conservation and restoration.
- Carbon capture and storage.

## B. Adaptation

**Adaptation** refers to adjustments in human or natural systems in response to actual or expected climate conditions.

### Examples

- Drought-resistant crops.
- Flood protection infrastructure.
- Early warning systems.
- Climate-resilient agriculture.
- Coastal protection.
- Improved water management.

### Important Comparison

| Mitigation | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| Addresses causes of climate change | Addresses impacts and risks |
| Reduces emissions or increases removals | Reduces vulnerability or exposure |
| Example: Solar power | Example: Flood-resistant infrastructure |

### Maladaptation

Adaptation measures that unintentionally increase vulnerability, emissions or climate risks.

**Example:** Coastal infrastructure that protects one location but increases erosion or flood risk elsewhere.

# 7.18 Net Zero, Carbon Neutrality and Carbon Negative

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Net-zero CO₂ emissions | Anthropogenic CO₂ emissions balanced by anthropogenic CO₂ removals |
| Net-zero GHG emissions | Greenhouse gas emissions balanced by removals, using an appropriate common metric |
| Carbon neutrality | Balance between CO₂ emissions and removals or qualifying offsets within a defined accounting boundary |
| Carbon negative | Net removal of more CO₂ than is emitted within the defined boundary |
| Carbon budget | Cumulative CO₂ emissions consistent with limiting warming to a specified level |

### Important Facts

- **Net zero does not necessarily mean zero gross emissions.**
- Emission reductions remain essential even when carbon removals are used.
- Carbon neutrality and net-zero GHG emissions are not always identical accounting concepts.
- Cumulative CO₂ emissions are closely related to long-term global warming.

---

# 7.19 Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage

### Important Technologies

| Technology | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) | Capturing CO₂ and storing it in suitable geological formations |
| Carbon Capture, Utilization and Storage (CCUS) | Capturing CO₂ for use or storage |
| Direct Air Capture (DAC) | Removing CO₂ directly from ambient air |
| Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS) | Combining bioenergy production with CO₂ capture and storage |
| Enhanced Weathering | Accelerating mineral reactions that remove atmospheric CO₂ |

### Important Distinctions

- Capturing CO₂ from a fossil-fuel power plant prevents some emissions but does not automatically constitute carbon dioxide removal.
- DAC combined with durable geological storage can constitute carbon dioxide removal.
- CO₂ utilization does not necessarily provide permanent carbon storage.

# 7.20 Nature-Based Solutions

Nature-based solutions use the protection, restoration or sustainable management of ecosystems to address societal challenges.

### Important Examples

| Ecosystem | Climate Benefit |
|---|---|
| Forests | Carbon storage and sequestration |
| Mangroves | Carbon storage and coastal protection |
| Peatlands | Long-term carbon storage |
| Seagrass meadows | Blue carbon storage |
| Urban green spaces | Local cooling and stormwater management |

**Important:** Nature-based solutions can support both mitigation and adaptation.

# 7.21 Climate-Smart Agriculture

Climate-smart agriculture aims to:

1. Increase agricultural productivity and incomes sustainably.
2. Strengthen adaptation and resilience.
3. Reduce or remove greenhouse gas emissions where possible.

### Important Practices

- Improved water management.
- Agroforestry.
- Soil conservation.
- Climate-resilient crop varieties.
- Improved fertilizer management.
- Efficient irrigation.

**Important:** Climate-smart agriculture is an approach, not a single agricultural technology.

# 7.25 Paris Agreement

### Important Facts

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Adopted | **2015** |
| Conference | COP21 |
| Location | Paris, France |
| Entered into force | **2016** |
| Legal framework | Under UNFCCC |

### Temperature Goal

Hold the increase in global average temperature to **well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels** and pursue efforts to limit it to **1.5°C**.

### Important Features

1. Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs).
2. Global Stocktake.
3. Enhanced Transparency Framework.
4. Climate finance.
5. Adaptation.
6. International cooperation under Article 6.

### Nationally Determined Contributions

- Countries prepare and communicate their own climate commitments.
- Successive NDCs are communicated on a five-year cycle.
- Each successive NDC is expected to represent progression.
- The obligation to prepare, communicate and maintain NDCs is legally binding; achievement of each NDC's emission target is not itself an internationally enforceable obligation of the same kind.

### Global Stocktake

- Assesses collective progress towards the Paris Agreement's goals.
- Conducted every five years.
- First concluded at **COP28 in 2023**.

### Article 6

| Provision | Main Subject |
|---|---|
| Article 6.2 | Cooperative approaches involving internationally transferred mitigation outcomes |
| Article 6.4 | UN-supervised carbon-crediting mechanism |
| Article 6.8 | Non-market approaches |

**Important:** Article 6.8 concerns non-market approaches, not international carbon-credit trading.

# 7.27 India's Climate Commitments

## A. Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)

India's NDCs contain targets concerning emissions intensity, non-fossil electricity capacity and sustainable lifestyles.

### Important Targets

| Target Year | Commitment |
|---|---|
| **2030** | Reduce emissions intensity of GDP by **45%** from the 2005 level |
| **2030** | Achieve about **50%** of cumulative installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources |
| **2035** | Reduce emissions intensity of GDP by **47%** from the 2005 level |
| **2035** | Achieve **60%** cumulative installed electric power capacity from non-fossil sources |
| **2070** | Achieve net-zero emissions |

The 2030 targets were included in India's **2022 NDC update**. The 2035 targets were included in its **2026 NDC update**. [Press Information Bureau](https://www.pib.gov.in/Pressreleaseshare.aspx?PRID=1885731\&lang=2\&reg=48\&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Important Prelims Distinctions

**1. Emissions intensity vs absolute emissions**

Emissions intensity of GDP means greenhouse gas emissions per unit of GDP.

A reduction in emissions intensity does not necessarily mean a reduction in total emissions.

**2. Installed capacity vs electricity generation**

India's non-fossil electricity target refers to the proportion of **installed power capacity**, not the proportion of electricity actually generated.

**3. Non-fossil vs renewable energy**

Non-fossil electricity includes nuclear power as well as renewable sources.

**4. Net-zero target**

India's long-term net-zero target is **2070**, not 2050.

# 7.30 Carbon Markets and Carbon Credits

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Carbon credit | Tradable unit generally representing one tonne of CO₂-equivalent emission reduction or removal |
| Carbon market | System for trading eligible carbon units or allowances |
| Emissions trading system (ETS) | System in which regulated entities trade emission allowances |
| Carbon offset | Emission reduction or removal used to compensate for emissions elsewhere |
| Carbon tax | Tax imposed on greenhouse gas emissions or their carbon content |

### Compliance vs Voluntary Carbon Markets

| Compliance Market | Voluntary Carbon Market |
|---|---|
| Operates under regulatory requirements | Participation generally voluntary |
| May involve mandatory emission limits | Often used for voluntary climate commitments |
| Example: Regulated emissions trading system | Example: Voluntary carbon-credit purchases |

### Important Facts

- Carbon credits are not the same as renewable energy certificates.
- Carbon markets may cover emission reductions, removals or emission allowances, depending on the system.
- Credible carbon credits require safeguards against double counting.
- Additionality means that the credited reduction or removal would not have occurred without the incentive provided by the crediting mechanism.

| Carbon stock | Carbon sink | Stored carbon vs net carbon uptake |
