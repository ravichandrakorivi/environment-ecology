# Chapter 8: Environmental Pollution and Waste Management
## UPSC Civil Services Preliminary Examination – Study Notes

## 8.1 Environmental Pollution: Basic Concepts

**Environmental pollution** refers to the introduction of substances or energy into the environment at levels that adversely affect organisms, ecosystems or human health.

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Pollutant | Substance or form of energy causing pollution |
| Contaminant | Substance present where it is not normally expected or at an undesirable concentration |
| Primary pollutant | Pollutant emitted directly from a source |
| Secondary pollutant | Pollutant formed through chemical reactions in the environment |
| Point-source pollution | Pollution originating from an identifiable source |
| Non-point-source pollution | Pollution originating from diffuse sources |
| Persistent pollutant | Pollutant that resists environmental degradation |
| Bioaccumulation | Accumulation of a substance within an organism over time |
| Biomagnification | Increase in concentration of a substance at successive trophic levels |
| Biodegradable waste | Waste that microorganisms can decompose |
| Non-biodegradable waste | Waste that does not readily undergo biological decomposition |

### Major Types of Pollution

1. Air pollution.
2. Water pollution.
3. Soil pollution.
4. Noise pollution.
5. Thermal pollution.
6. Radioactive pollution.
7. Light pollution.
8. Plastic and marine pollution.

---

# 8.2 Air Pollution

Air pollution occurs when harmful gases, particles or biological materials are present in the atmosphere at concentrations that adversely affect health or the environment.

## Primary and Secondary Air Pollutants

| Primary Pollutants | Secondary Pollutants |
|---|---|
| Carbon monoxide (CO) | Tropospheric ozone (O₃) |
| Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) | Peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN) |
| Nitrogen monoxide (NO) | Secondary sulphate aerosols |
| Ammonia (NH₃) | Secondary nitrate aerosols |
| Particulate matter emitted directly | Secondary organic aerosols |
| Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) emitted directly | Photochemical oxidants |

**Important:** Some pollutants, particularly particulate matter, have both primary and secondary sources.

### Important Sources

| Pollutant | Major Sources |
|---|---|
| SO₂ | Coal combustion, industrial processes |
| NOₓ | Vehicles, power plants, high-temperature combustion |
| CO | Incomplete combustion |
| PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ | Combustion, road dust, industries, construction |
| NH₃ | Agriculture, livestock, fertilizers |
| VOCs | Fuels, solvents, industries, vegetation |
| Ground-level O₃ | Secondary formation involving NOₓ, VOCs and sunlight |

# 8.3 Particulate Matter: PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀

**Particulate matter (PM)** consists of small solid particles and liquid droplets suspended in air.

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| PM₁₀ | Particles with aerodynamic diameter generally ≤10 micrometres |
| PM₂.₅ | Particles with aerodynamic diameter generally ≤2.5 micrometres |

### Important Facts

- PM₂.₅ can penetrate deeply into the respiratory system.
- Some fine particles or their constituents may enter the bloodstream.
- PM₁₀ includes PM₂.₅.
- Fine particles can travel long distances through the atmosphere.
- Particulate matter may contain black carbon, sulphates, nitrates, metals and organic compounds.

### PM₂.₅ vs PM₁₀

| PM₂.₅ | PM₁₀ |
|---|---|
| Fine particulate matter | Includes fine and coarse particulate matter |
| ≤2.5 µm | ≤10 µm |
| Strongly associated with combustion and secondary aerosol formation | Also includes substantial contributions from dust and mechanical processes |

**UPSC Trap:** PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ are size-based categories, not specific chemical substances.

# 8.4 National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS)

India's **National Ambient Air Quality Standards, 2009**, specify permissible concentrations of selected air pollutants.

### Important Standards

| Pollutant | Annual Standard | Short-Term Standard |
|---|---:|---:|
| PM₁₀ | 60 µg/m³ | 100 µg/m³ (24 hours) |
| PM₂.₅ | 40 µg/m³ | 60 µg/m³ (24 hours) |
| SO₂ | 50 µg/m³ | 80 µg/m³ (24 hours) |
| NO₂ | 40 µg/m³ | 80 µg/m³ (24 hours) |
| O₃ | — | 100 µg/m³ (8 hours) |
| CO | — | 2 mg/m³ (8 hours) |

**Note:** SO₂ and NO₂ annual standards shown are for industrial, residential, rural and other areas. Certain ecologically sensitive areas have different annual standards.

### Important Facts

- NAAQS are notified by the **Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)** under the relevant statutory framework.
- Standards differ according to pollutant and averaging period.
- Carbon monoxide standards are expressed in **mg/m³**, unlike several other pollutants expressed in µg/m³.

# 8.5 Air Quality Index (AQI)

India's National Air Quality Index was launched in **2014**.

### AQI Categories

| AQI Value | Category |
|---|---|
| 0–50 | Good |
| 51–100 | Satisfactory |
| 101–200 | Moderately Polluted |
| 201–300 | Poor |
| 301–400 | Very Poor |
| 401–500 | Severe |

### Eight AQI Pollutants

1. PM₁₀.
2. PM₂.₅.
3. NO₂.
4. SO₂.
5. CO.
6. O₃.
7. NH₃.
8. Pb.

### Important Facts

- AQI communicates overall air-quality conditions.
- The overall AQI is generally determined by the highest pollutant sub-index among pollutants considered.
- AQI is not the arithmetic average of pollutant concentrations.
- AQI values are dimensionless.

**Prelims Trap:** Carbon dioxide is not one of the eight pollutants used in India's National AQI.

# 8.6 Photochemical Smog

Photochemical smog forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in the presence of sunlight.

### Important Components

- Tropospheric ozone.
- Peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN).
- Aldehydes.
- Other photochemical oxidants.

### Formation

\[
NO_2 + h\nu \rightarrow NO + O
\]

\[
O + O_2 \rightarrow O_3
\]

The accumulation of ozone depends on further reactions involving VOCs, NOₓ and atmospheric conditions.

### Important Facts

- Photochemical smog is associated with sunlight and atmospheric chemical reactions.
- It is commonly called **Los Angeles-type smog**.
- Ground-level ozone is a secondary pollutant.
- PAN is an important photochemical oxidant.

### Classical Smog vs Photochemical Smog

| Classical Smog | Photochemical Smog |
|---|---|
| Associated with coal smoke and SO₂ | Associated with NOₓ, VOCs and sunlight |
| Favoured by cool, humid conditions | Favoured by sunny conditions |
| Historically associated with London | Historically associated with Los Angeles |
| Often called sulphurous smog | Often called oxidizing smog |

# 8.7 Acid Rain

Acid rain is precipitation with unusually low pH due mainly to atmospheric acids formed from sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.

### Principal Acids

- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).
- Nitric acid (HNO₃).

### Important Facts

- Normal rainwater is naturally slightly acidic because dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid.
- Acid rain is commonly defined as precipitation with **pH below approximately 5.6**.
- Acid deposition may be wet or dry.

### Effects

1. Acidification of lakes and streams.
2. Soil nutrient depletion.
3. Mobilization of toxic aluminium.
4. Damage to vegetation.
5. Corrosion of buildings and monuments.

### Important Distinction

| Wet Deposition | Dry Deposition |
|---|---|
| Acidic substances deposited through rain, snow or fog | Acidic gases and particles deposited without precipitation |

**UPSC Trap:** Carbon dioxide is responsible for the natural mild acidity of rainwater, but SO₂ and NOₓ are the principal precursors of anthropogenic acid rain.

# 8.8 Ozone Depletion

The ozone layer is concentrated in the **stratosphere** and absorbs much of the Sun's harmful ultraviolet radiation.

### Important Ozone-Depleting Substances

| Substance | Important Use/Source |
|---|---|
| Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) | Historical refrigeration, aerosols and foam production |
| Halons | Fire suppression |
| Carbon tetrachloride | Industrial uses |
| Methyl chloroform | Industrial solvent |
| Methyl bromide | Fumigation |
| Hydrochlorofluorocarbons (HCFCs) | Transitional refrigerants |

### Mechanism

- Certain ozone-depleting substances reach the stratosphere.
- Ultraviolet radiation releases reactive chlorine or bromine.
- These radicals catalytically destroy ozone molecules.

### Important Facts

- The Antarctic ozone hole is a region of severe seasonal stratospheric ozone depletion.
- It is not a literal hole containing no ozone.
- Polar stratospheric clouds facilitate reactions important for Antarctic ozone depletion.

### Montreal Protocol

| Feature | Fact |
|---|---|
| Vienna Convention | 1985 |
| Montreal Protocol | 1987 |
| Montreal Protocol entered into force | 1989 |
| Kigali Amendment | 2016 |
| Kigali Amendment entered into force | 2019 |

### Kigali Amendment

- Addresses the phasedown of **hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs)**.
- HFCs are generally not ozone-depleting substances.
- Many HFCs are powerful greenhouse gases.

**Important:** Ozone depletion and climate change are distinct but interconnected environmental issues.

# 8.10 Important Water-Quality Parameters

| Parameter | Meaning | Important Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Dissolved Oxygen (DO) | Oxygen dissolved in water | Low DO may indicate ecological stress |
| Biological Oxygen Demand (BOD) | Oxygen consumed during microbial decomposition of biodegradable matter | High BOD often indicates organic pollution |
| Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD) | Oxygen equivalent required to chemically oxidize substances | Measures chemically oxidizable pollution load |
| pH | Acidity or alkalinity | Indicates hydrogen-ion activity |
| Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) | Dissolved inorganic and organic substances | Indicates dissolved material concentration |
| Turbidity | Cloudiness caused by suspended particles | High turbidity reduces light penetration |
| Coliform bacteria | Indicator organisms | Used to assess possible faecal contamination |

### BOD vs COD

| BOD | COD |
|---|---|
| Measures oxygen demand associated with biological decomposition | Measures oxygen equivalent for chemical oxidation |
| Commonly measured over five days | Usually measured over a shorter period |
| Primarily reflects biodegradable organic matter | Includes a broader range of chemically oxidizable substances |

**Important:** COD is generally higher than or equal to BOD for the same sample under commonly used testing methods, but the measurements are not interchangeable.

# 8.11 Eutrophication

**Eutrophication** is nutrient enrichment of a water body, particularly with nitrogen and phosphorus.

### Process

1. Excess nutrients enter a water body.
2. Algal and cyanobacterial growth increases.
3. Excess biomass dies and decomposes.
4. Decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen.
5. Hypoxia or anoxia may develop.
6. Aquatic organisms may die or migrate.

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Algal bloom | Rapid increase in algal abundance |
| Harmful algal bloom | Bloom causing ecological or health-related harm |
| Hypoxia | Low dissolved oxygen |
| Anoxia | Absence of dissolved oxygen |
| Dead zone | Area with severe oxygen depletion |

### Important Facts

- Phosphorus is often a limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems.
- Nitrogen frequently limits productivity in marine ecosystems.
- Nutrient limitation varies with local conditions.
- Not all algal blooms are toxic.

# 8.12 Biomagnification and Bioaccumulation

## Bioaccumulation

Increase in the concentration of a substance within an organism over time.

## Biomagnification

Increase in pollutant concentration at successive trophic levels of a food chain.

### Example

Water → Plankton → Small fish → Large fish → Fish-eating birds.

Persistent pollutants such as methylmercury may become more concentrated at higher trophic levels.

### Important Distinction

| Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification |
|---|---|
| Occurs within an organism over time | Occurs across successive trophic levels |
| Depends on uptake and elimination | Depends on food-web transfer |
| Example: Mercury accumulating in a fish | Higher mercury concentration in predatory fish |

### Pollutants Associated with Biomagnification

- Methylmercury.
- DDT.
- Certain PCBs.
- Some other persistent, bioaccumulative pollutants.

**UPSC Trap:** Not every pollutant undergoes biomagnification.

# 8.13 Heavy Metal Pollution

| Metal/Element | Important Sources | Major Health or Environmental Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Mercury (Hg) | Mining, coal combustion, industrial processes | Neurotoxicity |
| Lead (Pb) | Batteries, industrial emissions, contaminated materials | Neurological damage |
| Cadmium (Cd) | Batteries, industrial activities | Kidney and bone damage |
| Arsenic (As) | Geogenic groundwater contamination, industrial sources | Chronic toxicity |
| Chromium (Cr) | Tanneries, electroplating | Toxicity varies with oxidation state |
| Nickel (Ni) | Industrial processes | Toxicity and allergic effects |

### Important Facts

**Mercury**
- Methylmercury can biomagnify in aquatic food chains.
- Minamata disease is associated with methylmercury poisoning.

**Cadmium**
- Itai-itai disease is historically associated with cadmium pollution in Japan.

**Arsenic**
- Groundwater contamination is a major concern in parts of the Ganga–Brahmaputra plains.

**Chromium**
- Hexavalent chromium, Cr(VI), is generally more toxic than trivalent chromium, Cr(III).

### Important Distinction

Arsenic is chemically a **metalloid**, although it is frequently grouped with heavy metals in environmental pollution discussions.

# 8.15 Thermal Pollution

Thermal pollution refers to undesirable changes in water temperature caused by human activities.

### Major Sources

- Thermal power plants.
- Industrial cooling-water discharge.
- Other heated effluents.

### Important Effects

- Reduced dissolved oxygen solubility as water temperature rises.
- Changes in aquatic metabolism.
- Thermal stress to aquatic organisms.
- Changes in species composition.

**Important:** Thermal pollution does not necessarily involve the addition of a toxic chemical.

# 8.16 Marine Pollution

### Major Sources

- Plastic waste.
- Untreated sewage.
- Oil spills.
- Agricultural runoff.
- Industrial effluents.
- Shipping activities.

## Oil Spills

### Important Effects

- Oil coating bird feathers reduces insulation and waterproofing.
- Marine mammals may experience adverse effects.
- Toxic hydrocarbons can affect aquatic organisms.
- Coastal habitats may suffer long-term damage.

### Oil Spill Response Methods

| Method | Principle |
|---|---|
| Booms | Contain floating oil |
| Skimmers | Remove oil from water surfaces |
| Sorbents | Absorb or adsorb oil |
| Dispersants | Break oil into smaller droplets |
| Bioremediation | Uses microorganisms to degrade hydrocarbons |

**Important:** Dispersants do not eliminate oil; they redistribute it into smaller droplets and may introduce additional ecological trade-offs.

# 8.17 Plastic Pollution

Plastics are synthetic or semi-synthetic polymeric materials used in numerous applications.

### Important Categories

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Macroplastics | Relatively large plastic items |
| Microplastics | Plastic particles generally smaller than 5 mm |
| Nanoplastics | Extremely small plastic particles; definitions vary |
| Primary microplastics | Manufactured at microscopic sizes |
| Secondary microplastics | Formed by fragmentation of larger plastics |

### Major Sources of Microplastics

- Breakdown of plastic litter.
- Synthetic textile fibres.
- Tyre wear.
- Industrial plastic pellets.
- Certain personal-care products.

### Important Facts

- Microplastics occur in marine, freshwater, terrestrial and atmospheric environments.
- They may be ingested by organisms.
- Plastic additives and associated chemicals can create additional environmental concerns.
- Biodegradable plastics do not necessarily degrade rapidly under natural environmental conditions.

# 8.18 Single-Use Plastics in India

### Important Legal Framework

**Plastic Waste Management Rules, 2016**, as amended.

### Important Prohibition

India prohibited specified single-use plastic items with low utility and high littering potential from **1 July 2022**.

### Examples of Prohibited Items

- Plastic cutlery.
- Plastic plates, cups and glasses.
- Plastic straws.
- Plastic stirrers.
- Certain plastic wrapping films.
- Plastic sticks for balloons.
- Certain thermocol decorative items.

### Important Distinction

The prohibition applies to **specified single-use plastic items**, not every plastic product designed for a single use.

# 8.21 Composting and Biomethanation

## A. Composting

Biological decomposition of organic waste, generally under aerobic conditions.

### Products

- Compost.
- Carbon dioxide.
- Water.
- Heat.

## B. Biomethanation

Anaerobic decomposition of organic matter by microorganisms.

### Products

- Biogas.
- Digestate.

### Biogas

Contains mainly:
- Methane.
- Carbon dioxide.

### Comparison

| Composting | Biomethanation |
|---|---|
| Primarily aerobic | Anaerobic |
| Produces compost | Produces biogas and digestate |
| Requires oxygen | Occurs without oxygen |
| Suitable for biodegradable organic waste | Suitable for biodegradable organic waste |

**Prelims Trap:** Methane production is particularly associated with anaerobic decomposition.

# 8.22 Landfills

A sanitary landfill is an engineered facility for controlled waste disposal.

### Important Features

- Liner systems.
- Leachate collection.
- Landfill gas management.
- Waste compaction.
- Environmental monitoring.

### Landfill Gas

Mainly contains:
- Methane.
- Carbon dioxide.

### Important Facts

- Anaerobic decomposition in landfills produces methane.
- Leachate can contaminate groundwater if not properly managed.
- Landfills should primarily receive waste that cannot be appropriately recovered or treated.

# 8.23 Waste-to-Energy Technologies

| Technology | Process | Important Product |
|---|---|---|
| Incineration | Combustion | Heat/electricity |
| Biomethanation | Anaerobic digestion | Biogas |
| Pyrolysis | Thermal decomposition without oxygen | Gas, oil, char |
| Gasification | Partial oxidation | Syngas |

### Important Facts

# 8.24 Electronic Waste (E-Waste)

E-waste consists of discarded electrical and electronic equipment covered by applicable waste-management rules.

### Important Components

- Metals.
- Plastics.
- Glass.
- Electronic circuits.
- Batteries and other components.

### Important Environmental Concerns

- Lead.
- Mercury.
- Cadmium.
- Brominated flame retardants.
- Unsafe informal recycling practices.

### E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022

- Came into force on **1 April 2023**.
- Establish an Extended Producer Responsibility framework.
- Provide for registration and recycling-related compliance.
- Cover specified electrical and electronic equipment.

### Important Distinction

E-waste and battery waste are regulated through separate rules.

# 8.25 Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

**Extended Producer Responsibility** is an environmental policy approach under which producers bear specified responsibilities for the post-consumer management of products or packaging.

### Important Applications in India

- Plastic packaging waste.
- E-waste.
- Battery waste.
- Waste tyres under the applicable hazardous-waste framework.

### Important Facts

- EPR aims to shift part of the waste-management responsibility towards producers.
- EPR obligations differ among waste categories.
- EPR may involve collection, recycling, recovery and compliance targets.

**Prelims Trap:** EPR does not mean that only producers are responsible for every aspect of waste management.

# 8.30 Noise Pollution

Noise pollution refers to unwanted or excessive sound that adversely affects health or the environment.

### Measurement

Sound levels are commonly expressed in **decibels (dB)**.

Environmental noise standards in India generally use **dB(A) Leq**.

## Ambient Noise Standards in India

| Area | Daytime | Nighttime |
|---|---:|---:|
| Industrial | 75 dB(A) | 70 dB(A) |
| Commercial | 65 dB(A) | 55 dB(A) |
| Residential | 55 dB(A) | 45 dB(A) |
| Silence Zone | 50 dB(A) | 40 dB(A) |

### Important Facts

- Daytime: **6 a.m. to 10 p.m.**
- Nighttime: **10 p.m. to 6 a.m.**
- Silence zones are declared according to applicable rules.
- Noise can affect humans and wildlife.

### Important Distinction

Decibels are logarithmic units; sound levels cannot be interpreted as ordinary linear quantities.

# 8.31 Radioactive Pollution

Radioactive pollution involves the presence or release of radioactive substances at harmful levels.

### Important Sources

- Nuclear accidents.
- Improper radioactive-waste disposal.
- Mining and processing of radioactive materials.
- Certain medical and industrial activities.

### Important Radiation Types

| Radiation | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| Alpha | Low penetration; high ionizing ability |
| Beta | Moderate penetration |
| Gamma | High penetration |
| Neutron | Highly penetrating under many conditions |

### Important Facts

- Ionizing radiation can damage biological tissues and DNA.
- Alpha radiation is particularly hazardous when alpha-emitting materials enter the body.
- Radioactive half-life is the time required for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay.

# 8.32 Light Pollution

Light pollution refers to excessive or misdirected artificial light.

### Important Forms

- Skyglow.
- Glare.
- Light trespass.
- Excessive artificial illumination.

### Ecological Effects

- Disruption of nocturnal animal behaviour.
- Effects on migratory birds.
- Disorientation of sea turtle hatchlings.
- Changes in insect activity.
- Disturbance of biological rhythms.

# 8.33 Important UPSC Prelims Distinctions

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Primary pollutant | Secondary pollutant | Directly emitted vs formed through reactions |
| PM₂.₅ | PM₁₀ | Fine particles vs broader inhalable particle category |
| Classical smog | Photochemical smog | Sulphurous smoke-related smog vs sunlight-driven oxidizing smog |
| Stratospheric ozone | Tropospheric ozone | Protective ozone layer vs harmful ground-level pollutant |
| Ozone depletion | Global warming | Stratospheric ozone loss vs climate warming |
| BOD | COD | Biological oxygen demand vs chemical oxygen demand |
| BOD | DO | Oxygen consumed in a test vs oxygen present in water |
| Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification | Accumulation within an organism vs increase across trophic levels |
| Eutrophication | Biomagnification | Nutrient enrichment vs contaminant concentration across food chains |
| Composting | Biomethanation | Aerobic decomposition vs anaerobic digestion |
| Incineration | Pyrolysis | Combustion vs thermal decomposition without oxygen |
| Recycling | Reuse | Reprocessing materials vs using an item again |
| Sanitary landfill | Open dumping | Engineered disposal vs uncontrolled disposal |
| Microplastics | Nanoplastics | Different particle-size categories |
| E-waste | Biomedical waste | Discarded electronics vs healthcare-related waste |
| Carbon monoxide | Carbon dioxide | Toxic gas from incomplete combustion vs major greenhouse gas |
