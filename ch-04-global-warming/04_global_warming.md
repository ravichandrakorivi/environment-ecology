# **1. Solar Radiation and Earth's Heat Balance**

![Rasiation Spectrum](images/01_radiation_spectrum.png)

## **1.1 Insolation**

Insolation (Incoming Solar Radiation) is the solar energy received by the Earth–atmosphere system from the Sun in the form of electromagnetic radiation.

- The Sun emits radiation predominantly in the shortwave spectrum, including ultraviolet (UV), visible and near-infrared radiation.
- Of the total solar radiation reaching the top of Earth's atmosphere:
  - Approximately 30% is reflected back into space by clouds, the atmosphere and Earth's surface.
  - Approximately 23% is absorbed by the atmosphere.
  - Approximately 47% is absorbed by Earth's surface.
- Thus, approximately 70% of incoming solar radiation is absorbed by the Earth–atmosphere system.

### **1.1.1 Factors Affecting Insolation**

The amount of insolation received at a particular location depends on:

- **Latitude:** Determines the angle at which sunlight strikes Earth's surface.
- **Season:** Influences the solar angle and duration of daylight.
- **Time of day:** Determines the Sun's elevation above the horizon.
- **Atmospheric conditions:** Clouds, aerosols and gases influence the solar radiation reaching the surface.

**Note:** Insolation refers to the total incoming solar radiation (100%), not merely the 70% absorbed by Earth's atmosphere and surface. It includes ultraviolet, visible and infrared radiation.

---

## **1.2 Terrestrial Radiation**

Terrestrial radiation is the energy emitted by Earth's surface in the form of longwave infrared radiation.

The process occurs as follows:

1. **Absorption:** Earth's surface absorbs part of the incoming solar radiation and becomes warmer.
2. **Emission:** The warmed surface emits energy as longwave infrared radiation (terrestrial radiation).
3. **Interaction with atmosphere:**
   - Some infrared radiation escapes directly into space.
   - Much of it is absorbed by greenhouse gases, such as water vapour, CO₂ and methane.
4. **Re-emission:** Greenhouse gases emit infrared radiation in all directions:
   - **Towards Earth's surface:** Contributes to surface warming (greenhouse effect).
   - **Towards space:** Contributes to Earth's energy loss.

**Note**: The Sun supplies energy mainly as shortwave radiation, while Earth's surface emits energy mainly as longwave infrared radiation. Greenhouse gases absorb and re-emit much of this longwave radiation.

### **1.2.1 Insolation vs Terrestrial Radiation**

| Feature                           | Insolation                                                         | Terrestrial radiation                                                                 |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Source                            | Sun                                                                | Earth's surface                                                                       |
| Nature                            | Predominantly shortwave                                            | Predominantly longwave infrared                                                       |
| Components                        | UV, visible and infrared                                           | Mainly infrared                                                                       |
| Peak wavelength                   | Approximately 0.5 µm                                               | Approximately 10 µm                                                                   |
| Role                              | Supplies energy to the Earth–atmosphere system                     | Transfers energy from Earth's surface to the atmosphere and space                     |
| Interaction with greenhouse gases | Much of the incoming solar radiation passes through the atmosphere | Much of the emitted infrared radiation is absorbed and re-emitted by greenhouse gases |

**Note**:

- Insolation refers to incoming solar radiation, whereas terrestrial radiation is emitted by Earth's surface.
- Greenhouse gases primarily absorb and re-emit longwave infrared radiation.

---

## **1.3 Earth's Radiation Budget**

Earth's radiation budget is the balance between incoming solar radiation and outgoing radiation to space.

### **1.3.1 Distribution of Incoming Solar Radiation**

Of the total solar radiation (100%) reaching the top of Earth's atmosphere:

| Component                                                           | Approximate percentage |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------- |
| Reflected back into space by clouds, atmosphere and Earth's surface | 30%                    |
| Absorbed by the atmosphere                                          | 23%                    |
| Absorbed by Earth's surface                                         | 47%                    |
| Total incoming solar radiation                                      | 100%                   |

![Earth's Radiation Budget](images/02_earth_radiation_budget.png)


### **1.3.2 How Does Earth Maintain Its Heat Balance?**

1. Absorption: Earth's surface and atmosphere together absorb approximately 70% of incoming solar radiation.
2. Emission: The warmed Earth's surface emits energy as longwave infrared radiation.
3. Interaction with atmosphere:
   - Some infrared radiation escapes directly into space through the atmospheric window (approximately 6%).
   - Much of the remaining infrared radiation is absorbed by greenhouse gases and clouds. The atmosphere subsequently emits infrared radiation into space (approximately 64%).
4. Energy balance: At equilibrium, the Earth–atmosphere system emits approximately 70% of the incoming solar energy back into space as longwave infrared radiation.

Thus, at equilibrium:

70% absorbed solar radiation = 70% outgoing longwave radiation.

The percentages are approximate global averages used in a simplified radiation-budget model.

### **1.3.3 Energy Balance and Temperature**

Earth's energy balance depends on two quantities:

1. Energy absorbed: Solar radiation absorbed by Earth's surface and atmosphere.
2. Energy emitted to space: Longwave infrared radiation escaping into space from Earth's surface and atmosphere.

| Condition                                                                                    | Effect                                     |
| -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ |
| Solar energy absorbed by Earth's surface and atmosphere = Infrared energy emitted into space | Energy balance; stable average temperature |
| Solar energy absorbed by Earth's surface and atmosphere > Infrared energy emitted into space | Energy surplus; Earth warms                |
| Solar energy absorbed by Earth's surface and atmosphere < Infrared energy emitted into space | Energy deficit; Earth cools                |

Example: If the Earth–atmosphere system absorbs 70 units of solar energy:

- Emits 70 units: Energy balance.
- Emits 68 units: Energy surplus of 2 units; Earth warms.
- Emits 72 units: Energy deficit of 2 units; Earth cools.     |

### **1.3.4 Albedo**

Albedo is the fraction of incoming solar radiation reflected by a surface.

- **High albedo:** More solar radiation is reflected and less is absorbed.
- **Low albedo:** Less solar radiation is reflected and more is absorbed.
- Albedo ranges from 0 to 1 (or 0% to 100%).

| Surface             | General albedo  |
| ------------------- | --------------- |
| Fresh snow          | Very high       |
| Ice                 | Generally high  |
| Deserts             | Relatively high |
| Forests             | Low             |
| Dark ocean surfaces | Low             |

Earth's planetary albedo is approximately 0.30 (30%), meaning about 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected back into space.

### **1.3.5 Ice–Albedo Feedback**

Ice–albedo feedback is a process in which melting snow and ice amplify global warming.

1. Rising temperatures cause snow and ice to melt.
2. Melting exposes darker land or ocean surfaces with lower albedo.
3. These surfaces absorb more solar radiation, causing additional warming.
4. Additional warming accelerates further melting.

This is called positive feedback because it amplifies the initial warming.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### **1.3.6 UPSC Prelims Key Points**

- Approximately 30% of incoming solar radiation is reflected and 70% is absorbed by the Earth–atmosphere system.
- At equilibrium, the absorbed energy is balanced by outgoing longwave infrared radiation.
- Albedo measures reflection, not absorption.
- Melting snow and ice generally reduce albedo and increase solar energy absorption.
- Positive ice–albedo feedback amplifies global warming.

# **2. Greenhouse Effect**

## **2.1 Meaning and Mechanism**

The greenhouse effect is the warming of Earth's surface and lower atmosphere due to the absorption and re-emission of longwave infrared radiation by greenhouse gases.

**Process:**

1. **Incoming solar radiation:** Shortwave solar radiation passes through the atmosphere, and part of it is absorbed by Earth's surface.
2. **Surface warming:** The absorbed solar energy warms Earth's surface.
3. **Terrestrial radiation:** The warmed surface emits longwave infrared radiation.
4. **Absorption and re-emission:** Greenhouse gases, such as CO₂, methane and water vapour, absorb and re-emit infrared radiation in all directions, including towards Earth's surface.
5. **Greenhouse warming:** This reduces the rate at which energy escapes into space, keeping Earth's surface and lower atmosphere warmer.

**Important:** Greenhouse gases primarily absorb infrared radiation emitted by Earth, rather than incoming visible sunlight.

## **2.2 Natural vs Enhanced Greenhouse Effect**

| Natural greenhouse effect                                  | Enhanced greenhouse effect                                        |
| ---------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Occurs naturally due to greenhouse gases in the atmosphere | Results from increased concentrations of greenhouse gases         |
| Keeps Earth's surface warm enough to support life          | Causes additional warming of Earth's surface and lower atmosphere |
| Essential for maintaining Earth's habitable climate        | Principal mechanism behind human-induced global warming           |

- **Without the natural greenhouse effect:** Earth's estimated average surface temperature would be approximately −18°C.
- **With the natural greenhouse effect:** Earth's commonly cited average surface temperature is approximately +15°C.
- Thus, the natural greenhouse effect keeps Earth's surface approximately 33°C warmer.
- Human activities, particularly fossil-fuel combustion and deforestation, increase greenhouse gas concentrations and strengthen the greenhouse effect.

# **3. Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)**

## **3.1 Major Greenhouse Gases**

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) are atmospheric gases that absorb and emit infrared radiation, contributing to the greenhouse effect.

| Greenhouse gas       | Major sources                                                            | Important facts                                                    |
| -------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Water vapour (H₂O)   | Evaporation and transpiration                                            | Major natural greenhouse gas; primarily acts as a climate feedback |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Fossil fuel combustion, cement production, deforestation                 | Largest contributor to long-term human-induced warming             |
| Methane (CH₄)        | Wetlands, rice cultivation, livestock, landfills, fossil fuel extraction | Powerful greenhouse gas                                            |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O)  | Agricultural soils, nitrogen fertilisers, industry                       | Long-lived greenhouse gas                                          |
| Ozone (O₃)           | Atmospheric chemical reactions                                           | Tropospheric ozone is both a greenhouse gas and an air pollutant   |
| Fluorinated gases    | Refrigeration, industrial processes, electrical equipment                | Many have very high global warming potential                       |

![Major sources of GHGs](images/GHGs_sources.png)

## **3.2 Important Distinctions**

- **Nitrogen (N₂) and oxygen (O₂):** Not significant greenhouse gases because they absorb very little terrestrial infrared radiation.
- **Water vapour:** Primarily acts as a climate feedback. Warming increases atmospheric water vapour, which can amplify warming.
- Tropospheric ozone: Contributes to global warming and air pollution.
- **Stratospheric ozone:** Absorbs harmful ultraviolet radiation and also participates in Earth's radiative balance.
- **Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs):** Both greenhouse gases and ozone-depleting substances.

## **3.3 Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Rice Cultivation**

Rice cultivation contributes to global warming mainly through emissions of methane (CH₄) and nitrous oxide (N₂O).

| Gas                 | How it is produced                                                                                                             |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Methane (CH₄)       | Flooded paddy fields develop oxygen-deficient (anaerobic) conditions, allowing methane-producing microorganisms to produce CH₄ |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O) | Nitrogen fertilisers supply nitrogen that soil microorganisms can convert into N₂O through nitrification and denitrification   |

**Important Points:**

- **Methanogens:** Microorganisms that produce methane under anaerobic conditions.
- **Nitrification:** Microbial conversion of ammonium into nitrite and nitrate; can release N₂O.
- **Denitrification:** Microbial conversion of nitrate/nitrite into gaseous nitrogen compounds, including N₂O.
- N₂O emissions depend on soil moisture, oxygen availability and nitrogen management. Continuously flooded soils generally favour methane production, while alternating wet and dry conditions can favour N₂O emissions.

# **4. Global Warming and Climate Change**

| Global warming                                            | Climate change                                                                    |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Long-term increase in Earth's average surface temperature | Long-term changes in temperature, precipitation and other climate characteristics |
| Refers specifically to rising temperatures                | Includes changes in rainfall patterns, sea levels and extreme weather events      |
| Major component of present-day climate change             | Broader phenomenon that includes global warming and its consequences              |

## **4.1 Causes of Modern Global Warming**

Human activities are the principal cause of modern global warming because they increase greenhouse gas concentrations in the atmosphere.

| Human activity                             | Major greenhouse gases released |
| ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| Burning coal, petroleum and natural gas    | CO₂                             |
| Deforestation and land-use changes         | CO₂                             |
| Agriculture and livestock rearing          | CH₄ and N₂O                     |
| Industrial processes and cement production | CO₂ and fluorinated gases       |
| Landfills and waste management             | CH₄ and N₂O                     |

**Natural influences:**

- **Solar variability:** Changes in solar energy reaching Earth.
- **Volcanic eruptions:** Large eruptions generally cause temporary cooling because sulphate aerosols reflect sunlight.
- **Orbital changes:** Long-term variations in Earth's orbit and axial orientation influence climate over thousands of years.

**Note:** Human activities, particularly greenhouse gas emissions, are the dominant cause of observed global warming since the Industrial Revolution. Natural factors cannot explain the magnitude of recent warming.

## **4.2 Global Warming Potential (GWP)**

Global Warming Potential (GWP) measures the cumulative warming effect of emitting one unit mass of a greenhouse gas relative to the same mass of CO₂ over a specified period.

- CO₂: Reference gas with GWP = 1.
- Standard comparison period: 100 years (GWP₁₀₀).
- Higher GWP: Greater warming effect per unit mass of emissions over the specified period.

| Greenhouse gas             | 100-year GWP (IPCC AR6) |
| -------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂)       | 1                       |
| Methane (CH₄)              | 27–30                   |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O)        | 273                     |
| Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) | 25,200                  |

## **4.3 Atmospheric Abundance of Greenhouse Gases**

Atmospheric abundance refers to the concentration of a greenhouse gas present in Earth's atmosphere.

It is commonly expressed in:

- ppm (parts per million): 1 molecule per million air molecules.
- ppb (parts per billion): 1 molecule per billion air molecules.
- ppt (parts per trillion): 1 molecule per trillion air molecules.

| Greenhouse gas       | Approximate atmospheric abundance        |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------- |
| Water vapour (H₂O)   | 0–4% by volume (highly variable)         |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | 425 ppm                                  |
| Methane (CH₄)        | 1,940 ppb (1.94 ppm)                     |
| Nitrous oxide (N₂O)  | 338 ppb                                  |
| Ozone (O₃)           | Variable; generally trace concentrations |
| Fluorinated gases    | Very low; generally measured in ppt      |

Values are approximate recent global concentrations; actual concentrations vary by year and location.

**Note**:

- Water vapour is the most abundant greenhouse gas, but its concentration varies considerably with temperature and humidity.
- CO₂ is the most abundant well-mixed, long-lived greenhouse gas among CO₂, CH₄ and N₂O.
- Methane and N₂O are present in much smaller concentrations than CO₂ but have higher GWP values.
- Atmospheric abundance and GWP are different: Abundance measures concentration, whereas GWP measures warming impact per unit mass of emissions over a specified period.
- Nitrogen (N₂) and oxygen (O₂) are the most abundant atmospheric gases, but they are not significant greenhouse gases.

# **5. Carbon Cycle and Carbon Sinks**

The carbon cycle is the movement of carbon between the atmosphere, living organisms, oceans, soils and rocks through natural processes.

## **5.1 Carbon Sources and Sinks**

- **Carbon source:** Releases more carbon than it absorbs over a specified period.
- **Carbon sink:** Absorbs more carbon than it releases over a specified period.
- **Carbon reservoir:** Stores carbon; it may act as a source or sink depending on carbon exchanges.

| Carbon sink or reservoir               | Role                                                                       |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Forests                                | Absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in vegetation and soils |
| Oceans                                 | Absorb atmospheric CO₂ through physical and biological processes           |
| Soils                                  | Store carbon mainly as soil organic matter                                 |
| Wetlands and peatlands                 | Store large quantities of carbon, particularly in waterlogged soils        |
| Mangroves, seagrasses and salt marshes | Capture and store blue carbon in vegetation and sediments                  |

**Important:** A forest can become a carbon source if carbon released through fires, deforestation and decomposition exceeds the carbon absorbed through photosynthesis.

## **5.2 Role of Phytoplankton**

- **Phytoplankton:** Microscopic photosynthetic organisms found in aquatic ecosystems.
- They absorb dissolved CO₂ during photosynthesis.
- This reduces CO₂ in surface waters, facilitating further absorption of atmospheric CO₂ by oceans.
- Some of the carbon fixed by phytoplankton is transported into deeper ocean waters through the biological carbon pump.

**Biological carbon pump:** The transfer of carbon from surface waters to deeper ocean waters through biological processes, including the sinking of dead organisms, faecal pellets and other organic particles.

## **5.3 Blue Carbon**

Blue carbon refers to carbon captured and stored by oceans and coastal ecosystems.

Major coastal blue-carbon ecosystems include:

- Mangrove forests
- Seagrass meadows
- Salt marshes

These ecosystems absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in vegetation and especially in waterlogged sediments.

**Note:**

- **Blue carbon:** Carbon captured and stored by marine and coastal ecosystems, particularly mangroves, seagrasses and salt marshes.
- **Green carbon:** Carbon captured and stored by terrestrial vegetation, particularly forests.
- **Black carbon:** Soot particles produced by incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, biomass and biofuels. It contributes to global warming by absorbing sunlight and reducing snow and ice albedo.
- **Grey carbon:** A less-standardised term sometimes used for carbon emissions associated with fossil-fuel combustion and industrial activities. Its meaning varies by context.
- **Peatlands:** Important carbon stores. Inland peatlands are not conventionally classified as coastal blue-carbon ecosystems.

**UPSC Prelims Key Point**: Black carbon is a short-lived climate pollutant, not a greenhouse gas. It contributes to warming primarily by absorbing solar radiation.

## **5.4 Carbon Fertilization**

Carbon fertilization refers to increased plant photosynthesis or growth resulting from higher atmospheric CO₂ concentrations.

- CO₂ is a raw material for photosynthesis.
- Increased CO₂ can stimulate photosynthesis and growth in some plants.
- The response depends on water availability, nutrients, temperature and other environmental conditions.
- Increased atmospheric CO₂ does not guarantee increased growth in all plants.

# **6. Climate Feedback Mechanisms**

A climate feedback is a process that either amplifies or reduces an initial change in Earth's climate.

## **6.1 Types of Climate Feedback**

- **Positive feedback:** Amplifies the initial climatic change.
- **Negative feedback:** Opposes the initial climatic change.

## **6.2 Major Climate Feedback Mechanisms**

| Feedback                   | Mechanism                                                                                                                | Type     |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | -------- |
| Water vapour feedback      | Warming increases atmospheric water vapour, strengthening the greenhouse effect and causing further warming              | Positive |
| Ice–albedo feedback        | Warming melts snow and ice, exposing darker surfaces that absorb more solar radiation and cause further warming          | Positive |
| Permafrost carbon feedback | Warming thaws frozen soils, allowing decomposition of organic matter and release of CO₂ and CH₄, causing further warming | Positive |
| Planck feedback            | Warming increases Earth's outgoing infrared radiation, reducing the initial warming                                      | Negative |

# **7. Methane Hydrates**

## **7.1 What Are Methane Hydrates?**

Methane hydrates are ice-like crystalline substances in which methane (CH₄) molecules are trapped within cage-like structures formed by water (H₂O) molecules.

- Also called methane clathrates or gas hydrates.
- They contain large quantities of methane, a powerful greenhouse gas.
- They are sometimes called burning ice because the released methane can burn.

## **7.2 Formation and Distribution**

Methane hydrates form when methane and water are present under suitable high-pressure and low-temperature conditions.

| Location           | Occurrence                                                                       |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Ocean floor        | Mainly in sediments beneath the seafloor, particularly along continental margins |
| Permafrost regions | In permanently frozen ground, including Arctic regions                           |

## **7.3 Methane Release and Global Warming**

1. **Destabilisation:** Rising temperatures or decreasing pressure can cause methane hydrates to dissociate into methane gas and water.
2. **Methane release:** Methane may escape from the deposits into surrounding sediments or water.
3. **Atmospheric entry:** Some released methane may reach the atmosphere. However, much of the methane released from marine sediments may dissolve or be oxidised before reaching it.
4. **Global warming:** Methane entering the atmosphere strengthens the greenhouse effect and contributes to further warming.

**Climate feedback:** Global warming can destabilise methane hydrates, potentially releasing methane that causes additional warming. This is a positive climate feedback.

## **7.4 Fate of Methane in the Atmosphere**

- Atmospheric methane is removed mainly through chemical reactions with hydroxyl (OH) radicals.
- Its atmospheric lifetime is approximately a decade.
- Methane oxidation ultimately produces carbon dioxide (CO₂) and water (H₂O).

Overall reaction for methane oxidation:

$$
\boxed{\mathrm{CH_4 + 2O_2 \rightarrow CO_2 + 2H_2O}}
$$

- CH₄: Methane
- O₂: Oxygen
- CO₂: Carbon dioxide
- H₂O: Water

**Note:** In the atmosphere, methane undergoes a series of chemical reactions initiated mainly by hydroxyl (OH) radicals. The equation represents the overall net oxidation reaction, not a single atmospheric reaction step.

# **8. Impacts of Global Warming and Climate Change**

## **8.1 Major Impacts**

| Impact                          | Consequence                                                                  |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Rising temperatures             | More frequent and intense heatwaves                                          |
| Altered precipitation           | Changes in rainfall patterns, increased drought and heavy rainfall risks     |
| Melting glaciers and ice sheets | Changes in freshwater availability and rising sea levels                     |
| Ocean warming                   | Marine heatwaves, coral bleaching and marine ecosystem disruption            |
| Ocean acidification             | Reduced carbonate availability, affecting corals and shell-forming organisms |
| Biodiversity loss               | Habitat shifts, species migration and increased extinction risks             |
| Agricultural impacts            | Changes in crop yields, growing seasons and food security                    |
| Sea-level rise                  | Coastal flooding, erosion and saltwater intrusion                            |

## **8.2 Climate Impacts at Higher Warming Levels**

As global temperatures increase, climate impacts generally become more severe.

![](images/climate_impacts.png)

### **8.2.1 Terrestrial Biosphere Becoming a Carbon Source**

- Terrestrial biosphere: Land-based living organisms and ecosystems, including forests, grasslands and their associated soils.
- Normally, many terrestrial ecosystems act as carbon sinks, absorbing more CO₂ than they release.
- Higher temperatures, droughts, forest fires and vegetation loss can reduce carbon absorption and increase carbon emissions.
- Consequently, some terrestrial ecosystems may change from net carbon sinks to net carbon sources.

### **8.2.2 Widespread Coral Mortality**

- Increasing ocean temperatures cause more frequent and severe coral bleaching.
- Prolonged heat stress can lead to widespread coral mortality.
- Coral reefs are particularly vulnerable to higher levels of global warming.

## **8.3 Coral Bleaching**

Coral bleaching occurs when stressed corals lose their symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) or the algae's photosynthetic pigments, causing the corals to appear white.

**Mechanism:**

1. Rising sea temperatures cause heat stress in corals.
2. Corals expel or lose their symbiotic algae.
3. Corals lose much of their colour and an important source of nutrition.
4. Prolonged stress can cause coral starvation and mortality.

Important points:

- Coral bleaching does not necessarily mean coral death.
- Corals may recover if environmental conditions improve.
- Repeated or prolonged bleaching increases mortality risk.
- Ocean acidification creates additional stress for reef-building corals.

## **8.4 Ocean Acidification**

Ocean acidification is the reduction in seawater pH caused primarily by the absorption of atmospheric CO₂.

**Mechanism:**

1. Oceans absorb atmospheric CO₂.
2. Dissolved CO₂ reacts with water to form carbonic acid.
3. Carbonic acid releases hydrogen ions, reducing seawater pH.
4. Increased hydrogen ions reduce the availability of carbonate ions needed by many marine organisms to form calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.

Chemical reactions:

$$
\mathrm{CO_2 + H_2O \rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3}
$$

$$
\mathrm{H_2CO_3 \rightleftharpoons H^+ + HCO_3^-}
$$

$$
\mathrm{H^+ + CO_3^{2-} \rightleftharpoons HCO_3^-}
$$

**Note:**

- Ocean acidification is primarily caused by increasing atmospheric CO₂.
- Seawater remains generally alkaline (pH above 7) despite becoming less alkaline.
- Corals, molluscs and certain plankton are particularly vulnerable.
- Ocean acidification and ocean warming are different processes, although both are linked to increasing atmospheric CO₂.

# **9. Carbon Sequestration and Carbon Storage**

## **9.1 Meaning**

Carbon sequestration is the process of capturing and storing carbon in biological, geological or other reservoirs.

It helps mitigate global warming by removing CO₂ from the atmosphere or preventing captured CO₂ from entering the atmosphere.

## **9.2 Types of Carbon Sequestration**

| Type                            | Mechanism                                                                                            | Examples                                                                       |
| ------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Biological carbon sequestration | Living organisms, particularly plants, absorb atmospheric CO₂ and store carbon in biomass and soils. | Forests, grasslands, soils, wetlands                                           |
| Geological carbon sequestration | CO₂ is injected into suitable underground geological formations for long-term storage.               | Depleted oil and gas reservoirs, deep saline formations, unmineable coal seams |

Distinction: Biological sequestration involves natural carbon uptake and storage, whereas geological sequestration involves engineered underground CO₂ storage.

## **9.3 Biological Carbon Sequestration**

![](images/biological_carbon_sequestration.png)

Biological carbon sequestration occurs when plants absorb atmospheric CO₂ through photosynthesis and store carbon in vegetation and soils.

| Ecosystem          | Carbon storage                                                                                          |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Forests            | Trees store carbon in trunks, branches, leaves and roots; forest soils also store organic carbon.       |
| Grasslands         | Carbon is stored mainly in plant roots and soil organic matter, as well as above-ground vegetation.     |
| Wetlands           | Carbon accumulates in vegetation and waterlogged soils, where decomposition is often slow.              |
| Coastal ecosystems | Mangroves, seagrass meadows and salt marshes capture and store blue carbon in vegetation and sediments. |

## **9.4 Geological Carbon Sequestration**

Geological carbon sequestration is the long-term storage of CO₂ in suitable underground geological formations.

Captured CO₂ is injected into deep underground formations, where geological trapping mechanisms help prevent its escape.

### **9.4.1 Major Geological CO₂ Storage Sites**

| Storage site                       | How CO₂ is stored                                                                                                                                             |
| ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Depleted oil and gas reservoirs | CO₂ is injected into porous rock formations that previously contained oil or natural gas. Overlying low-permeability rocks help retain the injected CO₂.      |
| Deep saline formations          | CO₂ is injected into deep porous rocks containing saline water. It can be trapped in pore spaces, dissolve in saline water and react with minerals over time. |
| Unmineable coal seams           | CO₂ is injected into suitable coal seams, where it is adsorbed onto the internal surfaces of coal.                                                            |

Important terms

- Porous rock: Rock containing small spaces (pores) that can hold fluids.
- Permeable rock: Rock that allows fluids to move through connected pores or fractures.
- Caprock: A low-permeability rock layer that acts as a barrier to upward CO₂ movement.
- Adsorption: Accumulation of molecules on the surface of a material.
- Absorption: Entry of a substance into the bulk of another material.

### **9.4.2 Mechanisms of Geological CO₂ Storage**

| Mechanism           | How CO₂ is retained                                                                |
| ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Structural trapping | CO₂ is retained beneath low-permeability caprock and within geological structures. |
| Residual trapping   | CO₂ becomes immobilised as small disconnected quantities within rock pores.        |
| Solubility trapping | CO₂ dissolves in underground saline water.                                         |
| Mineral trapping    | Dissolved CO₂ reacts with minerals and can form stable carbonate minerals.         |

Geological storage requires suitable reservoir rocks, effective trapping mechanisms and monitoring to minimise leakage.

## **9.5 Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)**

Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) is the complete technological process of capturing CO₂ from an emission source, transporting it and storing it for the long term, generally in underground geological formations.

### **9.5.1 Stages of CCS**

1. **Capture:** CO₂ is separated from emissions produced by facilities such as cement factories, steel plants and power stations.
2. **Transport:** Captured CO₂ is transported, commonly through pipelines, to a suitable storage site.
3. **Injection:** CO₂ is injected into a suitable underground geological formation.
4. **Storage and monitoring:** CO₂ is retained underground, and the storage site is monitored for containment and possible leakage.

### **9.5.2 CCS vs Geological Carbon Sequestration**

![](images/ccs_geographical_carbon_sequestration.png)

| Feature | Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)                                              | Geological carbon sequestration                                  |
| ------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Meaning | Complete process of capturing, transporting and storing CO₂                   | Long-term storage of CO₂ in underground geological formations    |
| Scope   | Includes capture, transport and storage                                       | Focuses on underground injection and storage                     |
| Example | Capturing CO₂ from a cement plant, transporting it and storing it underground | Injecting CO₂ into a deep saline formation for long-term storage |

Relationship: Geological carbon sequestration is the underground storage component of CCS. The two terms are closely related but are not identical.

## **9.6 Agricultural Practices and Soil Carbon Sequestration**

### **9.6.1 Meaning**

Soil carbon sequestration is the process of increasing the amount of carbon stored in soil, mainly as soil organic carbon (SOC).

Soil organic carbon comes from decomposed plant residues, dead roots, microorganisms and other organic matter.

Agricultural practices can help increase or conserve soil carbon by:

1. **Increasing organic matter inputs:** Adding plant residues, roots and other organic materials to soil.
2. **Reducing soil erosion:** Preventing the loss of carbon-rich topsoil through water or wind.
3. **Reducing soil disturbance:** Limiting disruption of soil structure and conditions that can accelerate organic matter decomposition.

### **9.6.2 Important Agricultural Practices**

| Practice           | Meaning                                                                                                                      | Contribution to soil carbon storage                                                                           |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Contour bunding | Constructing earthen embankments along contour lines (lines joining places of equal elevation) on sloping agricultural land. | Slows surface runoff and reduces soil erosion, helping prevent the loss of soil organic carbon.               |
| Relay cropping  | Sowing a second crop before harvesting the first crop, so both crops grow together for a short period.                       | Maintains vegetation cover and can increase plant residues and root-derived organic matter entering the soil. |
| Zero tillage    | Sowing crops without conventional ploughing, with minimal soil disturbance.                                                  | Reduces soil disturbance and erosion, helping conserve soil organic carbon, particularly near the surface.    |

# **10. Climate Change Mitigation and Adaptation**

Climate change can be addressed through two major approaches: mitigation and adaptation.

## **10.1 Climate Change Mitigation**

Mitigation refers to measures that reduce greenhouse gas emissions or increase the removal of greenhouse gases from the atmosphere.

Its objective is to limit the magnitude of future climate change.

**Examples:**

- **Renewable energy:** Replacing fossil fuels with solar, wind and other low-emission energy sources.
- **Energy efficiency:** Reducing energy consumption for the same output.
- **Afforestation and reforestation:** Increasing CO₂ absorption through vegetation.
- **Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS):** Capturing CO₂ from emission sources and storing it underground.
- **Methane emission reduction:** Reducing methane leakage from oil and gas operations, landfills and other sources.

**Note:**
- Afforestation: Establishing forests on land that was not previously forested.
- Reforestation: Re-establishing forests on land where forests were previously removed or destroyed.

## **10.2 Climate Change Adaptation**

Adaptation refers to adjustments in human or natural systems in response to actual or expected climate change and its effects.

Its objective is to reduce climate-related risks and vulnerability.

**Examples:**

- **Flood protection:** Constructing embankments and improving drainage systems.
- **Drought-resistant crops:** Using crop varieties that tolerate water scarcity.
- **Heat Action Plans:** Establishing early warnings and protective measures against extreme heat.
- **Water conservation:** Rainwater harvesting and efficient irrigation.
- **Coastal protection:** Restoring mangroves and constructing suitable coastal defences.

### **10.3 Mitigation vs Adaptation**

| Feature   | Mitigation                                              | Adaptation                                                   |
| --------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Focus     | Causes of climate change                                | Impacts of climate change                                    |
| Objective | Limit future climate change                             | Reduce climate-related risks                                 |
| Approach  | Reduce emissions or increase greenhouse gas removals    | Adjust to actual or expected climatic changes                |
| Examples  | Renewable energy, energy efficiency, afforestation, CCS | Flood protection, drought-resistant crops, Heat Action Plans |

**Important:** Some measures serve both purposes. For example, mangrove restoration can store carbon (mitigation) and reduce coastal flood damage (adaptation).

## **10.4 Important Climate Change Terms**

### **10.4.1 Carbon Footprint**

The carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emissions attributable to a person, product, activity or organisation, including relevant direct and indirect emissions.

- Expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e).
- CO₂e allows different greenhouse gases to be compared using their global warming potential (GWP).

![](images/carbon_footprint.png)

### **10.4.2 Net-Zero Emissions**

Net-zero emissions means achieving a balance between greenhouse gas emissions caused by human activities and greenhouse gases removed from the atmosphere through human activities over a specified period.

It does not mean completely stopping greenhouse gas emissions.

**How is net zero achieved?**

1. Reducing emissions: Using renewable energy, improving energy efficiency, adopting cleaner transport and reducing industrial emissions.
2. Removing greenhouse gases: Increasing carbon removal through afforestation, reforestation and technologies such as Direct Air Carbon Capture and Storage (DACCS).
3. Balancing residual emissions: Ensuring that the greenhouse gases still emitted are balanced by an equivalent amount of greenhouse gas removals.

**Example:**

If human activities emit 100 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e) and human-caused removals equal 100 tonnes of CO₂e over the same period, net-zero greenhouse gas emissions are achieved.

**Important facts:**

- Net zero refers to a balance between emissions and removals, not the complete elimination of emissions.
- Different greenhouse gases are expressed in a common unit called CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e).
- Conventional Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS) prevents captured CO₂ from entering the atmosphere but does not itself remove CO₂ already present in the atmosphere.

**Net-Zero CO₂ Emissions:**

Net-zero CO₂ emissions occur when human-caused CO₂ emissions alone are balanced by human-caused CO₂ removals.

**Note:** Net-zero greenhouse gas emissions cover all greenhouse gases, whereas net-zero CO₂ emissions cover only carbon dioxide.

![](images/net_zero_emissions.png)

### **10.4.3 Carbon Budget**

A remaining carbon budget is the estimated cumulative amount of CO₂ that humanity can still emit while limiting global warming to a specified temperature threshold with a stated probability.

- Usually expressed in gigatonnes of CO₂ (GtCO₂).
- Example: Remaining CO₂ emissions compatible with limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C above pre-industrial levels.
- The remaining budget decreases as CO₂ emissions continue.

### **10.4.4 Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS)**

CCS is a technological process involving:

1. Capture of CO₂ from emission sources.
2. Transport of captured CO₂.
3. Long-term storage, generally in suitable underground geological formations.

CCS and geological carbon sequestration have already been covered in Section 8.

## **10.5 Social Cost of Carbon (SCC)**

### **10.5.1 Meaning**

The Social Cost of Carbon (SCC) is the estimated monetary value of the additional damages caused by emitting one extra tonne of carbon dioxide (CO₂) in a particular year.

It represents the estimated economic cost of the resulting climate impacts over time.

### **10.5.2 Damages Included**

| Category                    | Examples of climate-related damages                        |
| --------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Agriculture                 | Reduced crop yields and agricultural productivity          |
| Human health                | Heat-related illnesses, mortality and other health impacts |
| Infrastructure and property | Damage from floods, storms and sea-level rise              |
| Environment                 | Ecosystem degradation and loss of ecosystem services       |

# **11. Effects of Clouds**

## **11.1 Two Effects of Clouds**

| Effect         | Mechanism                                                                                                     | Impact                                                  |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cooling effect | Clouds reflect incoming shortwave solar radiation back into space.                                            | Reduces solar energy absorbed by Earth.                 |
| Warming effect | Clouds absorb and emit outgoing longwave infrared radiation, reducing the amount of heat escaping into space. | Increases heat retained by the Earth–atmosphere system. |

The net effect depends on which process dominates.

## **11.2 Why Cirrus Clouds Are Different**

### **11.2.1 High-altitude cirrus clouds**

![](images/high_altitude_cirrus_clouds.jpeg)

- Thin clouds composed mainly of ice crystals.
- Generally reflect relatively little incoming sunlight.
- Absorb and emit outgoing infrared radiation.
- Usually produce a net warming effect.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### **11.2.2 Low-altitude, thick clouds**

![](images/low_altitude_clouds.jpeg)

- Reflect substantial incoming sunlight.
- Also absorb and emit infrared radiation.
- Their solar reflection generally dominates.
- Usually produce a net cooling effect.

## **11.3 Why Cirrus Cloud Thinning Is Proposed**

Cirrus cloud thinning aims to reduce the infrared-trapping effect of certain high-altitude cirrus clouds.

**The proposed sequence is:**

Thinner or fewer suitable cirrus clouds → Less infrared trapping → More outgoing infrared radiation escapes to space → Potential cooling.

However, this outcome is not guaranteed because changing clouds also changes how much sunlight they reflect.

**Note:** Clouds can both cool and warm the Earth. Low, thick clouds generally have a net cooling effect, whereas high, thin cirrus clouds generally have a net warming effect.

# **12. Solar Radiation Modification and Climate Intervention**

## **12.1 Meaning**

Solar Radiation Modification (SRM) refers to proposed climate intervention techniques intended to reduce global warming by modifying Earth's radiation balance.

These techniques aim to reduce the amount of heat retained by the Earth–atmosphere system.

Two important proposed techniques are:

1. Stratospheric Aerosol Injection
2. Cirrus Cloud Thinning

## **12.2 Stratospheric Aerosol Injection (SAI)**

![](images/stratospheric_aerosol_injection.png)

Stratospheric Aerosol Injection is a proposed technique involving the introduction of reflective particles, such as sulphate aerosols, into the stratosphere.

**Mechanism:**

1. Reflective aerosol particles are introduced into the stratosphere.
2. These particles scatter a portion of incoming solar radiation back into space.
3. Less solar energy is absorbed by the Earth–atmosphere system, potentially reducing global temperatures.

**Natural example:** Major volcanic eruptions can inject sulphur dioxide (SO₂) into the stratosphere, where it forms sulphate aerosols that temporarily cool the Earth.

## **12.3 Cirrus Cloud Thinning (CCT)**

Cirrus Cloud Thinning is a proposed technique intended to reduce the warming effect of certain high-altitude cirrus clouds.

**Mechanism:**

1. Cirrus clouds are high-altitude clouds composed mainly of ice crystals.
2. They absorb and emit outgoing longwave infrared radiation, often producing a net warming effect.
3. Modifying the formation or properties of suitable cirrus clouds could reduce their infrared-trapping effect.
4. More infrared radiation could escape into space, potentially reducing global warming.

**Important:** Cirrus cloud thinning is a proposed and uncertain technique. Its effectiveness depends on cloud conditions, and some interventions could produce unintended warming.

![](images/cirrus_cloud_thinning.png)

## **12.4 Comparison of the Two Techniques**

| Feature            | Stratospheric Aerosol Injection            | Cirrus Cloud Thinning                        |
| ------------------ | ------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Target             | Stratospheric aerosols                     | High-altitude cirrus clouds                  |
| Radiation affected | Incoming shortwave solar radiation         | Outgoing longwave infrared radiation         |
| Proposed mechanism | Increase reflection of sunlight into space | Reduce infrared trapping by cirrus clouds    |
| Intended result    | Reduce solar energy absorbed by Earth      | Increase infrared energy escaping into space |

Scientific distinction: Cirrus cloud thinning is often discussed alongside SRM as a form of radiation-based climate intervention. Strictly speaking, it primarily modifies outgoing terrestrial infrared radiation rather than incoming solar radiation.

## **12.5 Limitations**

- Neither technique directly removes excess atmospheric CO₂.
- Neither addresses ocean acidification caused by increased atmospheric CO₂.
- Both involve scientific uncertainties and potential environmental, climatic and governance risks.
- Neither is an established, proven solution for large-scale climate change mitigation.

## Section 6: Climate Feedback Mechanisms

Q15. Consider the following climate feedback mechanisms:

1\. Water vapour feedback\
2\. Ice–albedo feedback\
3\. Permafrost carbon feedback\
4\. Planck feedback

Which of the above are generally positive climate feedback mechanisms?

A. 1 and 4 only\
B. 2 and 4 only\
C. 1, 2 and 3 only\
D. 1, 2, 3 and 4

Answer: C

> Explanation:
>
> - Water vapour feedback: Additional warming increases atmospheric water vapour and strengthens greenhouse warming.
> - Ice–albedo feedback: Melting snow and ice expose darker surfaces, increasing solar absorption.
> - Permafrost carbon feedback: Thawing can release CO₂ and methane, amplifying warming.
> - Planck feedback: Increased outgoing infrared radiation opposes warming and is therefore a negative feedback.

Q16. Which one of the following best explains the Planck feedback in Earth's climate system?

A. Warming increases atmospheric methane concentrations through permafrost thaw.\

C. Warming melts ice, exposing darker surfaces that absorb more sunlight.\
D. Warming increases outgoing infrared radiation, thereby opposing the initial warming.

Answer: D

> Explanation:
>
> Planck feedback is a negative climate feedback. As Earth becomes warmer, it emits more infrared radiation into space, which tends to reduce the initial warming.
>
> The other options describe positive feedback mechanisms.

Q18. Which one of the following correctly describes the principal atmospheric removal mechanism of methane?

A. Direct conversion into oxygen through sunlight\
B. Permanent trapping in the stratosphere\
C. Chemical oxidation initiated mainly by hydroxyl radicals\
D. Immediate conversion into solid carbon particles

Answer: C

> Explanation:
>

>
>
>$$

>$$
>
>
