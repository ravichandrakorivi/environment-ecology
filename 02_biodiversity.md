# Chapter 2: Biodiversity
## UPSC Civil Services Preliminary Examination – Study Notes

## 2.1 Biodiversity: Basic Concepts

### Definition

**Biodiversity (Biological Diversity)** refers to the variability among living organisms from all sources, including terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems, and the ecological complexes of which they are part.

According to the **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), 1992**, biodiversity includes diversity:

1. Within species.
2. Between species.
3. Of ecosystems.

### Important Facts

- **Walter G. Rosen:** Coined the term *biodiversity* in 1985.
- **E. O. Wilson:** Popularized the term through the 1988 publication *Biodiversity*.
- **Thomas Lovejoy:** Associated with the earlier term *biological diversity*.
- **International Day for Biological Diversity:** 22 May.
- **India:** One of the world's **17 megadiverse countries** under the commonly used Conservation International classification.

---

## 2.2 Levels of Biodiversity

Biodiversity is commonly classified into three levels.

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Genetic diversity** | Variation in genetic composition within a species | Different varieties of rice |
| **Species diversity** | Variety and relative abundance of species in an area | Different species of trees, birds and insects in a forest |
| **Ecosystem diversity** | Variety of ecosystems, habitats and ecological processes | Forests, grasslands, wetlands and deserts |

### A. Genetic Diversity

- Genetic variation occurs among individuals and populations of the same species.
- Provides the raw material for evolution through natural selection.
- Helps populations adapt to changing environmental conditions.

**Examples:**
- Different varieties of rice and wheat.
- Genetic variation among populations of the same wildlife species.
- Different breeds of domesticated animals.

**Prelims Trap:** Genetic diversity does not refer to the number of different species.

### B. Species Diversity

Species diversity depends on:

1. **Species richness:** Number of different species present.
2. **Species evenness:** Relative distribution of individuals among species.

**Example:**

Two forests may contain 20 species each but have different species diversity if one forest is dominated by a single species while the other has a more even distribution.

### C. Ecosystem Diversity

Refers to variation among ecosystems within a region.

**Examples in India:**
- Tropical rainforests of the Western Ghats.
- Alpine ecosystems of the Himalayas.
- Mangrove ecosystems of the Sundarbans.
- Desert ecosystems of Rajasthan.
- Coral reef ecosystems of the Gulf of Mannar.

---

## 2.3 Alpha, Beta and Gamma Diversity

These concepts are important for understanding biodiversity at different spatial scales.

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Alpha diversity (α)** | Diversity within a particular habitat or local community |
| **Beta diversity (β)** | Change in species composition between habitats or communities |
| **Gamma diversity (γ)** | Overall diversity across a larger region or landscape |

### Examples

Consider three forest patches:

- **Alpha diversity:** Species diversity within Forest A.
- **Beta diversity:** Difference in species composition between Forest A and Forest B.
- **Gamma diversity:** Total diversity across all three forests.

**Important:** Beta diversity measures species turnover or compositional differences; it is not simply the number of species shared between habitats.

# 2.4 Patterns of Biodiversity

## A. Latitudinal Gradient

Species diversity generally **increases from the poles towards the equator**.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Tropical biodiversity > Temperate biodiversity > Polar biodiversity}}
\]

This is a broad global pattern, not an absolute rule for every taxonomic group or habitat.

### Reasons for Greater Tropical Biodiversity

1. Relatively stable climatic conditions over long evolutionary periods.
2. Greater average availability of solar energy.
3. High primary productivity in many tropical ecosystems.
4. Longer periods for diversification in some tropical regions.
5. Complex habitats supporting numerous ecological niches.

### UPSC PYQ – 2011

**Statement:** Biodiversity is normally greater in lower latitudes than in higher latitudes.

**Answer: Correct.**

---

## B. Altitudinal Gradient

Species diversity generally tends to decrease with increasing altitude, although some groups exhibit maximum diversity at intermediate elevations.

| Lower Altitudes | Higher Altitudes |
|---|---|
| Generally warmer | Generally colder |
| Longer growing seasons | Shorter growing seasons |
| Often greater habitat complexity | More restrictive environmental conditions |
| Generally higher species richness | Generally lower species richness |

### UPSC PYQ – 2011

**Statement:** Along mountain gradients, biodiversity is normally greater at lower altitudes than at higher altitudes.

**Answer: Correct as a general pattern.**

---

## C. Species–Area Relationship

Associated with **Alexander von Humboldt**.

He observed that within a region, species richness generally increases with increasing area.

The relationship is commonly expressed as:

\[
\boxed{S=CA^Z}
\]

Where:

- \(S\) = Species richness
- \(A\) = Area
- \(C\) = Constant
- \(Z\) = Slope or species–area exponent

Taking logarithms:

\[
\boxed{\log S=\log C+Z\log A}
\]

### Important Facts

- Species richness generally increases with habitat area.
- On a logarithmic scale, the relationship is approximately linear over many datasets.
- The value of \(Z\) varies with the organisms, spatial scale and geographical context.

**NCERT Fact:**
- For many small-area comparisons, \(Z\) commonly lies between **0.1 and 0.2**.
- For some very large-scale comparisons, including entire continents, substantially higher slopes of approximately **0.6–1.2** have been reported.

**Prelims Trap:** Species richness does not necessarily increase in direct proportion to area.

# 2.5 Biodiversity Hotspots

## Definition

A **biodiversity hotspot** is a biogeographical region characterized by exceptional plant endemism and severe loss of its original natural vegetation.

The concept was introduced by **Norman Myers in 1988**.

### Criteria for Identifying Biodiversity Hotspots

Under the Conservation International framework, a region must satisfy **both conditions**:

1. Contain at least **1,500 endemic vascular plant species**.
2. Have lost at least **70% of its original natural vegetation**.

| Criterion | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Endemism | At least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species |
| Habitat loss | At least 70% of original natural vegetation lost |

**Important:** High species richness alone does not qualify a region as a biodiversity hotspot.

### Global Biodiversity Hotspots

- **36 biodiversity hotspots** are recognized under the widely used Conservation International classification.
- They are not restricted to tropical regions.
- They include tropical, temperate and Mediterranean-type ecosystems.

---

## 2.6 Biodiversity Hotspots in India

India overlaps with **four global biodiversity hotspots**.

| Hotspot | Indian Regions Covered |
|---|---|
| **Himalaya** | Parts of the Indian Himalayan region |
| **Indo-Burma** | Parts of Northeast India |
| **Western Ghats–Sri Lanka** | Western Ghats |
| **Sundaland** | Nicobar Islands |

### Important Distinctions

**1. Himalaya Hotspot**
- Includes parts of the Himalayan mountain system.
- Characterized by considerable altitudinal variation and endemism.

**2. Indo-Burma Hotspot**
- Includes parts of Northeast India.
- The exact coverage must be understood according to hotspot boundaries rather than assuming every northeastern state lies entirely within it.

**3. Western Ghats–Sri Lanka Hotspot**
- Covers the Western Ghats of India and Sri Lanka.
- Known for high plant, amphibian and reptile endemism.

**4. Sundaland Hotspot**
- In India, represented by the **Nicobar Islands**.
- The Andaman Islands are associated with the Indo-Burma hotspot in the commonly used hotspot delineation.

### Important UPSC Trap

The following are **not four separate biodiversity hotspots**:

- Eastern Himalayas
- Western Himalayas
- Western Ghats
- Andaman and Nicobar Islands

The recognized global hotspot names are **Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka and Sundaland**.

### UPSC PYQ – 2010

**Statement 1:** Biodiversity hotspots are located only in tropical regions.

**Incorrect.**

**Statement 2:** India has four hotspots, namely Eastern Himalayas, Western Himalayas, Western Ghats and Andaman and Nicobar Islands.

**Incorrect.**

**Answer: Neither statement is correct.**

## 2.7 Why Are the Himalayas Rich in Biodiversity?

### Major Reasons

1. Confluence of different biogeographical regions.
2. Wide variation in altitude.
3. Diverse climatic conditions.
4. Variety of vegetation types.
5. Ecological transitions between tropical, temperate and alpine ecosystems.

### UPSC PYQ – 2011

**Question:** Why is the Himalayan range particularly rich in species diversity?

**Answer:** Because it is a **confluence of different biogeographical zones**.

# 2.9 Endemic, Native, Exotic and Invasive Species

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Native species** | Occurs naturally in a particular geographical region |
| **Endemic species** | Naturally restricted to a particular geographical region |
| **Exotic species** | Introduced outside its natural distribution |
| **Invasive alien species** | Introduced species that spreads and threatens biodiversity or causes other adverse impacts |

### A. Endemic Species

Species naturally restricted to a particular region.

**Examples:**

| Species | Region |
|---|---|
| Lion-tailed macaque | Western Ghats |
| Nilgiri tahr | Western Ghats |
| Purple frog | Western Ghats |
| Nilgiri langur | Western Ghats |

**Important:** Endemism is relative to the geographical area being considered.

### B. Exotic Species

Species introduced outside their natural distribution.

An exotic species is **not necessarily invasive**.

### C. Invasive Alien Species

Invasive alien species can threaten native biodiversity through:

- Competition.
- Predation.
- Habitat alteration.
- Disease transmission.
- Changes in ecosystem processes.

### Important Examples in India

| Species | Type |
|---|---|
| Lantana camara | Invasive alien shrub |
| Parthenium hysterophorus | Invasive alien herb |
| Eichhornia crassipes (Water hyacinth) | Invasive alien aquatic plant |
| Prosopis juliflora | Invasive alien tree/shrub in many Indian ecosystems |
| African catfish (*Clarias gariepinus*) | Invasive alien fish |

**Prelims Trap:** Not every introduced species becomes invasive.

# 2.10 Keystone, Foundation, Indicator and Flagship Species

## Important Ecological Categories

| Category | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Keystone species | Has disproportionately large ecological influence relative to abundance | Sea otter |
| Foundation species | Creates or strongly structures a habitat | Reef-building corals |
| Indicator species | Indicates environmental conditions | Lichens |
| Flagship species | Used to generate public support for conservation | Tiger |
| Umbrella species | Protection of its habitat benefits many other species | Tiger in suitable landscapes |
| Ecosystem engineer | Physically modifies habitat conditions | Beaver |

### Keystone Species

A keystone species plays a disproportionately important role in maintaining ecosystem structure.

**Important characteristics:**

- Need not be the most abundant species.
- Need not be the largest species.
- Its removal may cause major ecological changes.
- Can be a predator, herbivore, plant or other organism.

### Examples

**Sea Otter**
- Predates on sea urchins.
- Helps prevent excessive grazing of kelp forests.
- Supports kelp forest ecosystem structure.

**African Elephant**
- Can suppress woody vegetation.
- Helps maintain open savanna habitats in certain ecosystems.

### UPSC PYQ – 2000

**Question:** Species that determine the ability of numerous other species to persist in a biological community are called:

**Answer: Keystone species.**

# 2.11 Biodiversity and Ecosystem Stability

Biodiversity contributes to ecosystem functioning and can enhance resilience.

### Major Ecological Benefits

1. Supports ecosystem productivity.
2. Contributes to nutrient cycling.
3. Maintains food-web interactions.
4. Supports pollination.
5. Helps regulate pests.
6. Contributes to ecosystem recovery following disturbances.

### Stability and Resilience

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Resistance | Ability to withstand disturbance without substantial change |
| Resilience | Ability to recover or reorganize following disturbance |
| Stability | Persistence of ecosystem structure or functioning over time |

**Important:** Greater biodiversity does not automatically guarantee greater stability under every circumstance. The relationship depends on species composition, ecological interactions and the nature of disturbance.

---

# 2.12 Ecosystem Services Provided by Biodiversity

Biodiversity supports several services essential for human well-being.

### Classification

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Provisioning | Food, timber, medicinal resources |
| Regulating | Pollination, climate regulation, erosion control |
| Cultural | Recreation, aesthetic and spiritual values |
| Supporting | Soil formation, nutrient cycling, primary production |

### Important Functions

**1. Soil Formation**
- Soil organisms contribute to decomposition.
- Microorganisms release nutrients.
- Plant roots and soil organisms influence soil structure.

**2. Prevention of Soil Erosion**
- Vegetation cover reduces the impact of rainfall.
- Roots help stabilize soil.
- Forests and grasslands can reduce surface runoff.

**3. Waste Recycling**
- Decomposers break down organic waste.
- Nutrients are returned to the environment.

**4. Pollination**
- Insects, birds and bats pollinate many flowering plants.
- Animal pollination contributes to the reproduction of numerous wild plants and crops.

### UPSC PYQ – 2011

Biodiversity forms the basis of human existence through:

1. Soil formation.
2. Prevention of soil erosion.
3. Recycling of waste.
4. Pollination of crops.

**Answer: All four.**

# 2.13 Threats to Biodiversity

The major direct drivers of biodiversity loss include land- and sea-use change, direct exploitation, climate change, pollution and invasive alien species.

## A. Habitat Loss and Fragmentation

### Habitat Loss

Destruction or conversion of natural habitats.

Examples:
- Deforestation.
- Conversion of wetlands.
- Expansion of urban settlements.
- Conversion of natural grasslands into agricultural land.

### Habitat Fragmentation

Division of continuous habitats into smaller, isolated patches.

**Consequences:**
- Reduced connectivity.
- Restricted movement of wildlife.
- Reduced gene flow.
- Greater vulnerability of small populations.
- Increased edge effects.

**Important:** Habitat fragmentation can harm biodiversity even when some habitat area remains.

---

## B. Overexploitation

Unsustainable extraction or use of biological resources.

Examples:
- Overfishing.
- Excessive hunting.
- Unsustainable timber extraction.
- Overharvesting of medicinal plants.

---

## C. Invasive Alien Species

May reduce native biodiversity through competition, predation, disease or habitat modification.

Examples:
- Lantana.
- Water hyacinth.
- Parthenium.

---

## D. Climate Change

Can cause:

- Changes in species distribution.
- Altered breeding and flowering periods.
- Coral bleaching.
- Habitat shifts.
- Increased extinction risks for vulnerable species.

---

## E. Pollution

Important examples:

- Agricultural pesticide contamination.
- Nutrient pollution.
- Plastic pollution.
- Industrial effluents.
- Heavy metal contamination.

---

## 2.14 Evil Quartet

The NCERT framework identifies four major causes of biodiversity loss, collectively called the **Evil Quartet**.

| Cause | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Habitat loss and fragmentation | Destruction and isolation of habitats |
| Overexploitation | Unsustainable use of biological resources |
| Alien species invasions | Harmful impacts of introduced species |
| Co-extinctions | Extinction of one species following the loss of another closely associated species |

### Co-extinction

Occurs when the extinction of one species leads to the extinction of another dependent species.

**Examples:**

- Extinction of a host may lead to extinction of a host-specific parasite.
- Extinction of a plant may threaten an obligate pollinator dependent on it.

**Important Distinction:**

The **Evil Quartet** is a traditional textbook classification. Modern global assessments, including IPBES, recognize five major direct drivers, explicitly including climate change and pollution.

# 2.15 Biodiversity Loss and Extinction

### Types of Extinction

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Local extinction (extirpation) | Species disappears from a particular area but survives elsewhere |
| Global extinction | Species disappears from the entire Earth |
| Functional extinction | Species becomes too rare to perform its former ecological role effectively |
| Co-extinction | Extinction resulting from the loss of a dependent or associated species |

### Mass Extinction

A mass extinction is a geologically rapid event involving exceptionally widespread biodiversity loss.

**Five major mass extinction events** are conventionally recognized in Earth's geological history.

| Event | Approximate Time |
|---|---|
| End-Ordovician | 444 million years ago |
| Late Devonian | 372–359 million years ago |
| End-Permian | 252 million years ago |
| End-Triassic | 201 million years ago |
| End-Cretaceous | 66 million years ago |

**Important:**
- The End-Permian extinction is generally considered the most severe of the five.
- The End-Cretaceous extinction is associated with the disappearance of non-avian dinosaurs.
- Present-day biodiversity loss is sometimes described as a potential sixth mass extinction, but the classification and comparison with geological events remain subjects of scientific discussion.

# 2.16 Biodiversity Measurement and Indices

### Species Richness

Total number of species in a given area.

### Species Evenness

Degree to which individuals are distributed evenly among different species.

### Shannon Diversity Index

\[
\boxed{H'=-\sum p_i\ln p_i}
\]

Where:

- \(p_i\) = Proportion of individuals belonging to species \(i\).

### Simpson Diversity Index

One common form is:

\[
\boxed{D=\sum p_i^2}
\]

**Important:** Simpson's index has multiple conventions, including \(D\), \(1-D\) and \(1/D\). Their numerical interpretations differ.

### Prelims Relevance

For UPSC Prelims, prioritize:
- Species richness.
- Species evenness.
- Alpha, beta and gamma diversity.
- Basic interpretation of diversity indices.

Detailed numerical calculations of Shannon and Simpson indices are generally lower priority.

# 2.19 Important UPSC Prelims Distinctions

| Concept A | Concept B | Key Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Genetic diversity | Species diversity | Variation within a species vs variation among species |
| Species richness | Species evenness | Number of species vs relative distribution of individuals |
| Alpha diversity | Beta diversity | Within-community diversity vs between-community turnover |
| Beta diversity | Gamma diversity | Species turnover vs regional diversity |
| Native species | Endemic species | Naturally occurring vs geographically restricted |
| Exotic species | Invasive alien species | Introduced species vs introduced species causing adverse impacts |
| Keystone species | Dominant species | Disproportionate ecological influence vs numerical or biomass dominance |
| Flagship species | Umbrella species | Conservation appeal vs broader habitat protection |
| Habitat loss | Habitat fragmentation | Destruction vs division of habitat |
| Local extinction | Global extinction | Disappearance from one area vs disappearance worldwide |
| Biodiversity hotspot | Megadiverse country | Threatened high-endemism region vs biodiversity-rich country |
| Resistance | Resilience | Withstanding disturbance vs recovering after disturbance |
