# **1. Biodiversity: Meaning and Basic Facts**

**Biodiversity (biological diversity)** refers to the variability among living organisms from all sources, including terrestrial, marine and other aquatic ecosystems, and the ecological complexes of which they are part.

According to the **Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD, 1992)**, biodiversity includes three levels:

1. **Genetic diversity:** Genetic variation within a species, including differences among individuals and populations.
2. **Species diversity:** Variety of species and their relative abundance within a geographical region.
3. **Ecosystem diversity:** Variety of ecosystems, habitats, biological communities and ecological processes within a geographical region.

**Examples:**

- **Genetic diversity:** Different varieties of rice and wheat; different breeds of cattle.
- **Species diversity:** Tigers, elephants, deer, birds and insects in a forest.
- **Ecosystem diversity:** Forests, grasslands, deserts, wetlands and coral reefs.

![Levels of Biodiversity](images/01_biodiversity_levels.png)

## **1.1 Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD)**

- **Adopted:** 1992, at the Rio Earth Summit (UNCED), Rio de Janeiro, Brazil.
- **Entered into force:** 29 December 1993.
- **Nature:** Legally binding international environmental treaty.

**Three objectives of the CBD:**

1. Conservation of biological diversity.
2. Sustainable use of its components.
3. Fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilisation of genetic resources.

# **2. Species Richness and Species Evenness**

# **2. Species Richness and Species Evenness**

**Species diversity** is commonly described using two components:

1. **Species richness:** Number of different species present in a particular area.
2. **Species evenness:** Degree to which individuals are distributed evenly among the species present.

**Example:**

Consider two forests, each containing 20 species:

- **Forest A:** Most individuals belong to a single species.
- **Forest B:** Individuals are distributed relatively evenly among all 20 species.

Both forests have the **same species richness**, but Forest B has **greater species evenness** and, consequently, higher species diversity under commonly used diversity indices.

![Species Richness Vs. Species Evenness](images/02_species_richness_vs_species_evenness.png)

**Note:**
- Two areas with identical species richness may have different species diversity because of differences in evenness.

# **3. Patterns of Biodiversity**

## **3.1 Latitudinal Gradient in Biodiversity**

- Species richness generally **increases from the poles towards the equator**.
- Tropical regions generally support more species than temperate and polar regions.
- Major reasons include relatively stable climatic conditions, greater solar energy availability and high biological productivity.
- Exceptions to this general pattern occur.

**Tropical moist forests**, particularly tropical rainforests, support exceptionally high species richness because of favourable temperatures, abundant rainfall and diverse habitats.

## **3.2 Altitudinal Gradient in Biodiversity**

- **General pattern:** Species richness generally decreases with increasing altitude.
- **Reasons:** Lower temperatures, shorter growing seasons and increasingly restrictive environmental conditions at higher elevations.
- **Exception:** Some species groups and mountain regions show maximum species richness at intermediate elevations rather than at the lowest elevations.

## **3.3 Species–Area Relationship**

**Alexander von Humboldt** observed that, within a region, species richness generally increases with increasing area explored.

The relationship is expressed as:

$$
S = CA^Z
$$

Where:

- **S:** Species richness (number of different species).
- **A:** Area of the region studied.
- **C:** Constant.
- **Z:** Species–area exponent, representing the slope of the line on a log–log graph.

# **4. Biodiversity Hotspots**

A **biodiversity hotspot** is a biogeographical region characterised by exceptionally high endemism of vascular plants and substantial loss of its original natural vegetation.

The concept was introduced by **Norman Myers in 1988**.

## **4.1 Biodiversity Hotspots: Characteristics and Criteria**

### **4.1.1 General Characteristics**

Biodiversity hotspots are generally associated with:

1. **Species richness:** High number of different species in a region.
2. **Endemism:** High concentration of species naturally restricted to a particular geographical region.
3. **Threat perception:** Significant threats to biodiversity, particularly habitat destruction and loss of natural vegetation.

These are broad characteristics associated with biodiversity hotspots, not necessarily the formal criteria for their designation.

### **4.1.2 Formal Criteria for Designation**

According to **Conservation International**, a region must satisfy both criteria:

| Criterion | Requirement |
|---|---|
| Endemism | At least **1,500 endemic vascular plant species**. |
| Habitat loss | At least **70% loss of original natural vegetation**. |

**Important UPSC Prelims Facts:**

- **36 biodiversity hotspots** are commonly recognised worldwide.
- Biodiversity hotspots are **not restricted to tropical regions**.

![](images/17_world_biodiversity_hotspots.png)

## **4.2 Biodiversity Hotspots in India**

India overlaps with **four global biodiversity hotspots**.

| Biodiversity Hotspot | Indian Geographical Regions |
|---|---|
| Himalaya | Parts of the Indian Himalayan region |
| Indo-Burma | Parts of Northeast India and the Andaman Islands |
| Western Ghats–Sri Lanka | Western Ghats |
| Sundaland | Nicobar Islands |

**Important:** The Andaman Islands are associated with the **Indo-Burma** hotspot, whereas the Nicobar Islands are associated with the **Sundaland** hotspot.

# **5. Biogeographical Classification of India**

## **5.1 Meaning**

A **biogeographical region** is a geographical area characterised by its climate, landforms, vegetation and distribution of plants and animals.

India has diverse biogeographical regions because of variations in climate, altitude, topography, soil and ecological conditions.

## **5.2 Biogeographical Classification of India**

The **Rodgers and Panwar classification (1988)**, associated with the **Wildlife Institute of India (WII)**, divides India into:

- **10 biogeographical zones**
- **26 biogeographical provinces**

A **biogeographical zone** is a broad ecological region, whereas a **biogeographical province** is a smaller subdivision within a zone.

## **5.3 Ten Biogeographical Zones of India**

| No. | Biogeographical Zone | Important Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Trans-Himalaya | Cold deserts and high-altitude plateaus; Ladakh and adjoining areas |
| 2 | Himalaya | Mountain ecosystems, temperate forests, subalpine and alpine vegetation |
| 3 | Indian Desert | Hot arid ecosystems, particularly the Thar Desert |
| 4 | Semi-Arid | Dry grasslands, scrublands and thorn forests |
| 5 | Western Ghats | Tropical forests, high rainfall and significant endemism |
| 6 | Deccan Peninsula | Extensive plateau region with tropical deciduous forests |
| 7 | Gangetic Plain | Alluvial plains, riverine ecosystems and wetlands |
| 8 | North-East India | Humid forests, high rainfall and rich species diversity |
| 9 | Islands | Andaman–Nicobar and Lakshadweep island ecosystems |
| 10 | Coasts | Coastal ecosystems, mangroves, estuaries and beaches |

![Biogeographical Zones of India](images/07_biogeographical_regios_of_india.png)

| Feature | Biogeographical Zones | Biodiversity Hotspots |

# **6. Native, Endemic, Exotic and Invasive Species**

Species are classified based on their natural geographical distribution, origin and ecological impact.

## **6.1 Classification of Species**

| Category | Definition | Examples in India |
|---|---|---|
| Native (Indigenous) species | Species that occur naturally in a geographical region without human introduction. | Asian elephant, Bengal tiger |
| Endemic species | Species naturally restricted to a particular geographical region and found nowhere else in the wild. | Nilgiri tahr, lion-tailed macaque |
| Exotic (Alien) species | Species introduced outside their natural geographical range through intentional or accidental human activities. | Eucalyptus, water hyacinth |
| Invasive alien species (IAS) | Alien species that establish and spread outside their natural range, threatening native biodiversity and ecosystems. | Lantana camara, Parthenium hysterophorus, water hyacinth |

## **6.2 Important Endemic Species of India**

![Endemic Species](images/09_endemic_species_in_india.png)

| Species | Region of Endemism |
|---|---|
| Lion-tailed macaque (*Macaca silenus*) | Western Ghats |

## **6.3 Important Invasive Alien Species in India**

![Important Invasive Species](images/10_invasive_alien_species_in_india.png)

| Species | Type | Important Impact |
|---|---|---|
| *Lantana camara* | Invasive shrub | Forms dense thickets and suppresses native vegetation |
| *Parthenium hysterophorus* (Congress grass) | Invasive herb | Displaces native plants; causes allergies |

## **6.4 Ecological Impacts of Invasive Alien Species**

1. **Competition:** Displace native species by competing for light, nutrients, food and space.
2. **Predation:** Introduced predators may reduce populations of native species.
3. **Disease transmission:** May introduce pathogens or parasites affecting native organisms.
4. **Habitat alteration:** Modify vegetation structure, soil properties, hydrology or other ecosystem processes.
5. **Biodiversity loss:** May cause local declines or extinctions of native species.

# **7. Biodiversity, Ecosystem Functioning and Services**

Biodiversity supports ecosystem functioning through ecological processes such as primary production, decomposition, nutrient cycling and pollination.

## **7.1 Ecosystem Stability, Resistance and Resilience**

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| Stability | Ability of an ecosystem to maintain relatively consistent structure and functioning over time, including its response to disturbances. |
| Resistance | Ability of an ecosystem to withstand disturbance without substantial change. |
| Resilience | Ability of an ecosystem to recover or reorganise after disturbance while retaining its essential structure and functions. |

![Ecosystem Stability](images/13_ecosystem_stability.png)

### **Relationship Between Biodiversity and Ecosystem Stability**

- Greater biodiversity can improve ecosystem productivity and the consistency of ecosystem functioning over time.
- **Functional diversity:** Presence of species performing different ecological roles.
- **Functional redundancy:** Presence of multiple species performing similar ecological functions, allowing some functions to continue even if certain species decline.
- Greater biodiversity can improve resistance or resilience to certain disturbances.
- However, **greater species richness does not always guarantee greater ecosystem stability**.

## **7.2 Ecosystem Functions and Ecosystem Services**

| Concept | Definition |
|---|---|
| Ecosystem functions | Natural ecological processes occurring within an ecosystem, such as primary production, decomposition and nutrient cycling. |
| Ecosystem services | Benefits that humans obtain from ecosystems, such as food, freshwater, pollination and climate regulation. |

### **7.2.1 Classification of Ecosystem Services**

![Ecosystem Services](images/12_ecosystem_services.png)

According to the **Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MEA, 2005)**, ecosystem services are classified into four categories.

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Provisioning | Products obtained from ecosystems | Food, freshwater, timber, medicinal resources |
| Regulating | Benefits from the regulation of ecosystem processes | Climate regulation, pollination, erosion control, water purification |
| Cultural | Non-material benefits obtained from ecosystems | Recreation, tourism, spiritual and aesthetic values |
| Supporting | Processes necessary for the production of other ecosystem services | Nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production |

### **7.2.2 Important Biodiversity-Supported Functions and Services**

| Function / Service | Role of Organisms |
|---|---|
| Soil formation | Soil organisms and decomposers contribute to organic matter breakdown and soil development. |
| Prevention of soil erosion | Vegetation cover reduces erosion; plant roots bind soil. |
| Recycling of waste | Bacteria, fungi and other decomposers break down organic waste and recycle nutrients. |
| Pollination | Insects, birds, bats and other pollinators facilitate reproduction in many flowering plants. |
| Climate regulation | Forests, wetlands and other ecosystems store carbon and influence climate. |
| Water purification | Wetlands and their organisms retain sediments and remove or transform certain pollutants. |

**Note:** The four-category classification follows the Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2005). Other frameworks may classify ecosystem services differently.

# **8. Threats to Biodiversity**

Biodiversity loss occurs when species, genetic diversity or ecosystems decline due to habitat destruction, unsustainable resource use and other environmental pressures.

## **8.1 Five Major Direct Drivers of Biodiversity Loss (IPBES)**

The **Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES)** identifies five major direct drivers of biodiversity loss.

| Driver | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Land- and sea-use change | Conversion or alteration of natural habitats | Deforestation, agricultural expansion, wetland reclamation |
| 2. Direct exploitation of organisms | Unsustainable harvesting or use of wild organisms | Overfishing, excessive hunting, unsustainable logging |
| 3. Climate change | Changes in climatic conditions affecting species and ecosystems | Coral bleaching, shifts in species distribution |
| 4. Pollution | Contamination of ecosystems by harmful substances or excess nutrients | Pesticides, plastics, industrial effluents, eutrophication |
| 5. Invasive alien species | Introduced species that spread and adversely affect native biodiversity | *Lantana camara*, water hyacinth, *Parthenium hysterophorus* |

**Important UPSC Prelims Facts:**

According to the **IPBES Global Assessment (2019)**:

- **Terrestrial and freshwater ecosystems:** Land-use change has been the largest direct driver of biodiversity loss.
- **Marine ecosystems:** Direct exploitation of organisms, particularly fishing, has been the largest direct driver.

## **8.2 Habitat Loss and Habitat Fragmentation**

Habitat loss and habitat fragmentation are related but distinct threats to biodiversity.

| Feature | Habitat Loss | Habitat Fragmentation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Destruction or conversion of natural habitats | Division of continuous habitats into smaller, separated patches |
| Major effect | Reduction in available habitat area and resources | Reduced connectivity, restricted movement and gene flow |
| Example | Conversion of forests into agricultural land | Highway dividing a continuous forest |

### **Ecological Consequences**

- **Reduced population size:** Smaller habitats may support fewer individuals.
- **Population isolation:** Fragmentation restricts movement between populations.
- **Reduced gene flow:** Isolation may increase inbreeding and reduce genetic diversity.
- **Edge effects:** Habitat boundaries experience altered light, temperature, wind and humidity, affecting species adapted to interior conditions.
- **Increased extinction risk:** Small, isolated populations are more vulnerable to disturbances and local extinction.

### **Shifting Cultivation**

Shifting cultivation involves clearing vegetation for cultivation, followed by a fallow period that allows vegetation to regenerate.

- Repeated clearing can contribute to habitat disturbance and biodiversity loss.
- Shortened fallow periods may prevent adequate recovery of vegetation and soil.
- Traditional shifting cultivation with sufficiently long fallow periods can allow forest regeneration.

**Important:** Its ecological impact depends on cultivation intensity and the duration of fallow periods.

## **8.3 NCERT's Evil Quartet**

**NCERT Biology** identifies four major causes of biodiversity loss, collectively known as the **Evil Quartet**.

| No. | Cause | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Habitat loss and fragmentation | Destruction and division of natural habitats | Tropical rainforest clearing |
| 2 | Overexploitation | Excessive use of biological resources beyond sustainable levels | Overfishing; historical overhunting of passenger pigeons |
| 3 | Alien species invasions | Spread of introduced species that threaten native biodiversity | Nile perch in Lake Victoria; water hyacinth in India |
| 4 | Co-extinctions | Extinction of one species causing the extinction of another dependent species | Extinction of a host causing the loss of its host-specific parasite |

### **Co-extinction**

**Co-extinction** occurs when the extinction of one species causes another species dependent on it to become extinct.

**Examples:**

- **Host–parasite:** Extinction of a host may cause extinction of its host-specific parasite.
- **Plant–pollinator:** Extinction of a plant may cause extinction of a pollinator that depends exclusively on it.

Co-extinction is particularly likely when the dependent species has no alternative host or resource.

# **9. Biodiversity Loss and Extinction**

**Extinction** is the permanent disappearance of a species from Earth. It is a natural evolutionary process, but human activities have greatly accelerated extinction rates.

## **9.1 Types of Extinction**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Local extinction (Extirpation) | Disappearance of a species from a particular region while surviving elsewhere. | Cheetah became locally extinct in India in 1952. |
| Global extinction | Permanent disappearance of a species from Earth. | Dodo, passenger pigeon |
| Functional extinction | A species survives, but its population is too small to perform its former ecological role effectively. | Severely depleted seed-dispersing animals may no longer provide effective seed dispersal. |

**Important Distinctions:**

- **Local extinction ≠ Global extinction:** A species may disappear from one region while surviving elsewhere.
- **Functional extinction:** Does not necessarily mean the last individual has died.
- **Extinct in the Wild (EW):** An IUCN Red List category indicating that a species survives only in captivity, cultivation or as a naturalised population outside its past range.
- **Extinct (EX):** An IUCN Red List category indicating that no living individuals of the species remain.
- **Cheetah in India:** Declared extinct in India in **1952**; reintroduction began in **2022** using African cheetahs.

## **9.2 Five Major Mass Extinctions**

A **mass extinction** is a geologically rapid event during which a large proportion of species across multiple groups become extinct worldwide.

Five major mass extinction events, collectively called the **Big Five**, are recognised in Earth's geological history.

| No. | Mass Extinction | Approximate Time | Important Facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | End-Ordovician | 444 million years ago | Major glaciation, sea-level changes and extensive marine extinctions |
| 2 | Late Devonian | 372–359 million years ago | Multiple extinction episodes; severe losses among marine organisms and reef communities |
| 3 | End-Permian | 252 million years ago | **Most severe mass extinction**; associated with massive volcanism and major climatic and oceanic changes |
| 4 | End-Triassic | 201 million years ago | Associated with extensive volcanism and climate change |
| 5 | End-Cretaceous | 66 million years ago | Chicxulub asteroid impact; extinction of non-avian dinosaurs |

## **9.3 Sixth Mass Extinction**

The **Sixth Mass Extinction** refers to the present-day, predominantly human-driven biodiversity crisis, characterised by elevated extinction rates and widespread population declines.

### **Major Causes**

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| Habitat loss and fragmentation | Deforestation, wetland destruction, habitat conversion |
| Overexploitation | Overhunting, overfishing, unsustainable harvesting |
| Invasive alien species | Predation, competition and displacement of native species |
| Pollution | Pesticides, industrial effluents, plastics |
| Climate change | Rising temperatures, habitat shifts, coral bleaching |

### **Important Distinction**

- The **Big Five** are established geological mass extinction events.
- The **Sixth Mass Extinction** refers to the current human-driven biodiversity crisis.
- A commonly used geological benchmark for mass extinction is the loss of approximately **75% of species** over a geologically short period.
- Whether the present crisis has already reached that threshold remains debated, although accelerated extinction rates and biodiversity decline are well established.

# **10. Megadiverse Countries**

**Megadiverse countries** are countries recognised for their exceptionally high biodiversity and significant levels of species endemism.

## **10.1 The 17 Megadiverse Countries**

The commonly recognised list of **17 megadiverse countries** is associated with **Conservation International**.

| Region | Countries |
|---|---|
| Asia | China, India, Indonesia, Malaysia, Philippines |
| Africa | Democratic Republic of the Congo, Madagascar, South Africa |
| North America | Mexico, United States |
| Central and South America | Brazil, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Venezuela |
| Oceania | Australia, Papua New Guinea |

**Total: 17 countries**

## **10.2 India as a Megadiverse Country**

India is one of the **17 megadiverse countries** because of its:

- High species richness.
- Significant species endemism.
- Diverse ecosystems, including Himalayan mountains, tropical forests, deserts, wetlands, mangroves and marine ecosystems.

India also overlaps with **four global biodiversity hotspots**:

1. Himalaya
2. Indo-Burma
3. Western Ghats–Sri Lanka
4. Sundaland (Nicobar Islands)

## **10.3 Megadiverse Countries vs Biodiversity Hotspots**

| Feature | Megadiverse Country | Biodiversity Hotspot |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Country with exceptionally high biodiversity and endemism | Region with high plant endemism and severe habitat loss |
| Geographical unit | Country | Biogeographical region |
| Identification | Overall biodiversity and endemism | Specific quantitative criteria involving plant endemism and habitat loss |
| Number | 17 countries | 36 hotspots worldwide |
| India | One megadiverse country | Parts of India overlap with four hotspots |

### **Important UPSC Prelims Facts**

- **Megadiverse countries:** 17 commonly recognised countries.
- **Biodiversity hotspots:** 36 commonly recognised regions worldwide.
- **India:** Both a megadiverse country and a country overlapping four biodiversity hotspots.
- **Key distinction:** Megadiverse status concerns a country's overall biodiversity and endemism; hotspot designation requires specific thresholds for endemic vascular plants and habitat loss.
- A biodiversity hotspot may extend across **multiple countries**.

# **11. UPSC Previous Year Questions**

**Q1. Consider the following statements about biodiversity:**

**1. Genetic diversity includes variation among populations of the same species.**  
**2. Ecosystem diversity refers only to the number of species in an ecosystem.**  
**3. Species diversity may reflect both richness and relative abundance.** 

**Which of the statements given above are correct?**

**A. 1 and 2 only**  
**B. 1 and 3 only**  
**C. 2 and 3 only**  
**D. 1, 2 and 3**

**Answer: B**

> **Explanation:** Statements 1 and 3 are correct. Ecosystem diversity concerns variation among ecosystems, habitats and ecological communities, not merely species counts.

**Q2. Which of the following is NOT one of the three objectives of the Convention on Biological Diversity?**

**A. Conservation of biological diversity**  
**B. Sustainable use of biodiversity components**  
**C. Fair sharing of benefits from genetic resources**  
**D. Mandatory prohibition of all commercial use of genetic resources**

**Answer: D**

> **Explanation:** The CBD seeks conservation, sustainable use and fair and equitable benefit-sharing; it does not ban all commercial use.

**Q3. Two grasslands contain 15 species each. In grassland X, one species accounts for 90% of individuals; in Y, individuals are nearly equally distributed. Which statement is correct?**

**A. X has greater species richness**  
**B. Y has greater species richness**  
**C. Y has greater species evenness**  
**D. Both necessarily have identical Shannon diversity**

**Answer: C**

> **Explanation:** Both have richness of 15 species. Y has greater evenness and therefore a higher Shannon index.

**B. 1 and 2 only**

**Q5. In the species–area relationship S = CA^Z, what does Z represent?**

**A. The species–area exponent**  
**B. Total species richness**

**Q6. A region contains 1,800 endemic vascular plant species but has lost only 60% of its original natural vegetation. Under the stated Conservation International criteria, which conclusion follows?**

**A. It qualifies because endemism alone is sufficient**  
**B. It qualifies because it exceeds 1,500 species**  
**C. It does not qualify because both thresholds must be met**  
**D. It qualifies if it lies within the tropics**

**Answer: C**

> **Explanation:** A hotspot must have at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species AND at least 70% loss of original natural vegetation.

**Q13. Consider the following statements regarding ecosystem stability:**

**2. Resilience refers to recovery or reorganisation after disturbance.**

**Q19. A host-specific parasite disappears after its sole host species becomes extinct. This is an example of:**

**A. Functional redundancy**  
**B. Co-extinction**  
**C. Habitat resistance**

**Answer: B**

> **Explanation:** Co-extinction occurs when the loss of one species causes the extinction of another that depends on it.

**Q20. Consider the following statements about extinction:**
