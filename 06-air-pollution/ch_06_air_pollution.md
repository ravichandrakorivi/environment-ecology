# 1. Atmosphere: Composition and Structure

## 1.1 Composition of the Atmosphere

Approximate composition of dry air near Earth's surface:

| Gas                  | Percentage by volume |
| -------------------- | -------------------- |
| Nitrogen (N₂)        | 78.08%               |
| Oxygen (O₂)          | 20.95%               |
| Argon (Ar)           | 0.93%                |
| Carbon dioxide (CO₂) | Approximately 0.04%  |
| Other gases          | Trace amounts        |

- **Water vapour:** Variable, generally ranging from nearly 0% to about 4% by volume.
- **Dry air:** Excludes water vapour.
- **CO₂:** Its atmospheric concentration is increasing mainly due to human activities.

**Decreasing order of abundance:** Nitrogen > Oxygen > Argon > Carbon dioxide.

**Important:** Nitrogen and oxygen are the two most abundant gases in Earth's atmosphere. Argon is the third most abundant gas in dry air.

![Composition of Dry Air](images/01_dry_air_composition.png)

## 1.2 Layers of the Atmosphere

The atmosphere is divided into five principal layers based on temperature variations with altitude.

| Layer        | Approximate altitude         | Temperature variation                     | Important characteristics                                          |
| ------------ | ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Troposphere  | Surface to 8–18 km           | Decreases with altitude                   | Weather phenomena; most atmospheric water vapour and air pollution |
| Stratosphere | Above troposphere to \~50 km | Increases with altitude                   | Contains the ozone layer; ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation      |
| Mesosphere   | \~50–85 km                   | Decreases with altitude                   | Most meteors burn up                                               |
| Thermosphere | \~85–500/600 km              | Increases with altitude                   | Very thin air; absorbs high-energy solar radiation                 |
| Exosphere    | Above \~500–600 km           | No simple, well-defined temperature trend | Outermost atmospheric region; extremely low particle density       |

Layer boundaries are approximate and vary with latitude, season and atmospheric conditions.

### 1.3 Why does temperature vary?

- **Troposphere:** Heated mainly from Earth's surface; temperature generally decreases with altitude.
- **Stratosphere:** Ozone absorbs ultraviolet radiation, causing temperature to increase with altitude.
- **Mesosphere:** Temperature generally decreases with altitude because radiative heating is relatively weak.
- **Thermosphere:** Absorption of extreme ultraviolet and X-ray radiation causes temperature to increase with altitude.
- **Exosphere:** Extremely low particle density makes the conventional concept of temperature less useful; no simple vertical trend applies.

### 1.4 Important distinctions

- **Order from Earth's surface:** Troposphere → Stratosphere → Mesosphere → Thermosphere → Exosphere.
- **Temperature trend:** Decreases → Increases → Decreases → Increases (across the first four layers).
- **Ionosphere:** A region containing ionised particles, extending mainly through the upper mesosphere and thermosphere and into higher altitudes. It is not a separate temperature-defined atmospheric layer.
- **Ozone layer:** Concentrated mainly in the stratosphere, approximately 15–35 km above Earth's surface.
- **Troposphere:** Contains most atmospheric mass and water vapour; most weather phenomena occur here.

# 2. Air Pollution: Meaning and Classification

## 2.1 Definition

Air pollution is the presence of harmful substances (air pollutants) in the atmosphere at concentrations that adversely affect human health, plants, animals, materials or the environment.

**Note:** Air pollutants may originate from natural sources (such as volcanic eruptions and dust storms) or human activities (such as vehicles, industries and fuel combustion).

![Air Pollution](images/03_air_pollution.png)

## 2.2 Classification Based on Formation

Air pollutants are classified into primary pollutants and secondary pollutants based on whether they are directly emitted or formed through chemical reactions in the atmosphere.

| Feature        | Primary pollutants                                                                            | Secondary pollutants                                                                                              |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Definition     | Emitted directly into the atmosphere from a source                                            | Formed through chemical reactions in the atmosphere                                                               |
| Major examples | CO, SO₂, NOₓ, NH₃, VOCs, soot, dust, fly ash, lead and other directly emitted metal particles | Tropospheric O₃, peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN), secondary sulphate and nitrate particles, secondary organic aerosols |

![Classification of Pollutants based on Formation](images/04_air_pollution_classification.png)

![](images/05_products_of_nox_voc.png)

### 2.2.1 Important Distinctions

- **Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂):** Can be emitted directly (primary) or formed through oxidation of nitric oxide (NO) in the atmosphere (secondary).
- **Tropospheric ozone (O₃):** A secondary air pollutant formed through photochemical reactions involving nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the presence of sunlight.
- **VOCs:** Many are directly emitted primary pollutants and also act as precursors for secondary pollutants such as ozone and PAN.

### 2.2.2 Particulate matter (PM):

Tiny solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in air. It can be:
- **Primary PM:** Directly emitted into the atmosphere, such as dust, soot and fly ash.
- **Secondary PM:** Formed in the atmosphere through chemical reactions involving gases such as SO₂, NOₓ, NH₃ and VOCs.

**Important clarification:** PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀ are classifications based on particle size, not formation. Both may contain primary and secondary particles.

| Classification            | Basis                             |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| Primary PM / Secondary PM | How particles originate           |
| PM₂.₅ / PM₁₀              | Aerodynamic diameter of particles |

**Note:** PM₂.₅ refers to particles with aerodynamic diameter ≤ 2.5 µm, while PM₁₀ refers to particles with aerodynamic diameter ≤ 10 µm.

![PM](images/06_pm.png)

### 2.2.3 Important Terms

| Term                              | Meaning                                                                                                              |
| --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| NOₓ (Nitrogen oxides)             | Collective term referring mainly to nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) in air pollution studies            |
| VOCs (Volatile Organic Compounds) | Organic compounds that readily evaporate into the air; many participate in reactions forming secondary pollutants    |
| PAN (Peroxyacetyl Nitrate)        | Secondary pollutant formed through photochemical reactions involving NOₓ and VOCs; a component of photochemical smog |
| Secondary sulphate particles      | Formed through atmospheric oxidation of SO₂ and subsequent particle formation                                        |
| Secondary nitrate particles       | Formed through atmospheric reactions involving NOₓ, often producing particulate ammonium nitrate                     |
| Secondary organic aerosols (SOA)  | Particles formed when atmospheric oxidation products of VOCs condense into the particulate phase                     |

**Note**:
- NO₂ and PM can be both primary and secondary.
- Tropospheric ozone and PAN are secondary pollutants.
- SO₂, NOₓ, NH₃ and VOCs are important precursors of secondary particulate matter or other secondary pollutants.

## 2.3 Classification Based on Physical State

Air pollutants are also classified according to their physical state.

| Type                   | Definition                                                  | Examples                                              |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Gaseous pollutants     | Harmful substances present as gases                         | CO, SO₂, NOₓ, NH₃, O₃, VOCs                           |
| Particulate pollutants | Harmful solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in air | Dust, soot, smoke particles, fly ash, liquid aerosols |

Important: These classifications are independent.

- Primary/secondary: Describes how a pollutant is emitted or formed.
- Gaseous/particulate: Describes its physical state.

For example, CO is a primary gaseous pollutant, whereas tropospheric ozone is a secondary gaseous pollutant.

![](images/07_physical_state_classification.png)

## 2.4 Sources of Air Pollution

Air pollutants originate from both natural processes and human activities.

| Natural sources               | Human-made sources                     |
| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------- |
| Volcanic eruptions            | Thermal power plants                   |
| Dust storms                   | Motor vehicles                         |
| Naturally occurring wildfires | Industries and refineries              |
| Sea spray                     | Construction and road dust             |
| Pollen                        | Agricultural residue and waste burning |

**Note:** Wildfires can also be caused by human activities. Their classification depends on the cause of ignition.

## 2.5 Fog as a Colloidal System

### 2.5.1 What is a colloid?

A colloid is a mixture in which tiny particles or droplets of one substance remain dispersed (spread throughout) another substance.

A colloid has two components:

1. **Dispersed phase:** The particles or droplets that are spread throughout the mixture.
2. **Dispersion medium:** The substance in which those particles or droplets are suspended.

![](images/09_colloid.png)

### 2.5.2 Why is fog a colloid?

![Fog](images/01_fog.jpeg)

Fog forms when water vapour in the air condenses into tiny liquid water droplets. These droplets remain suspended in the surrounding air.

Therefore:

- **Dispersed phase:** Liquid water droplets.
- **Dispersion medium:** Air (gas).
- **Type of colloid:** Liquid in gas.

**Note**: Water vapour is a gas, but the tiny water droplets forming fog are liquid.

**Other examples of colloids:**

| Example   | What is dispersed?     | In what medium?    | Classification   |
| --------- | ---------------------- | ------------------ | ---------------- |
| Fog       | Liquid water droplets  | Air (gas)          | Liquid in gas    |
| Smoke     | Mainly solid particles | Air (gas)          | Solid in gas     |
| Milk      | Liquid fat droplets    | Water-based liquid | Liquid in liquid |
| Soap foam | Gas bubbles            | Liquid             | Gas in liquid    |

**Fog vs mist:** Both contain liquid water droplets suspended in air. Fog generally reduces horizontal visibility to less than 1 km, whereas mist permits greater visibility.

# 3. Major Air Pollutants

## 3.1 Particulate Matter (PM)

Particulate matter (PM) consists of tiny solid particles or liquid droplets suspended in air.

### 3.1.1 Classification Based on Particle Size

| Category                    | Aerodynamic diameter | Penetration into respiratory system                                                                  |
| --------------------------- | -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| PM₁₀                        | ≤ 10 µm              | Inhalable; particles may deposit in the respiratory tract (nose, throat and lungs)                                       |
| PM₂.₅                       | ≤ 2.5 µm             | Inhalable; can penetrate deep into the lungs, including the alveoli                                  |
| PM₀.₁ (Ultrafine particles) | ≤ 0.1 µm             | Inhalable; can reach the alveoli, and some particles or their constituents may enter the bloodstream |

**Note:**
- All three categories are inhalable.
- PM₂.₅ is a subset of PM₁₀, and PM₀.₁ is a subset of PM₂.₅.

### 3.1.2 Classification Based on Formation

| Type         | Definition                                                                                 | Examples                                                          |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Primary PM   | Particles emitted directly into the atmosphere                                             | Dust, soot, fly ash, smoke particles                              |
| Secondary PM | Particles formed in the atmosphere through chemical reactions involving gaseous precursors | Sulphate particles, nitrate particles, secondary organic aerosols |

**Major gaseous precursors:** SO₂, NOₓ, NH₃ and VOCs.

### 3.1.3 Sources of Particulate Matter

| Natural sources    | Human-made sources                  |
| ------------------ | ----------------------------------- |
| Dust storms        | Vehicle exhaust and tyre/brake wear |
| Volcanic eruptions | Coal combustion                     |
| Wildfires          | Biomass and waste burning           |
| Sea spray          | Construction and road dust          |
| Wind erosion       | Industrial activities               |

**Note:** Wildfires may also be human-caused.

### 3.1.4 Effects of Particulate Matter

| Area                  | Effects                                                                                     |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Respiratory system    | Irritation, aggravation of asthma and other respiratory diseases                            |
| Cardiovascular system | Increased risk of heart and blood vessel diseases, particularly with fine-particle exposure |
| Visibility            | Scattering and absorption of light, causing haze                                            |
| Vegetation            | Deposition on leaves, potentially affecting photosynthesis                                  |
| Climate               | Scattering or absorption of solar radiation and changes in cloud properties                 |

**Note**: PM₁₀ and PM₂.₅ are included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

## 3.2 Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂)

![](images/10_sulphur_dioxide.png)

Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) is a colourless, pungent gas and a primary air pollutant released mainly during the combustion of sulphur-containing fuels.

### 3.2.1 Sources

- **Human-made:** Burning of coal and sulphur-containing petroleum fuels, metal smelting and certain industrial processes.
- **Natural:** Volcanic eruptions.

**Note:** Coal combustion produces sulphur dioxide (SO₂) because coal naturally contains sulphur as an impurity.

### 3.2.2 Effects

- **Human health:** Irritates the respiratory tract and aggravates asthma and other respiratory conditions.
- **Acid rain:** SO₂ undergoes atmospheric oxidation and subsequent reactions to form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), contributing to acid deposition.
- **Secondary particulate matter:** Forms sulphate aerosols, which contribute to PM₂.₅.
- **Environment:** Acid deposition damages vegetation, soils, aquatic ecosystems and buildings.

### 3.2.3 Control Measures

- **Flue Gas Desulphurisation (FGD):** Removes SO₂ from industrial and power-plant exhaust gases, commonly using limestone or lime.
- **Use of low-sulphur fuels:** Reduces SO₂ emissions by lowering the amount of sulphur burned.

### 3.2.4 Important Prelims Facts

- SO₂ is a primary gaseous pollutant.
- SO₂ is a precursor of secondary sulphate aerosols and acid rain.
- Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) and nitric acid (HNO₃) are major contributors to acid rain.
- FGD is primarily used to control SO₂ emissions from flue gases.
- SO₂ is one of the pollutants monitored under India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

## 3.3 Nitrogen Oxides (NOₓ)

![](images/11_nitrogen_oxides.png)

Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) are gaseous air pollutants consisting mainly of nitric oxide (NO) and nitrogen dioxide (NO₂).

NOₓ is produced largely during high-temperature combustion, when nitrogen and oxygen react.

### 3.3.1 Sources

- **Human-made:** Vehicle engines, thermal power plants and industrial combustion.
- **Natural:** Lightning and microbial processes in soils.

### 3.3.2 Formation

At high temperatures, nitrogen and oxygen in air can react to form nitric oxide (NO).

$$
N_2 + O_2 \xrightarrow{\text{High temperature}} 2NO
$$

NO can subsequently undergo oxidation in the atmosphere to form NO₂.

$$
2NO + O_2 \rightarrow 2NO_2
$$

The second equation represents the overall oxidation reaction; atmospheric conversion also occurs through reactions involving ozone and other oxidants.

### 3.3.3 Effects

- **Human health:** NO₂ irritates the respiratory system and aggravates asthma.
- **Ground-level ozone:** NOₓ reacts with VOCs in sunlight-driven atmospheric chemistry, contributing to tropospheric ozone (O₃) formation.
- **Acid rain:** Atmospheric reactions involving NOₓ produce nitric acid (HNO₃), contributing to acid deposition.
- **Secondary PM₂.₅:** NOₓ contributes to the formation of secondary nitrate aerosols.

### 3.3.4 Control Measures

- **Low-NOₓ burners:** Reduce NOₓ formation during combustion.
- **Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR):** Uses a reducing agent, commonly ammonia (NH₃), and a catalyst to convert NOₓ mainly into nitrogen (N₂) and water (H₂O).
- **Catalytic converters:** Reduce NOₓ emissions from vehicle exhaust.

### 3.3.5 Important Prelims Facts

- **NO (nitric oxide):** Primarily emitted directly during combustion.
- **NO₂ (nitrogen dioxide):** Can be a primary pollutant (directly emitted) or a secondary pollutant (formed through atmospheric oxidation of NO).
- **NOₓ:** Important precursor of ground-level ozone, nitric acid and secondary nitrate aerosols.
- NO₂ is included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).
- Nitrous oxide (N₂O) is a greenhouse gas, chemically distinct from NO and NO₂, and is not normally included in NOₓ in air-pollution discussions.

## 3.4 Carbon Monoxide (CO)

![Carbon Monoxide](images/12_carbon_monoxide.png)

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a colourless, odourless and highly toxic gas produced mainly by incomplete combustion of carbon-containing fuels.

### 3.4.1 Formation

- **Complete combustion:** When sufficient oxygen is available, carbon burns to form carbon dioxide (CO₂).

$$
C + O_2 \rightarrow CO_2
$$

- **Incomplete combustion:** When oxygen is insufficient, carbon monoxide (CO) may form.

$$
2C + O_2 \rightarrow 2CO
$$

### 3.4.2 Sources

- **Vehicle exhaust:** Particularly from engines operating under fuel-rich or inefficient combustion conditions.
- **Biomass burning:** Burning of wood, crop residues and other organic materials.
- **Domestic appliances:** Faulty or poorly ventilated gas stoves, heaters and other fuel-burning appliances.
- **Industrial processes:** Incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and certain metallurgical operations.
- **Natural sources:** Wildfires.

### 3.4.3 Effects on Human Health

- CO combines with haemoglobin (Hb) in blood to form carboxyhaemoglobin (COHb).
- This reduces the blood's capacity to transport oxygen to body tissues.
- Exposure can cause headache, dizziness, nausea and, at high concentrations, unconsciousness or death.

### 3.4.4 Control Measures

- **Catalytic converters:** Oxidise CO in vehicle exhaust to CO₂.
- **Efficient combustion:** Ensuring adequate oxygen supply and proper maintenance of combustion equipment.
- **Proper ventilation:** Prevents dangerous CO accumulation in enclosed spaces.

### 3.4.5 Important Prelims Facts

- CO is a primary gaseous air pollutant.
- CO is produced mainly during incomplete combustion, whereas complete combustion of carbon produces CO₂.
- CO forms carboxyhaemoglobin, reducing oxygen transport in blood.
- CO is not generally classified as a direct greenhouse gas, but it indirectly influences climate by affecting atmospheric chemistry, including methane and tropospheric ozone.
- CO₂ is a major greenhouse gas, whereas CO is primarily known for its toxicity.
- CO is included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

## 3.5 Ground-level Ozone (O₃)

![Tropospheric Ozone](images/13_ground_level_ozone.png)

Ground-level ozone (O₃) is a secondary gaseous air pollutant formed in the troposphere through sunlight-driven chemical reactions involving nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs).

Unlike stratospheric ozone, ground-level ozone is harmful to human health and vegetation.

### 3.5.1 Formation

- **Precursors:** Nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs).
- **Sunlight:** Drives photochemical reactions that lead to ozone formation.
- **Major precursor sources:** Vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, power plants and solvent evaporation.

**Important reactions:**

1. Sunlight breaks down nitrogen dioxide (NO₂):

$$
NO_2 + h\nu \rightarrow NO + O
$$

2. The released oxygen atom combines with molecular oxygen:

$$
O + O_2 + M \rightarrow O_3 + M
$$

Here, $M$ represents another atmospheric molecule (usually N₂ or O₂) that carries away excess energy.

**Role of VOCs:** VOCs help convert NO back into NO₂ without consuming ozone, allowing ozone to accumulate under suitable atmospheric conditions.

### 3.5.2 Effects

- **Human health:** Irritates the respiratory tract, reduces lung function and aggravates asthma.
- **Vegetation:** Damages plant tissues, reduces photosynthesis and can decrease crop yields.
- **Photochemical smog:** An important component of photochemical smog, particularly in polluted urban areas.
- **Climate change:** Tropospheric ozone acts as a greenhouse gas, contributing to global warming.

### 3.5.3 Control Measures

- **Reduce NOₓ emissions:** Catalytic converters, low-NOₓ burners and Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR).
- **Reduce VOC emissions:** Control solvent evaporation, fuel vapours and industrial emissions.

**Note:** Ground-level ozone is not emitted directly in significant quantities by vehicles or industries. It forms through atmospheric chemical reactions involving precursor pollutants.

### 3.5.4 Stratospheric Ozone vs Tropospheric Ozone

| Feature               | Stratospheric ozone                                           | Tropospheric ozone                                                  |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Location              | Stratosphere, mainly 15–35 km altitude                        | Troposphere, from Earth's surface to the tropopause                 |
| Role                  | Protects life by absorbing harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation | Harmful air pollutant; also a greenhouse gas                        |
| Formation             | UV radiation splits O₂, enabling O₃ formation                 | Photochemical reactions involving NOₓ and VOCs                      |
| Environmental concern | Ozone-layer depletion                                         | Photochemical smog, health effects, crop damage and climate warming |

### 3.5.5 Important Prelims Facts

- Ground-level ozone is a secondary pollutant, not a primary pollutant.
- NOₓ and VOCs are major precursors of tropospheric ozone.
- Sunlight is essential for the main photochemical formation pathway.
- Ozone is an important constituent of photochemical smog.
- Tropospheric ozone is both an air pollutant and a greenhouse gas.
- Stratospheric ozone protects against harmful UV radiation.
- O₃ is included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

**Note:** The ozone molecule (O₃) is chemically identical in both atmospheric layers. Its environmental role differs mainly because of its location.

## 3.6 Ammonia (NH₃)

![Ammonia](images/14_ammonia.png)

Ammonia (NH₃) is a colourless gas with a strong, pungent smell. It is a primary gaseous air pollutant and an important precursor of secondary particulate matter (PM₂.₅).

### 3.6.1 Sources

- **Livestock farming:** Ammonia released from animal manure and urine.
- **Poultry farming:** Decomposition of nitrogen-containing compounds in poultry litter and waste.
- **Nitrogen fertilisers:** Ammonia volatilisation from fertilisers, particularly urea.
- **Industrial activities:** Ammonia production, fertiliser manufacturing and certain chemical industries.
- **Natural sources:** Soils, oceans and decomposition of organic matter.

Agriculture is the dominant source of atmospheric ammonia emissions globally.

### 3.6.2 Formation of Secondary Particulate Matter

Ammonia reacts with atmospheric acids, particularly sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) and nitric acid (HNO₃), to form fine ammonium-containing particles.

1. Formation of ammonium sulphate:

$$
2NH_3 + H_2SO_4 \rightarrow (NH_4)\_2SO_4
$$

2. Formation of ammonium nitrate:

$$
NH_3 + HNO_3 \rightleftharpoons NH_4NO_3
$$

Both ammonium sulphate and particulate ammonium nitrate contribute to secondary PM₂.₅.

### 3.6.3 Effects

- **Human health:** High concentrations of NH₃ irritate the eyes and respiratory tract.
- **Air pollution:** Contributes indirectly to PM₂.₅ formation, which is associated with respiratory and cardiovascular diseases.
- **Ecosystems:** Atmospheric deposition of reactive nitrogen can contribute to eutrophication and soil acidification.

### 3.6.4 Control Measures

- **Improved manure management:** Covered storage and rapid incorporation of manure into soil.
- **Efficient fertiliser application:** Appropriate fertiliser doses, timing and placement to reduce ammonia volatilisation.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Measures to prevent ammonia leakage and capture emissions.

### 3.6.5 Important Prelims Facts

- NH₃ is a primary gaseous pollutant but contributes to the formation of secondary PM₂.₅.
- Agriculture, particularly livestock waste and nitrogen fertilisers, is the major source.
- NH₃ reacts with atmospheric acids to form ammonium sulphate and ammonium nitrate.
- NH₃ is alkaline (basic), unlike acidic gases such as SO₂ and NO₂.
- NH₃ is not among the eight pollutants used to calculate India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

## 3.7 Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs)

![VOCs](images/15_voc.png)

Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs) are carbon-containing compounds that readily evaporate into the air at ordinary temperatures.

Many VOCs participate in atmospheric chemical reactions that contribute to ground-level ozone (O₃) and secondary organic aerosols (SOA).

### 3.7.1 Sources

- **Fuel evaporation:** Petrol storage, refuelling and fuel handling.
- **Solvents and paints:** Paints, thinners, adhesives and cleaning products.
- **Industrial activities:* Chemical manufacturing, petroleum refining and petrochemical industries.
- **Combustion:** Vehicle exhaust, biomass burning and other fuel-burning processes.
- **Natural sources:** Vegetation, particularly trees that release VOCs such as isoprene.

### 3.7.2 Important Examples

| VOC             | Major sources                                 | Important fact                                              |
| --------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Benzene (C₆H₆)  | Petrol, vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions | Known human carcinogen                                      |
| Toluene (C₇H₈)  | Paints, solvents, petrol                      | Can affect the nervous system at sufficiently high exposure |
| Isoprene (C₅H₈) | Primarily vegetation                          | Important naturally emitted VOC                             |

### 3.7.2 Effects

- **Ground-level ozone formation:** VOCs participate in sunlight-driven reactions involving NOₓ, contributing to tropospheric ozone formation.
- **Secondary particulate matter:** Atmospheric oxidation of certain VOCs produces secondary organic aerosols (SOA), which contribute to PM₂.₅.
- **Human health:** Effects vary by compound. Some VOCs cause irritation or neurological effects, while certain VOCs, such as benzene, are carcinogenic.
- **Photochemical smog:** VOCs contribute to smog formation through atmospheric chemical reactions.

### 3.7.3 Control Measures

- **Vapour recovery systems:*** Capture fuel vapours during storage and refuelling.
- **Low-VOC products:** Use paints, solvents and adhesives with lower VOC emissions.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Capture or treat VOC emissions from industrial processes.

### 3.7.4 Important Prelims Facts

- VOCs may originate from human-made and natural sources.
- Benzene and toluene are aromatic hydrocarbons; isoprene is a naturally emitted hydrocarbon.
- NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight contribute to ground-level ozone formation.
- VOCs can form secondary organic aerosols (SOA) through atmospheric oxidation.
- Benzene is carcinogenic and is one of the eight pollutants included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).
- VOCs are not all equally toxic, and not every VOC forms secondary organic aerosols to the same extent.

**Note:** Carbon monoxide (CO) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) are carbon-containing gases but are not classified as VOCs.

## 3.8 Benzene (C₆H₆)

![Benzene](images/16_benzene.png)

Benzene is a colourless, highly volatile, flammable liquid with a characteristic aromatic odour. It is an aromatic hydrocarbon, a Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) and a known human carcinogen.

### 3.8.1 Sources

- **Vehicle exhaust:** Released during combustion of petrol and other fuels.
- **Petroleum products:** Petrol contains benzene, which can enter the atmosphere through fuel evaporation, storage and refuelling.
- **Tobacco smoke:** An important source of indoor benzene exposure.
- **Biomass burning:** Burning of wood, crop residues and other organic materials.
- **Industrial activities:** Petroleum refining, petrochemical industries and chemical manufacturing.
- **Household products:** Certain paints, adhesives, solvents and other products containing benzene or benzene impurities.

### 3.8.2 Effects on Human Health

- **Carcinogenicity:** Benzene is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).
- **Leukaemia:** Long-term benzene exposure is strongly associated with acute myeloid leukaemia (AML).
- **Bone marrow toxicity:** Can damage bone marrow and reduce blood cell production.
- **Short-term exposure:** High concentrations may cause dizziness, headache and drowsiness.

**Note:** Leukemia is a group of blood cancers that start in the bone marrow, where genetic mutations cause blood-forming tissue to produce abnormal, non-functional white blood cells (often called blast cells). These abnormal cells multiply uncontrollably and crowd out healthy red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets.

### 3.8.3 Environmental Effects

- **Ground-level ozone:** Benzene participates in atmospheric photochemical reactions contributing to ozone formation.
- **Secondary organic aerosols:** Atmospheric oxidation of benzene can contribute to secondary organic particulate matter.

### 3.8.4 Control Measures

- **Vehicle emission controls:** Catalytic converters and improved fuel combustion.
- **Fuel vapour recovery:** Reduces benzene emissions during petrol storage and refuelling.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Capture and treatment of benzene-containing vapours.
- **Product substitution:** Use of benzene-free or low-benzene products.

### 3.8.5 Important Prelims Facts

- **Chemical formula:** C₆H₆.
- **Classification:** Aromatic hydrocarbon and VOC.
- **Major health concern:** Carcinogenicity, particularly leukaemia.
- **IARC classification:** Group 1 (carcinogenic to humans).
- **India's National AQI:** Benzene is not included among the eight pollutants used to calculate the AQI.
- **National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS):** Benzene is included, with an annual standard of 5 µg/m³.

**Note:** Benzene emissions from paints, varnishes, adhesives and furnishings depend on their chemical composition. Not all such products release benzene, even though they may emit other VOCs.

## 3.9 Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs)

![PAHs](images/17_pac.png)

Polycyclic Aromatic Hydrocarbons (PAHs) are organic compounds containing two or more fused aromatic rings. They are commonly produced during the incomplete combustion of carbon-containing materials.

### 3.9.1 Sources

- **Vehicle exhaust:** Particularly from incomplete combustion of petrol and diesel.
- **Coal combustion:** Coal-fired power plants, industries and domestic coal burning.
- **Biomass burning:** Burning of wood, crop residues and other organic materials.
- **Tobacco smoke:** Contains several PAHs.
- **Industrial processes:** Coke production, metal processing and petroleum-related industries.
- **Natural sources:** Wildfires and volcanic activity.

### 3.9.2 Important Examples

| PAH            | Important fact                                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| Benzo[a]pyrene | Known human carcinogen                           |
| Naphthalene    | Simplest PAH, containing two fused benzene rings |
| Anthracene     | Contains three linearly fused benzene rings      |
| Phenanthrene   | Contains three angularly fused benzene rings     |

### 3.9.3 Effects

- **Cancer:** Certain PAHs, particularly benzo[a]pyrene, can damage DNA and increase cancer risk.
- **Respiratory health:** Exposure to PAH-containing combustion emissions is associated with adverse respiratory effects.
- **Environmental contamination:** PAHs can accumulate in soils and sediments; some persist in the environment.

### 3.9.4 Control Measures

- **Efficient combustion:*** Reduces PAH formation during fuel burning.
- **Vehicle emission controls:** Catalytic converters and particulate filters help reduce emissions.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Capture and treatment of PAH-containing emissions.
- **Avoid open burning:** Reduces emissions from waste and biomass combustion.

### 3.9.5 Important Prelims Facts

- PAHs contain two or more fused aromatic rings.
- They are formed mainly during incomplete combustion.
- PAHs may occur in the gas phase or attached to airborne particulate matter, depending on the compound and atmospheric conditions.
- Benzo[a]pyrene is a known human carcinogen.
- Not all PAHs are carcinogenic.
- PAHs in automobile exhaust can increase cancer risk.
- PAHs are a group of compounds, whereas benzene (C₆H₆) contains only one aromatic ring and is not classified as a PAH.

## 3.10 Lead (Pb)

![Lead](images/18_lead.png)

Lead (Pb) is a toxic heavy metal that can contaminate air in the form of lead-containing particulate matter. It is a primary air pollutant.

### 3.10.1 Sources

- **Metal smelting and processing:** Emissions from lead smelters, foundries and metal-processing industries.
- **Lead-acid battery recycling:** Release of lead-containing dust and fumes, particularly during unsafe recycling.
- **Industrial activities:** Manufacturing and processing of lead-containing materials.
- **Leaded petrol:** Historically a major source of airborne lead from vehicle exhaust.
- **Other sources:** Dust from contaminated soil, certain mining operations and waste burning.

**Note:** India phased out leaded petrol nationwide in 2000, substantially reducing traffic-related lead emissions.

### 3.10.2 Effects on Human Health

- **Nervous system:** Damages the brain and nervous system, particularly in children.
- **Children:** Can impair brain development, reduce IQ and cause learning and behavioural difficulties.
- **Blood:** Interferes with haemoglobin synthesis, potentially causing anaemia.
- **Kidneys:** Prolonged exposure can damage kidney function.
- **Pregnancy:** Lead exposure can adversely affect fetal development.

Lead is a cumulative toxicant, meaning it can accumulate in the body, particularly in bones and teeth.

**Note:** Anemia is a medical condition in which your body lacks enough healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin to carry adequate oxygen to your tissues and organs.

### 3.10.3 Environmental Effects

- **Soil contamination:** Lead-containing particles settle on soil and may persist for long periods.
- **Water contamination:** Lead deposited on land can enter water bodies through runoff.
- **Food chains: Plants and animals may take up lead from contaminated environments.**

### 3.10.4 Control Measures

- **Unleaded petrol:** Eliminates lead emissions arising from lead additives in petrol.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Filters and dust-collection systems reduce lead-containing particulate emissions.
- **Safe battery recycling:** Proper collection, handling and recycling of lead-acid batteries.
- **Dust control:** Prevents the spread of lead-contaminated dust from industrial and mining sites.

### 3.10.5 Important Prelims Facts

- Lead is a toxic heavy metal, not a gaseous pollutant under ordinary atmospheric conditions.
- Airborne lead is generally associated with particulate matter.
- Children are particularly vulnerable to lead-induced neurological damage.
- Lead exposure can cause anaemia, kidney damage and developmental disorders.
- No level of lead exposure is known to be completely safe.
- India phased out leaded petrol nationwide in 2000.
- Lead (Pb) is one of the eight pollutants included in India's National Air Quality Index (AQI).

## 3.11 Magnetite Particles (Fe₃O₄)

![Magnetite Particles](images/19_magnetite_particles.png)

Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) is a naturally occurring magnetic iron oxide that can also be released into the atmosphere as fine particles or nanoparticles through human activities.

### 3.11.1 Sources

- **Vehicle brakes:** Friction and wear of brake components release iron-rich particles, including magnetite.
- **Vehicle engines and exhaust:** High-temperature combustion and engine-related processes can generate magnetite-containing particles.
- **Thermal power plants:** Coal combustion and other high-temperature processes can release iron oxide particles.
- **Industrial activities:** Metal processing, smelting and other high-temperature industrial operations.
- **Natural sources:** Soil dust, rock weathering and certain geological processes.

### 3.11.2 Effects on Human Health

- **Respiratory exposure:** Airborne magnetite particles can enter the respiratory system through inhalation.
- **Brain exposure:** Some inhaled magnetite nanoparticles may reach the brain through pathways such as the olfactory nerve.
- **Oxidative stress:** Magnetite nanoparticles may promote the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), potentially damaging cells.
- **Neurodegenerative diseases:** Their possible contribution to diseases such as Alzheimer's and Parkinson's is being investigated. A direct causal relationship has not been established.

### 3.11.3 Environmental Effects

- Magnetite particles contribute to airborne particulate pollution.
- They may settle on soil, vegetation and water bodies through atmospheric deposition.

### 3.11.4 Control Measures

- **Vehicle emission controls:** Reduce exhaust-related particulate emissions.
- **Brake-wear reduction:** Use low-emission braking technologies and effective brake-particle capture systems.
- **Industrial emission controls:** Use particulate filters, electrostatic precipitators and other dust-collection systems.

### 3.11.5 Important Prelims Facts

- **Chemical formula:** Fe₃O₄.
- **Classification:** Magnetic iron oxide; may occur as fine or nanoscale particulate matter.
- **Major human-made sources:** Vehicle brakes, engines, thermal power plants and industrial processes.
- **Health concern:** Possible transport of nanoparticles to the brain and associated oxidative stress.
- Magnetite is not a separate pollutant category under India's National Air Quality Index (AQI); airborne magnetite may contribute to measured particulate matter concentrations.

## 3.12 Other Important Air Pollutants

### 3.12.1 Mercury (Hg)

Mercury is a toxic heavy metal that can undergo long-range atmospheric transport and accumulate in ecosystems.

- **Sources:** Coal-fired power plants, metal smelting, waste incineration, artisanal gold mining and natural volcanic emissions.
- **Health effects:** Damages the nervous system, particularly the developing brain of fetuses and children.
- **Environmental effects:** Microorganisms can convert mercury into methylmercury, which bioaccumulates and biomagnifies in aquatic food chains.
- **Control:** Industrial emission controls, reduced mercury use and proper disposal of mercury-containing waste.

### 3.12.2 Radon (Rn)

Radon is a colourless, odourless, radioactive noble gas produced naturally during the radioactive decay of uranium and its decay products in rocks and soil.

- **Sources:** Soil, rocks and groundwater containing uranium or radium.
- **Indoor exposure:** Radon can enter buildings through cracks and openings in foundations and accumulate in poorly ventilated spaces.
- **Health effects:** Radioactive decay products of radon emit alpha particles that can damage lung tissue, increasing the risk of lung cancer.
- **Control:** Radon testing, improved ventilation and systems that prevent radon entry from soil.

Radon is a major cause of lung cancer after smoking.

### 3.12.3 Formaldehyde (HCHO or CH₂O)

Formaldehyde is a colourless, pungent-smelling Volatile Organic Compound (VOC) and an important indoor air pollutant.

- **Sources:** Pressed-wood products, plywood, particleboard, certain furniture, adhesives, building materials, tobacco smoke and combustion.
- **Health effects:** Causes irritation of the eyes, nose and throat; may aggravate respiratory symptoms.
- **Carcinogenicity:** Classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC).
- **Control:** Low-emission building materials, adequate ventilation and reduced indoor combustion emissions.

### 3.12.4 Asbestos Fibres

Asbestos refers to a group of naturally occurring fibrous silicate minerals formerly used extensively for insulation and fire-resistant materials.

- **Sources:** Disturbance of asbestos-containing insulation, roofing sheets, cement products and older construction materials.
- **Exposure:** Airborne asbestos fibres can enter the lungs through inhalation.
- **Health effects:**
  - **Asbestosis:** Chronic lung fibrosis (scarring).
  - **Lung cancer:** Malignant tumour of lung tissue.
  - **Mesothelioma:** Cancer affecting the lining of the lungs or abdomen, strongly associated with asbestos exposure.
- **Control:** Avoid disturbing asbestos-containing materials; ensure safe handling and removal by trained personnel.

All major commercial forms of asbestos are carcinogenic.

### 3.12.5 Hydrogen Sulphide (H₂S)

Hydrogen sulphide is a colourless, highly toxic and flammable gas with a characteristic rotten-egg smell at low concentrations.

- **Sources:** Decomposition of organic matter under oxygen-deficient conditions, sewage systems, petroleum refining, natural gas processing and volcanic activity.
- **Health effects:** Causes eye and respiratory irritation; high concentrations can cause rapid unconsciousness and death.
- **Important distinction:** At high concentrations, H₂S can impair the sense of smell. Therefore, smell is not a reliable warning of dangerous exposure.
- **Control:** Gas detection systems, adequate ventilation and industrial emission treatment.

## 6. Acid Rain and Acid Deposition

### 6.1 Meaning

Acid deposition occurs when acidic substances formed in the atmosphere reach Earth's surface.

| Type           | Mechanism                                                  |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| Wet deposition | Rain, snow, fog and cloud water                            |
| Dry deposition | Acidic gases and particles deposited without precipitation |

Normal unpolluted rainwater is mildly acidic, approximately pH 5.6, due mainly to dissolved atmospheric CO₂.

Acid rain is commonly defined as precipitation with pH below approximately 5.6.

### 6.2 Formation

Major precursors:

- SO₂: Oxidised to form sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).
- NOₓ: Atmospheric reactions produce nitric acid (HNO₃).

Major sources include fossil-fuel combustion, thermal power plants, industries and vehicles.

### 6.3 Effects

- Acidification of lakes and rivers.
- Soil nutrient depletion.
- Mobilisation of toxic aluminium in soils.
- Damage to aquatic life and vegetation.
- Corrosion of metals and deterioration of monuments.

### 6.4 Taj Mahal and air pollution

The Taj Mahal's marble is primarily composed of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃).

- Industrial emissions from the Mathura refinery and other sources have raised concerns about air pollution around Agra.
- Acid deposition can chemically attack calcium carbonate.
- Particulate deposition and other atmospheric pollutants can also cause surface discolouration and deterioration.
- The Taj Trapezium Zone (TTZ) is an important region for pollution-control measures around the monument.

Prelims fact: Acid precipitation and tropospheric ozone are relevant to the historical UPSC question on possible damage to the Taj Mahal. Acid deposition is particularly important for marble deterioration.

### 7.3 Mechanism of ozone depletion

1. CFCs and certain other ODS reach the stratosphere.
2. Ultraviolet radiation breaks down these compounds, releasing reactive chlorine or bromine.
3. These radicals participate in catalytic reactions that destroy ozone molecules.
4. A single reactive halogen atom can participate in many ozone-destruction cycles before being removed from the active cycle.

### 7.4 Antarctic ozone hole

Important contributing conditions:

- Extremely cold Antarctic stratosphere.
- Formation of polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs).
- Strong polar vortex, which isolates cold polar stratospheric air.
- Activation of chlorine and bromine compounds on PSC surfaces.
- Return of sunlight in Antarctic spring, triggering rapid ozone destruction.

Prelims facts:

- Antarctic ozone depletion is most pronounced during Southern Hemisphere spring, particularly September–October.
- The ozone hole refers to severe thinning of the ozone layer, not a literal empty hole.
- Polar stratospheric clouds are crucial to the chemical processes causing severe Antarctic ozone depletion.

### 7.5 Effects of ozone depletion

Increased UV-B exposure can contribute to:

- Skin cancer.
- Cataracts.
- Damage to crops and phytoplankton.
- Adverse effects on ecosystems.

### 7.6 International agreements

- Vienna Convention, 1985: Framework for protecting the ozone layer.
- Montreal Protocol, 1987: Controls ozone-depleting substances.
- Kigali Amendment, 2016: Phasedown of HFCs under the Montreal Protocol framework.

Prelims distinction: Ozone layer depletion and ground-level ozone pollution are separate environmental problems.

## 8. Temperature Inversion and Pollution Dispersion

### 8.1 Normal atmospheric conditions

In the lower troposphere, air temperature generally decreases with increasing altitude.

### 8.2 Temperature inversion

A temperature inversion occurs when air temperature increases with altitude over a particular atmospheric layer.

Normal mixing

Pollutants disperse upward

Temperature inversion

Vertical mixing is suppressed

Effects:

- Restricts vertical mixing.
- Allows pollutants to accumulate near the ground.
- Can aggravate winter smog.

Common conditions include clear nights, weak winds, wintertime surface cooling and valleys.

Prelims fact: Temperature inversion does not generate pollutants; it reduces their dispersion.

### 9.1 Major sources

- Biomass, coal and kerosene combustion.
- Tobacco smoke.
- Poorly ventilated cooking and heating appliances.
- Building materials, paints, solvents and furnishings.

### 9.3 Radon

- Naturally occurring radioactive noble gas.
- Produced through radioactive decay chains involving uranium in rocks and soil.
- Can enter buildings through cracks and openings.
- Accumulates particularly in poorly ventilated enclosed spaces.
- Long-term exposure increases lung-cancer risk.

## 13. Air Pollution and Climate Change

Air pollutants and greenhouse gases overlap, but they are not identical categories.

### 13.1 Important substances

| Substance                  | Air pollution significance                                         | Climate significance            |
| -------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------- |
| CO₂                        | Not normally treated as a conventional toxic ambient air pollutant | Major greenhouse gas            |
| Methane                    | Precursor of tropospheric ozone                                    | Greenhouse gas                  |
| Tropospheric ozone         | Harmful secondary pollutant                                        | Greenhouse gas                  |
| Black carbon / soot        | Fine particulate pollution                                         | Absorbs sunlight; warming       |
| Sulphate aerosols          | Particulate pollution                                              | Generally cooling               |
| Water vapour               | Not ordinarily classified as an air pollutant                      | Major natural greenhouse gas    |
| Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆) | Not a conventional urban air-quality pollutant                     | Extremely potent greenhouse gas |

### 13.2 Black carbon

- Produced mainly through incomplete combustion.
- Strongly absorbs sunlight.
- Contributes to atmospheric warming.
- Deposition on snow and ice lowers albedo and can accelerate melting.
- A constituent of particulate matter, not a greenhouse gas.

### 13.3 Sulphur hexafluoride (SF₆)

- Synthetic, highly stable greenhouse gas.
- Used in certain high-voltage electrical equipment and industrial applications.
- Has a very high Global Warming Potential (GWP).
- Has a very long atmospheric lifetime.

### 13.4 Water vapour

- The most abundant greenhouse gas in Earth's atmosphere.
- Its concentration varies strongly with temperature and humidity.
- Acts predominantly as a climate feedback in contemporary global warming.

### 13.5 Short-Lived Climate Pollutants (SLCPs)

Important examples:

- Methane.
- Black carbon.
- Tropospheric ozone.
- Hydrofluorocarbons (HFCs), whose lifetimes vary considerably by compound.

Prelims fact: Soot, SF₆ and water vapour can all contribute to atmospheric warming, although through different mechanisms.

### 15.2 Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)

- Constituted in 1974 under the Water Act.
- Entrusted with additional functions under the Air Act, 1981.
- Functions under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).
- Coordinates State Pollution Control Boards.
- Undertakes air-quality monitoring and provides technical guidance.

### 15.3 National Clean Air Programme (NCAP)

- Launched by MoEFCC in January 2019.
- Focuses on improving air quality in identified cities.
- Includes city-specific action plans, monitoring and pollution-control measures.
- Revised target: up to 40% reduction in PM₁₀ concentrations or achievement of national PM₁₀ standards by 2025–26, compared with the 2017–18 baseline.

Prelims fact: NCAP is a government programme, not a separate Act of Parliament.

### 15.4 Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP)

- Emergency air-pollution response framework for Delhi-NCR.
- Measures become progressively stricter with worsening air quality.
- Uses air-quality severity and forecasts.
- Implemented through directions coordinated by CAQM.

### 15.5 Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM)

- Established under the CAQM Act, 2021.
- Responsible for coordinating air-quality management in NCR and specified adjoining areas.
- Has statutory powers to issue directions and coordinate pollution-control measures.

### 15.6 Bharat Stage emission standards

- Regulate vehicular exhaust emissions.
- Based broadly on European emission-standard frameworks.
- India moved directly from BS-IV to BS-VI nationwide for new vehicles on 1 April 2020, skipping BS-V.
- BS-VI introduced stricter emission limits and additional testing and compliance requirements.

Important: Bharat Stage standards apply to vehicles; NAAQS apply to ambient air.

## 18. Common UPSC Prelims Traps

1. Ground-level ozone is not principally emitted directly; it forms through atmospheric photochemistry.
2. PM₂.₅ is a subset of PM₁₀, not the reverse.
3. Black carbon is not a greenhouse gas; it is a light-absorbing aerosol constituent.
4. Temperature inversion does not create pollutants; it restricts dispersion.
5. Anthropogenic acid rain is mainly associated with SO₂ and NOₓ, not CO₂.
6. CO and CO₂ are different: CO is a toxic product of incomplete combustion; CO₂ is a major greenhouse gas.
7. NOₓ and N₂O are different: NOₓ generally refers to NO and NO₂; N₂O is nitrous oxide.
8. Stratospheric ozone is protective, whereas elevated ground-level ozone is harmful.
9. HFCs are generally not ozone-depleting substances, unlike CFCs.
10. Polar stratospheric clouds contribute to Antarctic ozone depletion, not ordinary tropospheric clouds.
11. Copper smelting can cause both air and water/soil pollution through SO₂ emissions and metal-containing wastes.
12. Ammonia from cattle is associated mainly with manure and urine, whereas enteric fermentation primarily produces methane.
13. Magnetite particles can arise from brake wear, not only engine exhaust.
14. CO₂ and methane are not included in India's National AQI, although they are greenhouse gases.
15. WHO guidelines and India's NAAQS are different, and their numerical values need not match.
