# 13. Ecosystem Services

**Ecosystem services** are the benefits that humans obtain from ecosystems.

## 13.1 Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (MA)

The **Millennium Ecosystem Assessment (2001–2005)** was a United Nations-supported international scientific assessment of the world's ecosystems and their contributions to human well-being.

**Important facts:**

- **Launched:** 2001, following a call by UN Secretary-General Kofi Annan.
- **Completed:** 2005.
- **Participation:** More than 1,360 experts worldwide.
- **Objective:** Assess changes in ecosystems, their effects on human well-being, and measures for conservation and sustainable use.
- **Major report:** *Ecosystems and Human Well-being* (2005).
- **Key finding:** Approximately **60% (15 out of 24)** of the ecosystem services examined were being degraded or used unsustainably.

## 13.2 Classification of Ecosystem Services

The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment classified ecosystem services into **four major categories**.

| Category | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Provisioning services** | Material products obtained from ecosystems | Food, freshwater, timber, medicinal resources |
| **Regulating services** | Benefits obtained from the regulation of ecological processes | Climate regulation, pollination, flood regulation, water purification |
| **Supporting services** | Ecological processes necessary for the production of other ecosystem services | Nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production |
| **Cultural services** | Non-material benefits obtained from ecosystems | Recreation, tourism, spiritual, aesthetic and educational benefits |

### 13.2.1 Important Distinctions

- **Provisioning:** Ecosystems supply products used by humans.
- **Regulating:** Ecosystems regulate environmental conditions and ecological processes.
- **Supporting:** Ecological processes enable the production of other ecosystem services.
- **Cultural:** Ecosystems provide recreational, spiritual and other non-material benefits.

**Important:** Supporting services underpin the other three categories. Some ecological processes contribute to multiple ecosystem services.

## 13.3 UPSC Previous Year Question (2012)

**Q. The Millennium Ecosystem Assessment describes the following major categories of ecosystem services — provisioning, supporting, regulating, preserving and cultural.**

**Which one of the following is supporting service?**

A. Production of food and water  
B. Control of climate and disease  
C. Nutrient cycling and crop pollination  
D. Maintenance of diversity

**Expected examination answer: C**

**Explanation:**

- **Option A:** Food and water production are provisioning services.
- **Option B:** Climate and disease regulation are regulating services.
- **Option C:** Nutrient cycling is a supporting service, but crop pollination is classified as a regulating service.
- **Option D:** Maintenance of biodiversity contributes to ecosystem functioning and the continued provision of ecosystem services, but is not a separate major category in the MA classification.

**Important clarification:** The supplied question has two inconsistencies with the standard Millennium Ecosystem Assessment classification:

1. The MA recognizes **four categories**, not five. *Preserving* is not a separate category.
2. **Nutrient cycling** is a supporting service, whereas **crop pollination** is a regulating service.

Thus, **C is the expected answer**, but the option is not entirely consistent with the MA classification. The original UPSC question paper should be consulted to verify the exact wording.

For UPSC Prelims, remember the standard four-category classification and, particularly, the distinction between **nutrient cycling (supporting)** and **pollination (regulating)**.

## 11.1 Sustainable Development: Basic Concepts

**Sustainable development** is development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.

This definition was popularized by the **Brundtland Commission (1987)**.

### Three Dimensions of Sustainable Development

| Dimension | Main Focus |
|---|---|
| Environmental | Conservation of natural resources, ecosystems and biodiversity |
| Economic | Long-term economic prosperity and efficient resource use |
| Social | Equity, inclusion, health and human well-being |

### Important Concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Intergenerational equity | Fairness between present and future generations |
| Intragenerational equity | Fairness among people living in the same generation |
| Carrying capacity | Maximum population or level of activity an environment can sustain under specified conditions |
| Ecological footprint | Biologically productive land and water required to support consumption and absorb specified wastes |
| Biocapacity | Capacity of ecosystems to regenerate biological resources and absorb specified wastes |
| Ecological deficit | Ecological footprint exceeds available biocapacity |
| Resource efficiency | Obtaining more economic or social value from fewer resources |
| Decoupling | Reducing the relationship between economic growth and resource use or environmental pressure |

### Important Distinction

- **Relative decoupling:** Environmental pressure increases more slowly than economic output.
- **Absolute decoupling:** Environmental pressure decreases while economic output increases.

# 11.8 Environmental Management

Environmental management involves planning, implementing and monitoring measures to prevent environmental degradation and promote sustainable resource use.

### Important Approaches

| Approach | Main Purpose |
|---|---|
| Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) | Assess likely impacts of proposed projects |
| Strategic Environmental Assessment (SEA) | Assess environmental implications of policies, plans and programmes |
| Environmental Management System (EMS) | Systematic management of organizational environmental responsibilities |
| Environmental Audit | Evaluate environmental performance and compliance |
| Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) | Evaluate environmental impacts across a product's life cycle |
| Environmental Monitoring | Track environmental conditions and changes |
| Carrying Capacity Assessment | Evaluate the environmental capacity to support activities |

### EIA vs SEA

| EIA | SEA |
|---|---|
| Primarily project-level | Policy, plan or programme level |
| Usually undertaken before project approval | Applied earlier in strategic decision-making |
| Example: Proposed highway project | Example: Regional transport development plan |

# 11.11 Life Cycle Assessment (LCA)

Life Cycle Assessment evaluates environmental impacts associated with different stages of a product's life.

### Main Stages

1. Raw material extraction.
2. Manufacturing.
3. Transportation and distribution.
4. Use.
5. End-of-life treatment and disposal.

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Cradle-to-grave | Assessment from raw material extraction to final disposal |
| Cradle-to-gate | Assessment from raw material extraction to the factory gate |
| Cradle-to-cradle | Design approach emphasizing reuse and circular material flows |
| Carbon footprint | Greenhouse gas emissions associated with an activity or product |
| Water footprint | Freshwater use and pollution-related impacts associated with consumption or production |

### Important Facts

- LCA can identify environmental burdens that occur outside the direct manufacturing stage.
- A product's environmental impact cannot always be determined solely from its disposal characteristics.
- ISO 14040 and ISO 14044 are important standards relating to LCA.

---

# 11.12 Circular Economy

A **circular economy** seeks to minimize waste and resource consumption by keeping products and materials in use and regenerating natural systems.

### Important Principles

1. Eliminate waste and pollution through design.
2. Circulate products and materials.
3. Regenerate nature.

### Linear vs Circular Economy

| Linear Economy | Circular Economy |
|---|---|
| Take–Make–Dispose | Reduce–Reuse–Repair–Recycle |
| High dependence on virgin materials | Greater material recovery and reuse |
| Waste treated mainly as an end product | Waste prevention emphasized |

### Important Concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Reuse | Using a product again without major reprocessing |
| Repair | Restoring functionality |
| Refurbishment | Restoring a product to an acceptable working condition |
| Remanufacturing | Rebuilding a product using recovered components |
| Recycling | Processing waste into usable materials |
| Upcycling | Converting waste into products of higher perceived value |
| Downcycling | Recycling into materials of lower quality or utility |

**Important:** Recycling is one component of a circular economy; it is not the entire circular economy.

# 11.13 Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR)

EPR is an environmental policy approach that places specified responsibilities on producers for the management of products or packaging after consumer use.

### Important Applications in India

- Plastic packaging.
- Electronic waste.
- Battery waste.
- Waste tyres.

### Important Objectives

- Improve collection and recycling.
- Encourage environmentally sound waste management.
- Promote resource recovery.
- Reduce the burden on municipal waste systems.

### Important Distinction

| EPR | Polluter Pays Principle |
|---|---|
| Assigns specified post-consumer responsibilities to producers | Requires polluters to bear pollution-related costs |
| Often implemented through waste-management regulations | Broad environmental governance principle |

# 11.14 Green Economy

A **green economy** is generally understood as an economy that improves human well-being and social equity while reducing environmental risks and ecological scarcity.

### Important Features

- Low-carbon development.
- Resource efficiency.
- Reduced pollution.
- Biodiversity conservation.
- Social inclusion.

### Green Growth vs Sustainable Development

| Green Growth | Sustainable Development |
|---|---|
| Focuses on economic growth with reduced environmental impacts | Integrates environmental, economic and social objectives |
| Primarily an economic-development approach | Broader development framework |

### Important Fact

A green economy does not necessarily imply the complete elimination of industrial activity.

# 11.18 Carbon Markets and Carbon Pricing

|---|---|

| Emissions Trading System (ETS) | Trading of emission allowances under a regulatory framework |
| Carbon crediting | Issuance of credits for eligible emission reductions or removals |
| Carbon offsetting | Use of eligible reductions or removals to compensate for emissions |

### Important Concepts

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|

# 11.19 Payment for Ecosystem Services (PES)

PES refers to arrangements in which beneficiaries of ecosystem services provide incentives to those who maintain or improve those services.

### Examples

- Payments for watershed conservation.
- Incentives for forest conservation.
- Compensation for maintaining ecosystem services.
- Payments associated with verified carbon sequestration.

### Important Facts

- Ecosystem services include provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting services.
- PES can provide economic incentives for conservation.
- Not every ecosystem service is traded through a market.

The stock of natural resources and ecosystems that provides benefits to humans.

### Major Ecosystem Services

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Provisioning | Food, freshwater, timber |
| Regulating | Climate regulation, flood control, pollination |
| Cultural | Recreation, spiritual and aesthetic benefits |

**Natural capital** refers to the stock of natural assets.

# 11.21 Natural Capital Accounting

Natural capital accounting incorporates environmental assets and ecosystem services into economic and statistical accounts.

### Important Framework

**System of Environmental-Economic Accounting (SEEA)**

Developed under the United Nations statistical framework.

### Important Components

- Environmental assets.
- Physical resource flows.
- Ecosystem extent.
- Ecosystem condition.
- Ecosystem services.

### Important Facts

- SEEA complements conventional national accounts.
- It helps assess the relationship between economic activity and environmental resources.
- It does not mean that all environmental values can be fully expressed in monetary terms.

# 11.22 Ecological Footprint and Biocapacity

### Ecological Footprint

Measures the biologically productive land and water area required to support specified human consumption and absorb certain wastes, particularly carbon emissions.

### Biocapacity

Measures the capacity of ecosystems to produce renewable biological resources and absorb specified wastes.

### Important Terms

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Ecological deficit | Ecological footprint exceeds biocapacity |
| Ecological reserve | Biocapacity exceeds ecological footprint |
| Global hectare (gha) | Standardized unit used in ecological footprint accounting |
| Earth Overshoot Day | Estimated date when humanity's annual demand exceeds Earth's annual regenerative capacity |

### Important Distinction

Ecological footprint is not the same as carbon footprint.

- **Carbon footprint:** Greenhouse gas emissions.
- **Ecological footprint:** Broader biologically productive area required to support consumption.

# 11.23 Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC)

The Environmental Kuznets Curve is a hypothesis proposing an inverted-U relationship between environmental degradation and per-capita income for certain environmental indicators.

### Hypothesis

1. At low income levels, pollution may increase with economic development.
2. At higher income levels, environmental regulation and cleaner technologies may reduce certain pollutants.

### Important Facts

- EKC is a hypothesis, not a universal law.
- Its applicability differs across pollutants, countries and time periods.
- Economic growth does not automatically ensure environmental improvement.

# 11.24 Sustainable Agriculture

### Important Approaches

| Approach | Main Feature |
|---|---|
| Organic farming | Restricts use of specified synthetic inputs |
| Agroforestry | Integrates trees with crops or livestock |
| Conservation agriculture | Minimal soil disturbance, soil cover and crop diversification |
| Integrated Pest Management | Combines biological, cultural, physical and appropriate chemical pest-control methods |
| Precision agriculture | Uses data and technology for targeted input application |
| Climate-smart agriculture | Productivity, adaptation and mitigation objectives |
| Integrated Nutrient Management | Combines nutrient sources for sustainable soil fertility |

### Important Facts

- Organic farming does not necessarily mean zero pesticide use.
- Agroforestry can contribute to carbon storage and biodiversity.
- Conservation agriculture emphasizes soil cover and reduced disturbance.
- Integrated Pest Management does not prohibit all chemical pesticide use.

# 11.26 Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN)

### Definition

A condition in which the amount and quality of land resources necessary to support ecosystem functions and services remain stable or increase.

### International Framework

- United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD).
- SDG Target **15.3**.

### Important Approaches

1. Avoid land degradation.
2. Reduce ongoing degradation.
3. Restore degraded land.

### India's Target

India has announced a target to restore **26 million hectares of degraded land by 2030**.

### Important Distinction

Land degradation neutrality does not imply that no land anywhere can undergo degradation. It requires balancing losses with equivalent gains while prioritizing avoidance and reduction of degradation.
