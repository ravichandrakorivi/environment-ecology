# **1. Ecology: Basic Concepts**

## **1.1 Ecology**

**Ecology** is the branch of biology that studies interactions:

- Among living organisms.
- Between living organisms and their physical environment.

**Ernst Haeckel** coined the term *Ecology* in **1866**.

---

## **1.2 Levels of Ecological Organization**

![Levels of Ecological Organization](images/01_ecological_organization_levels.png)

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Organism** | An individual living being | One deer |
| **Population** | Individuals of the same species living in a particular area | Deer population in a forest |
| **Community** | Populations of different species living and interacting in an area | Plants, deer, tigers and microorganisms |
| **Ecosystem** | A biological community interacting with its abiotic environment | Forest ecosystem |
| **Biome** | Large ecological region characterized by climate and dominant vegetation | Tropical rainforest |
| **Biosphere** | Global zone of Earth supporting life | All ecosystems on Earth |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Population:** Individuals of one species.
- **Community:** Populations of different species.
- **Ecosystem:** Includes both biotic and abiotic components.
- **Biosphere:** Encompasses all ecosystems on Earth.

**A. G. Tansley** introduced the term *Ecosystem* in **1935**.

## **1.3 Habitat, Ecological Niche, Home Range and Territory**

These terms describe different aspects of where an organism lives and how it functions.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Habitat** | Physical environment in which an organism lives | Pond inhabited by a frog |
| **Ecological niche** | Functional role, resource requirements and interactions of an organism within its ecosystem | Frog feeding on insects and serving as prey for snakes |
| **Home range** | Area regularly used by an animal for feeding, resting, mating and other activities | Area regularly used by a deer |
| **Territory** | Area actively defended by an animal against other individuals | Nesting area defended by a bird |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Habitat:** Where an organism lives.
- **Ecological niche:** How an organism lives, obtains resources and interacts.
- **Home range:** Area regularly used by an animal; not necessarily defended.
- **Territory:** Area actively defended; may constitute only part of an animal's home range.

![Habitat vs Ecological Niche](images/02_habitat_homerange_territory_niche.png)

### **Competitive Exclusion Principle**

**G. F. Gause** is associated with the Competitive Exclusion Principle.

Two species occupying identical ecological niches and competing for the same limiting resources **cannot coexist indefinitely under stable environmental conditions**.

**Prelims fact:** Species sharing a habitat may coexist by occupying different ecological niches.

## **1.4 Ecotone and Edge Effect**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Ecotone** | Transition zone between two ecological communities or ecosystems |
| **Edge effect** | Changes in species composition, abundance or ecological processes near habitat boundaries |

**Examples of ecotones:**

- Forest–grassland boundary.
- Mangrove–terrestrial forest transition.

**Important distinction:**

- **Ecotone:** The transition zone itself.
- **Edge effect:** The ecological changes associated with habitat boundaries.

**Prelims fact:** Ecotones may support species from adjoining communities as well as species adapted to transitional conditions. Species diversity can be higher in some ecotones, but this is not universal.

## **1.5 Keystone Species**

![Sea Otters](images/03_01_keytone_species.png)

![Sea Stars](images/03_02_keytone_seastars.png)

A **keystone species** is a species that has a disproportionately large influence on the structure or functioning of an ecosystem relative to its abundance or biomass.

Its removal may cause major changes in the abundance, distribution or survival of other species.

**Examples:**

- **Sea otters:** Control sea urchin populations, helping maintain kelp forests.
- **Certain sea stars:** Control mussel populations, preventing mussels from monopolizing rocky shore habitats.
- **Beavers:** Modify habitats by building dams, creating wetlands that support other organisms.

**Important:** Keystone species are not necessarily the most abundant species, and they need not be apex predators.

![Beaver](images/03_02_keytone_beaver.png)

## **1.6 Other Important Ecological Categories**

| Category | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Keystone species** | Has a disproportionately large ecological influence relative to its abundance | Sea otter |
| **Dominant species** | Has high abundance or biomass and strongly influences the community | Dominant grasses in grasslands |
| **Foundation species** | Creates or maintains habitat that supports other organisms | Reef-building corals |
| **Indicator species** | Its presence, absence or condition provides information about environmental conditions | Lichens as indicators of air pollution |
| **Umbrella species** | Protecting its habitat can also protect many other species | Tiger |
| **Flagship species** | Used to generate public awareness and support for conservation | Giant panda |
| **Ecosystem engineer** | Physically modifies habitats or the availability of resources | Beaver |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Keystone vs dominant:** Keystone species are identified by their disproportionately large ecological effects, whereas dominant species are identified mainly by their abundance or biomass.
- **Umbrella vs flagship:** Umbrella species are selected for their potential to protect other species through habitat conservation; flagship species are selected mainly to promote conservation awareness and support.
- **Foundation species vs ecosystem engineer:** Foundation species create or maintain habitat, while ecosystem engineers physically modify the environment. These roles can overlap.

**Note:** These ecological categories are not mutually exclusive. A species may belong to more than one category.

![Key Ecological Caterogies](images/03_02_ecoloical_categories.png)

## **1.7 Trophic Cascades**

A **trophic cascade** occurs when changes at one trophic level produce indirect effects on organisms at other trophic levels through feeding relationships.

A keystone predator may initiate a trophic cascade by controlling herbivore populations.

**Example: Sea Otters, Sea Urchins and Kelp Forests**

| Organism | Ecological Role |
|---|---|
| **Sea otter** | Predator; keystone species in certain kelp forest ecosystems |
| **Sea urchin** | Herbivore that feeds on kelp |
| **Kelp** | Primary producer that provides food and habitat for marine organisms |

**When sea otters are present:**

1. Sea otters prey on sea urchins.
2. Sea urchin grazing pressure on kelp is reduced.
3. Kelp forests can flourish, supporting marine biodiversity.

**When sea otters decline or disappear:**

1. Sea urchin populations may increase.
2. Increased grazing reduces kelp abundance.
3. Loss of kelp habitat adversely affects other marine organisms.

This is an example of a **top-down trophic cascade**, because a predator indirectly influences primary producers by controlling herbivores.

**Top-down vs Bottom-up Control**

| Feature | Top-down Control | Bottom-up Control |
|---|---|---|
| **Meaning** | Higher trophic levels influence organisms at lower trophic levels | Resource availability and primary productivity influence higher trophic levels |
| **Example** | Sea otters control sea urchins, indirectly protecting kelp | Increased nutrients may increase plant growth and support more herbivores |

**Important:** Not every keystone species causes a trophic cascade. Keystone effects may also arise through habitat modification, mutualism or other ecological interactions.

## **1.8 Geographical Distribution and Modes of Speciation**

**Speciation** is the evolutionary process through which new species arise from existing populations.

Based on the geographical relationship between populations, four important modes of speciation are recognized: **allopatric, sympatric, parapatric and peripatric**.

### **1.8.1 Four Modes of Speciation**

| Mode | Geographical Relationship | How Speciation Occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **Allopatric** | Populations are geographically separated by a barrier or distance | Isolated populations evolve independently and develop reproductive isolation |
| **Sympatric** | Populations occupy the same geographical area | Reproductive isolation develops without geographical separation |
| **Parapatric** | Populations occupy adjacent geographical areas, with limited gene flow | Neighbouring populations gradually diverge despite some genetic exchange |
| **Peripatric** | A small population becomes geographically isolated at the edge of a larger population's range | The small isolated population evolves independently and may form a new species |

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### **1.8.2 Understanding the Differences**

**1. Allopatric Speciation — Geographical Separation**

- A population becomes divided by a geographical barrier, such as a mountain, river or sea.
- The separated populations evolve independently.
- Over time, reproductive isolation may develop, resulting in distinct species.

**Example:** Divergence of populations separated on different islands.

**2. Sympatric Speciation — Same Geographical Area**

- Populations remain within the same geographical area.
- Reproductive isolation develops without a physical geographical barrier.
- New species may arise through mechanisms such as **polyploidy** (increase in the number of complete chromosome sets), particularly in plants.

**Example:** Formation of new plant species through polyploidy.

**3. Parapatric Speciation — Adjacent Geographical Areas**

- Populations occupy neighbouring geographical areas.
- Some gene flow may occur where their ranges meet.
- Different environmental conditions and natural selection can cause the populations to diverge and eventually become reproductively isolated.

**Example:** Neighbouring plant populations adapting to contaminated and uncontaminated soils.

**4. Peripatric Speciation — Small Isolated Peripheral Population**

- A small population becomes isolated at the edge of a larger population's geographical range.
- Because the isolated population is small, **genetic drift** (random changes in allele frequencies) and natural selection may contribute strongly to its divergence.
- Over time, it may develop reproductive isolation and become a separate species.

**Example:** A small population colonizing an isolated island and subsequently evolving into a distinct species.

**Important:** Peripatric speciation is considered a special form of **allopatric speciation**, distinguished by the small size and peripheral location of the isolated population.

### **1.8.3 Important UPSC Prelims Distinctions**

| Term | Key Identification |
|---|---|
| **Allopatric** | Geographical separation |
| **Sympatric** | Same geographical area |
| **Parapatric** | Adjacent geographical areas with limited gene flow |
| **Peripatric** | Small, geographically isolated population at the periphery |

**Remember:**

- **Geographical isolation** is central to allopatric and peripatric speciation.
- **Sympatric speciation** occurs without geographical separation.
- **Parapatric speciation** can occur despite limited gene flow between neighbouring populations.
- **Peripatric speciation** is a special case of allopatric speciation.
- **Geographical separation does not automatically produce new species.** Speciation requires evolutionary divergence leading to reproductive isolation.
- These four terms describe **geographical patterns and modes of speciation**, not conservation status or extinction risk.

# **2. Ecosystem: Structure and Functions**

## **2.1 Definition**

An **ecosystem** is a functional unit of nature comprising **living organisms (biotic components)** interacting with one another and with their **physical environment (abiotic components)**.

**A. G. Tansley** introduced the term *Ecosystem* in **1935**.

## **2.2 Components of an Ecosystem**

![Components of an Ecosystem](images/04_ecosystem_components.png)

| Component | Category | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Biotic** | Producers | Green plants, algae, cyanobacteria |
| | Consumers | Herbivores, carnivores, omnivores |
| | Decomposers | Bacteria, fungi |
| **Abiotic** | Physical factors | Sunlight, temperature, water, soil |
| | Chemical factors | Oxygen, carbon dioxide, minerals |

**Important distinction:** Biotic components are living organisms, whereas abiotic components are the non-living environmental factors influencing them.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

## **2.3 Types of Ecosystems**

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Terrestrial** | Forests, grasslands, deserts, tundra |
| **Freshwater** | Rivers, lakes, ponds |
| **Marine** | Oceans, coral reefs |
| **Transitional** | Estuaries, mangrove ecosystems |
| **Artificial** | Agricultural fields, aquariums |

![Types of Ecosystems](images/05_ecosystem_types.png)

## **2.4 Fundamental Functions of an Ecosystem**

| Function | Description |
|---|---|
| **Energy flow** | Energy passes from producers to consumers and is eventually dissipated as heat |
| **Nutrient cycling** | Nutrients circulate between living organisms and their physical environment |

**UPSC Prelims facts:**

- **Energy flows in one direction** through trophic levels and is not recycled.
- **Nutrients are recycled** through biogeochemical cycles.

# **3. Functional Groups in Ecosystems**

Organisms are classified into functional groups based on how they obtain food and their roles in energy transfer and nutrient cycling.

![Functional Groups](images/07_functional_groups.png)

## **3.1 Producers (Autotrophs)**

**Producers** synthesize organic substances from inorganic materials using light or chemical energy.

| Type | Energy Source | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Photoautotrophs** | Sunlight | Green plants, algae, cyanobacteria |
| **Chemoautotrophs** | Energy released by oxidation of inorganic substances | Nitrifying bacteria, sulphur-oxidizing bacteria |

---

## **3.2 Consumers (Heterotrophs)**

**Consumers** are organisms that obtain organic food by feeding on other organisms.

### **3.2.1 Classification Based on Trophic Level**

| Category | Food Source | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary consumers** | Producers | Deer, grasshoppers, herbivorous zooplankton |
| **Secondary consumers** | Primary consumers | Frogs, small carnivorous fish |
| **Tertiary consumers** | Secondary consumers | Snakes, larger predatory fish |
| **Quaternary consumers** | Tertiary consumers | Large predatory fish, birds of prey |

### **3.2.2 Classification Based on Feeding Habits**

| Category | Food Source |
|---|---|
| **Herbivores** | Plants and other producers |
| **Carnivores** | Other animals |
| **Omnivores** | Both plant and animal matter |

### **3.2.3 Important UPSC Prelims Facts**

- **Primary consumers** occupy the **second trophic level**, immediately above producers.
- **Secondary consumers** occupy the third trophic level, while tertiary consumers occupy the fourth.
- **Omnivores** may occupy different trophic levels depending on their diet.
- **Trophic levels are positions in a food chain**, not permanent classifications of organisms.

![Photoautotrophs](images/08_autotrophs.png)

## **3.3 Decomposers**

![Decomposers](images/09_decomposers.png)

**Decomposers** break down dead organic matter and organic wastes into simpler substances through **enzymatic decomposition**, releasing nutrients back into the environment.

**Major decomposers:**
- **Bacteria**
- **Fungi**

**Important UPSC Prelims facts:**

- Decomposers primarily break down organic matter **externally using enzymes** and absorb the resulting soluble substances.
- They play a crucial role in **nutrient recycling**.
- **Viruses are not classified as decomposers**, as they lack independent metabolism.

**Detritivores** are organisms that feed on dead organic matter (detritus) by ingesting and digesting it.

**Examples:** Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice and dung beetles.

## **3.5 Decomposers vs Detritivores**

| Feature | Decomposers | Detritivores |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Primarily digest organic matter externally using enzymes and absorb the resulting soluble substances | Ingest and digest dead organic matter internally |
| **Examples** | Bacteria, fungi | Earthworms, millipedes, woodlice, dung beetles |
| **Ecological role** | Chemically break down organic matter and recycle nutrients | Fragment and digest detritus, facilitating further microbial decomposition |

**Important:** Both decomposers and detritivores contribute to the breakdown of dead organic matter and nutrient recycling.

# **4 Food Chains and Food Webs**

## **4.1 Food Chain**

A **food chain** is a linear sequence of organisms showing the transfer of food energy through feeding relationships.

**Example:** Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk

- Each organism obtains energy by consuming the preceding organism.
- **Arrows point in the direction of energy transfer** (from food to consumer).

## **4.2 Trophic Levels**

A **trophic level** is the feeding position of an organism in a food chain.

| Trophic Level | Category | Example |
|---|---|---|
| First | Producers | Grass |
| Second | Primary consumers | Grasshopper |
| Third | Secondary consumers | Frog |
| Fourth | Tertiary consumers | Snake |
| Fifth | Quaternary consumers | Hawk |

**Important:**
- Producers occupy the **first trophic level**; primary consumers occupy the **second**.
- An organism may occupy different trophic levels in different food chains.
- Decomposers act on organic matter originating from **multiple trophic levels**.

![Food Chains](images/11_food_chains.png)

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

## **4.3 Types of Food Chains**

Food chains are commonly classified by their starting point:

| Feature | Grazing Food Chain | Detritus Food Chain |
|---|---|---|
| Begins with | Living producers | Dead organic matter (detritus) |
| Example | Grass → Grasshopper → Frog | Dead leaves → Earthworm → Bird |
| Main pathway | Energy from living producers passes to consumers | Energy in dead organic matter passes through detritus-feeding organisms and associated decomposers |

- **Grazing food chains** begin with green plants, phytoplankton or other living producers.
- **Detritus food chains** begin with dead organic matter and involve detritivores and/or decomposers.
- Both types occur in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems.

## **4.4 Food Web**

![Food Web](images/12_food_webs.png)

A **food web** is a network of interconnected food chains within an ecosystem.

- Organisms may participate in more than one food chain.
- Multiple feeding pathways can provide alternative routes for energy transfer and may increase ecosystem resilience.

| Food Chain | Food Web |
|---|---|
| Linear feeding sequence | Network of interconnected feeding sequences |
| Single pathway | Multiple pathways |
| Simplified representation | More realistic representation of feeding relationships |

# **5. Energy Flow and Ecological Pyramids**

## **5.1 Energy Flow**

The **Sun is the principal energy source** for most ecosystems.

**Sunlight → Producers → Primary consumers → Secondary consumers → Higher consumers**

- Producers convert solar energy into chemical energy through photosynthesis.
- Consumers obtain chemical energy through feeding.
- Organisms use energy for metabolic activities, during which some energy is dissipated as heat.

**Prelims facts:**
- Energy flow through ecosystems is **unidirectional**.
- Energy available for transfer generally decreases at successive trophic levels.
- **Energy is not recycled; nutrients are recycled.**

## **5.2 Lindeman's 10% Law**

![Lindeman's 10% Law](images/13_lindeman_law.png)

**Raymond Lindeman (1942)** is associated with the **10% Law**: on average, approximately **10% of energy** at one trophic level is transferred to the next.

| Trophic Level | Illustrative Energy |
|---|---:|
| Producers | 10,000 kJ |
| Primary consumers | 1,000 kJ |
| Secondary consumers | 100 kJ |
| Tertiary consumers | 10 kJ |

**Important:** The 10% figure is an **approximation**, not a fixed rule. Energy not passed to the next trophic level may be dissipated as heat through metabolism or remain in unconsumed and unassimilated material.

## **5.3 Ecological Pyramids**

An **ecological pyramid** graphically represents the trophic structure of an ecosystem in terms of the **number of organisms, biomass or energy flow** at successive trophic levels.

### **5.3.1 Types of Ecological Pyramids**

| Type | What It Represents | Shape | Important Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pyramid of Numbers** | Number of individual organisms at each trophic level | Upright or inverted | Generally upright in grasslands; may be inverted in tree-based food chains |
| **Pyramid of Biomass** | Total mass of living organisms (standing biomass) at each trophic level | Upright or inverted | Generally upright in terrestrial ecosystems; may be inverted in aquatic ecosystems |
| **Pyramid of Energy** | Rate of energy flow through each trophic level per unit area per unit time | **Always upright** | Energy flow decreases at successive trophic levels |

### **5.3.2 Why Can Ecological Pyramids Be Inverted?**

**A. Pyramid of Numbers**

- In a grassland ecosystem, numerous grasses support fewer herbivores and still fewer carnivores. The pyramid is generally **upright**.
- In a tree-based food chain, a single tree may support numerous insects. Therefore, the number of primary consumers can exceed the number of producers, resulting in an **inverted pyramid of numbers**.

**B. Pyramid of Biomass**

- In terrestrial ecosystems, producer biomass generally exceeds consumer biomass, resulting in an **upright pyramid**.
- In some aquatic ecosystems, phytoplankton have **low standing biomass but high productivity and rapid turnover**. Consequently, the standing biomass of zooplankton may exceed that of phytoplankton at a given time, resulting in an **inverted pyramid of biomass**.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

**C. Pyramid of Energy**

- Energy available for transfer decreases at successive trophic levels because energy is used in metabolic activities and dissipated as heat, while some remains in unconsumed or unassimilated material.
- Therefore, the **pyramid of energy is always upright**.

### **Important UPSC Prelims Facts**

- **Pyramids of numbers and biomass** may be upright or inverted.
- **Pyramid of energy** is always upright.
- **Standing biomass** refers to the mass of living organisms present at a particular time.
- **Energy pyramids** represent energy flow per unit area per unit time, whereas biomass pyramids represent the amount of living material present at a particular time.
- An inverted aquatic biomass pyramid does **not** imply an inverted energy pyramid.

## **5.4 Higher Trophic Levels**

Organisms at higher trophic levels, particularly **top predators (apex predators)**, are often more vulnerable to disturbances in food chains.

**Why are top predators particularly vulnerable?**

- **Limited energy availability:** Energy decreases at successive trophic levels, leaving relatively little energy available to support top predators.
- **Smaller populations:** Top predators generally occur in smaller populations, making them more vulnerable to population declines.
- **Dependence on prey:** A decline in prey populations at lower trophic levels can reduce food availability and threaten their survival.
- **Biomagnification:** Certain persistent pollutants become increasingly concentrated at higher trophic levels, potentially causing greater harm to top predators (Section 1.6).

# **6 Bioaccumulation and Biomagnification**

## **6.1 Bioaccumulation**

**Bioaccumulation** is the buildup of a substance **within an organism over time** when its uptake exceeds its elimination.

## **6.2 Biomagnification**

**Biomagnification** is an increase in the concentration of certain persistent substances at **successively higher trophic levels** of a food chain.

Substances prone to biomagnification are typically **persistent**, difficult to metabolize or eliminate, and transferred through feeding.

**Examples:**
- **DDT** and certain other persistent organochlorine pesticides.
- **Methylmercury**.

### **Bioaccumulation vs Biomagnification**

| Bioaccumulation | Biomagnification |
|---|---|
| Occurs **within an organism** over time | Occurs **across trophic levels** in a food chain |
| Uptake exceeds elimination | Concentrations increase from prey to higher-level predators |
| Does not require comparing different trophic levels | Requires comparing concentrations along a food chain |

# **7. Primary Producers in Aquatic Ecosystems**

Aquatic primary producers include **phytoplankton, attached algae and aquatic macrophytes**.

## **7.1. Phytoplankton**

**Phytoplankton** are microscopic, predominantly photosynthetic organisms that drift or remain suspended in water. They form the base of many aquatic food chains.

**Major phytoplankton groups:**

| Group | Important Features | Distribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Diatoms** | Photosynthetic algae with silica-based cell walls (frustules) | Marine and freshwater |
| **Green algae** | Photosynthetic eukaryotes containing chlorophyll *a* and *b* | Marine and freshwater |
| **Cyanobacteria** | Photosynthetic prokaryotes; some species fix atmospheric nitrogen | Marine and freshwater |
| **Dinoflagellates** | Many are photosynthetic; some cause harmful algal blooms | Predominantly marine; also freshwater |
| **Coccolithophores** | Unicellular algae covered with calcium carbonate plates | Predominantly marine |

**Note:** Prokaryotes lack a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, whereas eukaryotes possess both.

## **7.2 Attached Algae**

- Algae that grow attached to submerged surfaces such as rocks and aquatic plants.
- Include certain **diatoms and green algae**.
- Important primary producers in shallow waters and streams.

## **7.3 Aquatic Macrophytes**

- Large, visible aquatic plants and algae.
- Include **submerged, floating and emergent aquatic plants**, as well as **marine macroalgae (seaweeds)**.
- Particularly important in shallow freshwater and coastal ecosystems.

**Note:** Emergent aquatic plants are plants rooted in waterlogged soil or submerged sediments, with their stems, leaves or flowers extending above the water surface.

### **Important UPSC Prelims Fact**

- Phytoplankton dominate primary production in many open-water ecosystems, whereas attached algae and aquatic macrophytes are particularly important in shallow waters.

![Primary Producers in Aquatic Ecosystem](images/15_primary_producers_in_aquatic_ecosystem.png)

## **7.4 Ecological Importance of Phytoplankton**

**Phytoplankton** are microscopic, predominantly photosynthetic organisms that drift in aquatic environments and act as primary producers.

### **Major Ecological Roles**

| Role | Importance |
|---|---|
| **Primary production** | Form the base of many marine food chains by producing organic matter through photosynthesis |
| **Carbon dioxide absorption** | Absorb dissolved CO₂ during photosynthesis, contributing to the ocean's role as a carbon sink |
| **Oxygen production** | Release oxygen during photosynthesis, contributing significantly to global oxygen production |
| **Biological carbon pump** | Help transfer carbon from surface waters to deeper ocean layers when organic matter sinks |

**Important:** The disappearance of phytoplankton would severely disrupt marine food chains and reduce biological carbon uptake and export, but would not directly cause a drastic decrease in ocean-water density.

# **8. Aquatic Grazing Food Chain**

An **aquatic grazing food chain** is a type of grazing food chain in which energy is transferred from **photosynthetic aquatic producers (phytoplankton)** to herbivorous consumers and subsequently to carnivorous consumers.

**Example:**

**Diatoms → Small crustaceans → Herrings**

| Organism | Trophic Level | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Diatoms** | First | Primary producers; photosynthetic phytoplankton |
| **Small crustaceans** (e.g., copepods) | Second | Primary consumers; feed on phytoplankton |
| **Herrings** | Third | Secondary consumers; feed on small crustaceans |

**Important UPSC Prelims Facts:**

- **Diatoms** are photosynthetic algae and act as primary producers in aquatic ecosystems.
- **Small crustaceans**, such as copepods, are important components of zooplankton.
- Arrows in a food chain indicate the **direction of energy transfer**, from food to consumer.
- **Aquatic grazing food chains are not a separate third category**; they are grazing food chains occurring in aquatic ecosystems.

# **9. Ecosystem Productivity**

## **9.1 Definition**

**Ecosystem productivity** is the rate of production of biomass or organic matter per unit area per unit time.

- **Productivity is a rate**, not the total biomass present.
- Common units: **g C/m²/year** or **g dry matter/m²/year**.

## **9.2 Primary Productivity**

**Primary productivity** is the rate at which autotrophs (primary producers) synthesize organic matter from inorganic substances.

It is classified into two types:

**A. Gross Primary Productivity (GPP)**

- Total rate of organic matter production by primary producers through photosynthesis, before deducting respiratory losses.

**B. Net Primary Productivity (NPP)**

- Rate of organic matter production remaining after subtracting the respiration of primary producers.
- Represents the net production of new plant biomass available for growth and consumption by other organisms.

$$
\boxed{\text{NPP}=\text{GPP}-R}
$$

Where **R = autotrophic respiration** (respiration by primary producers).

**Important:** GPP is greater than or equal to NPP because some of the organic matter produced is used in respiration.

## **9.3 Secondary Productivity**

**Secondary productivity** is the rate at which consumers (heterotrophs) produce new biomass from the organic matter they assimilate.

- Examples: Biomass production by zooplankton, insects, fish and herbivorous mammals.
- Unlike primary producers, consumers do not produce organic matter directly from inorganic carbon through photosynthesis.

## **9.4 Productivity, Standing Crop and Standing State**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Productivity** | Rate of biomass or organic matter production per unit area per unit time |
| **Standing crop** | Quantity of living biomass present in an ecosystem at a particular time |
| **Standing state** | Quantity of inorganic nutrients present in an ecosystem at a particular time |

**Key distinction:** Productivity measures **production over time**, whereas standing crop measures **biomass present at a particular time**.

## **9.5 Factors Affecting Primary Productivity**

Primary productivity depends mainly on:

1. **Solar radiation:** Availability of light for photosynthesis.
2. **Temperature:** Influences photosynthetic and metabolic processes.
3. **Water availability:** Particularly important in terrestrial ecosystems.
4. **Nutrient availability:** Essential nutrients such as nitrogen and phosphorus can limit productivity.
5. **Photosynthetic capacity:** Depends on the type, abundance and physiological characteristics of producers.

## **9.6 Productivity of Different Ecosystems**

Productivity comparisons must distinguish between:

- **Productivity per unit area:** Organic matter produced per unit area per unit time.
- **Total global productivity:** Total organic matter produced across the entire area occupied by an ecosystem.

| Ecosystem | General Primary Productivity per Unit Area |
|---|---|
| **Mangroves** | Generally very high |
| **Grasslands** | Moderate to high, depending on rainfall, temperature and nutrients |
| **Lakes** | Highly variable; nutrient-poor (oligotrophic) lakes have low productivity, while nutrient-rich (eutrophic) lakes can have high productivity |
| **Open oceans** | Generally low per unit area because nutrients limit productivity across large oceanic regions |

**Important distinctions:**

- Mangroves are generally highly productive ecosystems.
- Open oceans have relatively low productivity **per unit area**, but contribute enormously to **total global primary production** because of their vast area.
- The productivity of grasslands and lakes varies considerably; their relative ranking is not universally fixed.

**Note:** Oligotrophic lakes are nutrient-poor with low primary productivity, whereas eutrophic lakes are nutrient-rich with high primary productivity.

## **9.7 Marine Upwelling**

![Upwelling](images/27_upwelling.png)

**Marine upwelling** is the upward movement of deeper, generally colder and nutrient-rich ocean water towards the surface.

**How upwelling increases marine productivity:**

1. **Nutrient supply:** Nutrient-rich deep water reaches the sunlit surface layer (euphotic zone).
2. **Primary production:** Increased nutrient availability stimulates phytoplankton growth.
3. **Food-chain support:** Greater phytoplankton production supports zooplankton, fish and other marine organisms.

**Important Upwelling Regions:**

- **Peru–Chile coast:** Humboldt (Peru) Current system.
- **California coast:** California Current system.
- **Northwest African coast:** Canary Current system.
- **Southwest African coast:** Benguela Current system.

**Prelims fact:** Coastal upwelling is commonly driven by winds that transport surface water away from the coast, allowing deeper water to rise and replace it.

![Marine Upwelling](images/26_upwelling_zones.png)

## **9.8 Harmful Algal Blooms and Eutrophication**

### **9.8.1 Algal Blooms**

An **algal bloom** is a rapid increase in the abundance of algae or cyanobacteria in an aquatic ecosystem.

A **harmful algal bloom (HAB)** is a bloom that produces toxins or causes other harmful effects, such as oxygen depletion or damage to aquatic organisms.

### **9.8.2 Factors Promoting Algal Blooms**

1. **Nutrient discharge from estuaries:** Rivers and estuaries transport nitrogen, phosphorus and other nutrients into coastal waters.
2. **Land runoff during monsoons:** Agricultural fertilizers, sewage and other nutrient-rich materials enter aquatic ecosystems.
3. **Marine upwelling:** Nutrient-rich deep water rises to the surface, potentially stimulating phytoplankton growth.
4. **Favourable environmental conditions:** Light, temperature and water-column stability influence bloom development.

### **9.8.3 Eutrophication and Oxygen Depletion**

![Eutrophication](images/29_eutrophication.png)

**Eutrophication** is the enrichment of a water body with nutrients, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, which can stimulate excessive algal and aquatic plant growth.

**Process:**

1. Excess nutrients enter the water body.
2. Rapid algal growth may occur.
3. Algae die and their organic matter undergoes microbial decomposition.
4. Decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen.
5. Oxygen depletion may cause hypoxia, fish mortality and the formation of dead zones.

**Note:** Hypoxia is a medical condition in which your body's tissues do not receive enough oxygen to function properly..

### **9.8.4 Important Terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Eutrophication** | Enrichment of water with nutrients, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus |
| **Algal bloom** | Rapid increase in algae or cyanobacteria |
| **Harmful algal bloom (HAB)** | Bloom causing toxicity or other harmful effects |
| **Hypoxia** | Low dissolved oxygen concentration |
| **Anoxia** | Absence of dissolved oxygen |
| **Dead zone** | Aquatic region where oxygen depletion severely restricts many organisms |
| **Red tide** | Common term for certain marine algal blooms, often involving discoloration of water |

**Important distinctions:**

- Not all algal blooms are harmful.
- Not all harmful algal blooms are caused by human-induced nutrient enrichment.
- Upwelling can stimulate algal growth but does not necessarily cause harmful blooms.
- Not all harmful algal blooms are red, and not all red-coloured blooms are toxic.

# **10. Ecological Succession**

## **10.1 Definition**

**Ecological succession** is the gradual change in species composition and community structure in an area over time.

- It involves the establishment of species and changes in ecological communities.
- Succession may begin on a newly exposed surface or in an area previously occupied by an ecological community.

## **10.2 Primary and Secondary Succession**

| Feature | Primary Succession | Secondary Succession |
|---|---|---|
| **Starting condition** | Begins in an area without a previously established biological community | Begins in an area where a community existed but was disturbed |
| **Soil** | Often absent or poorly developed initially | Usually remains present |
| **Pioneer species** | Depend on habitat; lichens and mosses are common examples on bare rock | Often grasses, herbs and rapidly growing plants |
| **Rate** | Generally slower | Generally faster |
| **Example** | Colonization of newly exposed bare rock | Forest regeneration after a fire |

**Why is secondary succession generally faster?**

Soil, seeds, roots, microorganisms and other biological remnants may survive the disturbance, facilitating the re-establishment of vegetation.

## **10.3 Pioneer Species**

**Pioneer species** are the first species to colonize and establish themselves in a newly available habitat.

| Pioneer Species | Typical Habitat |
|---|---|
| **Lichens** | Bare rock |
| **Mosses** | Moist rock surfaces and areas with little developed soil |
| **Grasses and herbs** | Disturbed areas where soil remains |

Pioneer species can modify environmental conditions, making the habitat suitable for other organisms.

## **10.4 Succession on Bare Rock (Lithosere)**

**Lithosere** is ecological succession that begins on bare rock.

**Generalized sequence:**

> **Bare rock → Lichens → Mosses → Herbs and grasses → Shrubs → Trees**

**Process:**

1. **Lichens** colonize bare rock and contribute to rock weathering.
2. Weathered rock particles and accumulated organic matter gradually contribute to soil formation.
3. **Mosses** establish as moisture retention and soil conditions improve.
4. **Herbs, grasses and shrubs** may subsequently colonize.
5. **Trees** may establish if environmental conditions are suitable.

**Important:** This is a generalized succession sequence. The final vegetation depends on local environmental conditions and need not be a forest.

![Primary Succession](images/28_primary_succession.png)

![Secondary Succession](images/28_secondary_succession.png)

## **10.5 Lichens and Their Role in Succession**

A **lichen** is a symbiotic association between a fungus and a photosynthetic partner.

| Partner | Function |
|---|---|
| **Fungus (mycobiont)** | Provides structure, protection and helps obtain water and minerals |
| **Green alga or cyanobacterium (photobiont)** | Produces organic food through photosynthesis |

**Important UPSC Prelims Facts:**

- Lichens are conventionally described as an association between **algae and fungi**.
- The photosynthetic partner may be a **green alga or cyanobacterium**.
- Lichens can colonize bare rock and contribute to weathering and early soil formation.
- They are important pioneer organisms during primary succession on bare rock.

## **10.6 Organisms Surviving Without Developed Soil**

**Survival without soil does not necessarily mean that an organism is a pioneer species on bare rock.**

| Organism | Ability to Survive Without Developed Soil |
|---|---|
| **Lichens** | Can grow directly on bare rock and are common pioneer organisms |
| **Mosses** | Many species can grow on moist rocks without developed soil |
| **Ferns** | Certain species can grow on rocks or as epiphytes (on other plants) without mineral soil |
| **Mushrooms** | Can grow without mineral soil if suitable organic matter is available for nutrition |

**Important distinction:**

- **Lichens and mosses** are typical early colonizers of bare rock.
- **Some ferns and mushrooms** can also survive without developed mineral soil.
- Therefore, the ability to survive without soil is **not exclusive to lichens and mosses**.

## **10.7 Factors Determining the Course of Succession**

Ecological succession does not necessarily lead to the establishment of a forest.

The resulting vegetation depends on:

1. **Climate:** Temperature and rainfall influence which species can establish.
2. **Water availability:** Insufficient moisture may restrict tree growth.
3. **Soil conditions:** Soil depth, fertility and drainage influence vegetation.
4. **Fire:** Frequent fires may prevent the establishment of trees.
5. **Grazing:** Herbivores may suppress tree seedlings and help maintain grasslands.

**Example — Grasslands:**

Grasslands may persist because **water limitation and frequent fires** restrict tree establishment, while many grasses can survive and regenerate.

### Climax Community

In the traditional succession model, a **climax community** is a relatively stable community that develops during the later stages of succession.

However, ecological communities are not permanently fixed. Their composition can change because of disturbances and environmental changes.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

## **10.8 Types of Succession Based on Starting Habitat**

| Type | Starting Habitat |
|---|---|
| **Hydrarch succession** | Wet or aquatic habitat |
| **Xerarch succession** | Dry habitat |
| **Lithosere** | Bare rock |
| **Psammosere** | Sand |
| **Halosere** | Saline habitat |

**Classification distinction:**

- **Primary and secondary succession:** Based on whether a biological community previously occupied the site.
- **Hydrarch and xerarch succession:** Based on moisture conditions.
- **Lithosere, psammosere and halosere:** Based on the starting substrate or habitat.

# **11. Ecological Interactions**

**Ecological interactions** are relationships between organisms that affect their survival, growth or reproduction.

-----------------------------------------------------------------

## **11.1 Types of Ecological Interactions**

![Ecological Interaction Types](images/24_ecological_interaction_types.png)

| Interaction | Species A | Species B | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | + | + | Lichens: fungus and photosynthetic partner |
| **Commensalism** | + | 0 | Orchid growing on a tree |
| **Parasitism** | + | − | Tapeworm living in humans |
| **Predation** | + | − | Tiger feeding on deer |
| **Competition** | − | − | Plants competing for sunlight |
| **Amensalism** | − | 0 | *Penicillium* inhibiting bacterial growth |
| **Neutralism** | 0 | 0 | Species with no appreciable effect on each other |

**Symbols:**
- **+** Beneficial effect
- **−** Harmful effect
- **0** No appreciable effect

**Important distinctions:**

- **Mutualism vs commensalism:** Both species benefit in mutualism; only one benefits in commensalism, while the other is essentially unaffected.
- **Parasitism vs predation:** Parasites obtain resources from living hosts, generally without immediately killing them; predators capture and consume prey.
- **Competition:** Both species are negatively affected by competition for limited resources.
- **Neutralism:** A theoretical or approximate relationship in which neither species has an appreciable effect on the other; proving complete absence of interaction is difficult.

-----------------------------------------------------------------

## **11.2 Symbiosis**

**Symbiosis** is a close and persistent association between organisms belonging to different species.

In its broad biological meaning, symbiosis includes **mutualism, commensalism and parasitism**, depending on how the participating organisms are affected.

### **Types of Symbiosis**

| Type | Effect | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mutualism** | +/+ | Both species benefit | Mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots |
| **Commensalism** | +/0 | One species benefits; the other is essentially unaffected | Orchid growing on a tree |
| **Parasitism** | +/− | One species benefits at the expense of the other | Tapeworm living in humans |

**Note:** Predation, competition and amensalism are ecological interactions but are generally not classified as symbiosis.

### **Examples of Symbiotic Associations in Different Groups**

| Group | Symbiotic Association | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **Cnidarians** | Corals and photosynthetic dinoflagellates (zooxanthellae) | Mutualism |
| **Fungi** | Mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots | Mutualism |
| **Protozoa** | Certain flagellated protozoa and termites | Mutualism |
| **Bacteria** | Rhizobia and leguminous plants | Mutualism |
| **Lichens** | Fungus and green alga or cyanobacterium | Traditionally classified as mutualism |
| **Epiphytic plants** | Orchid growing on a tree | Commensalism |
| **Parasitic worms** | Tapeworm living in humans | Parasitism |
| **Parasitic plants** | *Cuscuta* growing on a host plant | Parasitism |

### **Important Points for UPSC Prelims**

- **Symbiosis does not necessarily mean mutualism.** It may involve mutualism, commensalism or parasitism.
- **Cnidarians, fungi and protozoa** all include species capable of forming symbiotic relationships.
- **Mycorrhiza:** Mutualistic association between fungi and plant roots.

- **Rhizobium–legume association:** Bacteria fix atmospheric nitrogen, while the plant supplies nutrients and a suitable environment.
- **Orchid–tree association:** The orchid obtains physical support without normally harming the tree.

## **11.3 Predation, Parasitism and Parasitoids**

### **11.3.1 Predation**

**Predation (+/−)** is an ecological interaction in which one organism (**predator**) captures and consumes another organism (**prey**), usually killing it.

**Examples:**
- Tiger feeding on deer.
- Spider capturing insects.
- Ladybird beetle feeding on aphids.

**Ecological significance:** Predation helps regulate prey populations and maintain ecological balance.

### **11.3.2 Parasitism**

**Parasitism (+/−)** is an ecological interaction in which one organism (**parasite**) obtains nourishment or other resources from another living organism (**host**), benefiting itself while harming the host.

Parasites generally maintain a prolonged association with their hosts and do not necessarily kill them.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

**Types of Parasites (Based on Location)**

| Type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Ectoparasites** | Live on the external surface of the host | Lice, ticks |
| **Endoparasites** | Live inside the host's body | Tapeworm, *Plasmodium* |

**Other examples of parasitism:**
- ***Cuscuta* (dodder):** A parasitic plant that obtains water and nutrients from its host plant.
- ***Rafflesia*:** A parasitic flowering plant that obtains resources from its host vine.

### **11.3.3 Parasitoids**

A **parasitoid** is an organism whose immature stage (usually a larva) develops in or on a living host, feeds on it and eventually kills the host as part of its normal development.

**Example — Parasitoid wasp:**

1. An adult female wasp lays eggs in or on an insect host.
2. The eggs hatch, and the larvae feed on the host.
3. The developing larvae eventually kill the host.
4. The parasitoid completes its development and emerges as an adult.

**Types of Parasitoids**

| Type | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Endoparasitoid** | Develops inside the host's body |
| **Ectoparasitoid** | Develops externally on the host's body |

### **11.3.4 Predators vs Parasites vs Parasitoids**

| Feature | Predator | Parasite | Parasitoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mode of feeding** | Captures and consumes prey | Obtains resources from a living host | Immature stage feeds on a living host |
| **Effect on prey/host** | Usually kills prey | Harms host; death is not inevitable | Eventually kills host |
| **Duration of association** | Usually brief | Often prolonged | Prolonged during immature development |
| **Example** | Spider feeding on an insect | Tapeworm in humans | Parasitoid wasp developing in a caterpillar |

**Key distinction:** Predators usually kill prey during feeding; parasites may or may not kill their hosts; parasitoids characteristically kill their hosts during development.

<div style="page-break-after: always;"></div>

### **11.3.5 Important Parasitoid Groups**

Parasitoid species are particularly common among certain **wasps and flies**, but also occur among some beetles.

| Organism Group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Wasps** | Ichneumonid and braconid wasps |
| **Flies** | Tachinid flies |
| **Beetles** | Certain carabid beetles, such as *Lebia* |

**Important:** Not all wasps, flies or beetles are parasitoids. Only certain species within these groups exhibit parasitoid behaviour.

**Ecological significance:** Parasitoids help regulate insect populations, and many are used as **biological control agents** against agricultural pests.

## **11.4 Fig–Fig Wasp Relationship**

## **11.5 Fungus Cultivation by Ants and Termites**

Certain species of **ants and termites cultivate fungi for food**. This association is an example of **mutualism (+/+)**, because both the insects and the cultivated fungi benefit.

### **11.5.1 Fungus-Cultivating Ants and Termites**

| Feature | Fungus-Cultivating Ants | Fungus-Cultivating Termites |
|---|---|---|
| **Examples** | Leafcutter ants (*Atta*, *Acromyrmex*) | Fungus-growing termites (subfamily Macrotermitinae) |
| **Cultivated fungi** | Mainly *Leucoagaricus* and related fungi | *Termitomyces* |
| **Cultivation method** | Collect plant material and use it to maintain fungal gardens | Construct fungus combs using processed plant material |
| **Benefit to insects** | Fungi provide food | Fungi help break down plant material and provide nutrients |
| **Benefit to fungi** | Receive plant material, protection and favourable growing conditions | Receive plant material, protection and favourable growing conditions |

### **11.5.2 How Leafcutter Ants Cultivate Fungi**

1. **Collection:** Worker ants cut and collect leaves and other plant material.
2. **Cultivation:** They prepare the collected material as a growing medium for fungi in underground gardens.
3. **Feeding:** Ants feed on the cultivated fungus, particularly its specialized nutrient-rich structures.
4. **Maintenance:** Workers maintain the gardens, remove contaminants and protect the fungal crop.

**Important:** Leafcutter ants do not primarily collect leaves to eat them directly; they use the leaves to cultivate their fungal food source.

## **11.6. Pollination and Its Ecological Importance**

### **11.6.1 Meaning of Pollination**

**Pollination** is the transfer of pollen grains from the **anther** (pollen-producing part of the stamen) to the **stigma** (pollen-receiving part of the pistil) of a flower.

- Pollination is a step in the sexual reproduction of flowering plants.
- Successful pollination may lead to fertilization, followed by seed and fruit development.

### **11.6.2 Types of Pollination**

| Type | Meaning | Genetic Significance |
|---|---|---|
| **Self-pollination** | Transfer of pollen to the stigma of the same flower or another flower on the same plant | Generally produces less genetic variation |
| **Cross-pollination** | Transfer of pollen between flowers of different plants of the same species | Promotes gene flow and genetic variation |

**Important:** Self-pollination does not always mean pollination within the same flower. It also includes pollen transfer between different flowers on the same plant.

### **11.6.3 Agents of Pollination**

Pollination may occur through **biotic agents** (living organisms) or **abiotic agents** (non-living factors).

| Category | Pollinating Agent | Term | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Biotic** | Insects | Entomophily | Bees, butterflies, moths, beetles |
| **Biotic** | Birds | Ornithophily | Sunbirds, hummingbirds |
| **Biotic** | Bats | Chiropterophily | Nectar-feeding bats |
| **Abiotic** | Wind | Anemophily | Grasses, maize |
| **Abiotic** | Water | Hydrophily | *Vallisneria*, *Zostera* |

**Important distinctions:**

- **Animal pollination:** Animals transfer pollen between flowers, often while collecting nectar or pollen.
- **Wind pollination:** Air currents transport pollen; flowers are often inconspicuous and produce abundant, lightweight pollen.
- **Water pollination:** Water transports pollen or pollen-bearing structures. It occurs in relatively few flowering plants.

**Prelims fact:** Most aquatic flowering plants are **not** pollinated by water. Many have flowers above the water surface and are pollinated by insects or wind.

![Pollination](images/33_pollination.png)

### **11.6.4 Important Plant–Pollinator Associations**

| Plant | Important Pollinators | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Fig (*Ficus*)** | Specialized fig wasps | Highly specialized pollination mutualism |
| **Mahua (*Madhuca longifolia*)** | Flower-visiting insects and bats | Animal-mediated pollination |
| **Sandalwood (*Santalum album*)** | Insects, including bees | Primarily insect-pollinated |
| **Silk cotton (*Bombax ceiba*)** | Birds and bats, among other floral visitors | Large, nectar-rich flowers attract animal visitors |

**Fig–fig wasp mutualism:**

- Fig wasps transfer pollen between the flowers enclosed within fig structures.
- Pollinating female wasps enter receptive figs and, in suitable flowers, lay eggs.
- The fig provides a site for wasp reproduction, while the wasps facilitate fig pollination.
- This association is a classic example of **mutualism and coevolution**.

**Important:** Many fig species have specialized pollinating wasps, but a strict one-fig-species–one-wasp-species relationship is not universal.

### **11.6.5 Ecological Importance of Pollination**

1. **Plant reproduction:** Enables pollen transfer necessary for fertilization and subsequent seed and fruit formation.
2. **Biodiversity conservation:** Supports the reproduction and persistence of flowering plant populations.
3. **Food webs:** Helps sustain plant populations that provide food and habitats for other organisms.
4. **Agriculture:** Supports the yield and quality of many fruits, vegetables, nuts and oilseeds.
5. **Genetic diversity:** Cross-pollination facilitates gene flow between plants and can increase genetic variation.

### **11.6.6 Pollination vs Fertilization**

| Feature  | Pollination                                                           | Fertilization                                                           |
| -------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Meaning  | Transfer of pollen grains from the anther to the stigma               | Fusion of male and female gametes                                       |
| Location | Pollen reaches the stigma of a flower                                 | Occurs inside the ovule, within the ovary                               |
| Process  | Pollen is transferred by agents such as insects, birds, wind or water | A male gamete delivered through the pollen tube fuses with the egg cell |
| Sequence | Occurs before fertilization                                           | Occurs after pollination and pollen-tube growth                         |
| Result   | Pollen is deposited on the stigma                                     | A zygote is formed                                                      |

Important UPSC Prelims facts:

- Pollination is not fertilization. It is the transfer of pollen, not the fusion of gametes.
- Pollination does not guarantee fertilization. Pollen must be compatible and successfully deliver male gametes.
- Double fertilization, a characteristic feature of flowering plants (angiosperms), involves two fusions: one male gamete fuses with the egg to form a zygote (syngamy), while the other fuses with two polar nuclei to form the primary endosperm nucleus (triple fusion).

## **11.7 Ecological Importance of Butterflies**

Butterflies play important roles in ecosystems during different stages of their life cycle.

1. **Pollination:** Adult butterflies transfer pollen between flowers, helping certain flowering plants reproduce.
2. **Herbivory:** Caterpillars (butterfly larvae) feed on plants and form part of the food chain.
3. **Food-web support:** Butterflies and caterpillars serve as prey for certain birds and spiders and as hosts for some parasitoid wasps.

**Consequences of butterfly population decline:**

- Reduced pollination of plants that depend on butterflies.
- Reduced food availability for certain predators and host availability for parasitoids.
- Disruption of food-web relationships.

## **11.9 Animal Communication: Honeybee Dances**

**Animal communication** is the exchange of information between animals through signals such as sounds, movements, chemical substances and visual displays.

Honeybees communicate information about food sources to other members of their colony through characteristic movements called **bee dances**.

### **Types of Honeybee Dances**

Honeybees primarily use two types of dances to communicate information about food sources.

| Feature | Waggle Dance | Round Dance |
|---|---|---|
| **Food source** | Generally farther from the hive | Generally close to the hive |
| **Movement** | Figure-eight pattern with a central waggle run | Circular movements, alternating directions |
| **Direction** | Indicates direction relative to the Sun | Does not indicate a precise direction |
| **Distance** | Indicates approximate distance | Does not indicate precise distance |

### **Important Facts**

- **Waggle dance:** Communicates both the direction and approximate distance of a food source.
  - **Direction:** The angle of the waggle run relative to vertical on a vertical comb indicates the direction of the food source relative to the Sun.
  - **Distance:** The duration of the waggle run conveys approximate distance; longer waggle runs generally indicate greater distances.
- **Round dance:** Signals the presence of a nearby food source without specifying its precise direction or distance.
- **Floral odours:** Help recruited bees recognize and locate the food source.
- **Karl von Frisch:** Deciphered honeybee dance communication and shared the **1973 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine** with Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen.

# **12. Ecological Adaptation**

**Ecological adaptations** are inherited structural, physiological or behavioural characteristics that improve an organism's survival or reproductive success under particular environmental conditions.

![Ecological Adaptations](images/35_ecological_adaptations.png)

## **12.1 Camouflage**

**Camouflage** is an adaptation that makes an organism difficult to detect or recognize in its surroundings. It helps organisms avoid predators or approach prey without being detected.

Camouflage may involve **body coloration, patterns, shape or behaviour**.

### **12.1.1 Types of Camouflage**

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Background matching** | The organism's body colour or pattern resembles its surroundings, making it difficult to distinguish from the background. | Green grasshopper among green leaves |
| **Disruptive coloration** | Stripes, spots or contrasting patterns break up the organism's visible body outline, making its shape difficult to recognize. | Tiger's stripes among vegetation |
| **Masquerade** | The organism resembles an object, such as a twig or leaf, so that it is mistaken for that object. | Stick insect resembling a twig |

**Protective coloration:** A general term for body colours or patterns that help conceal an organism. It includes **background matching and disruptive coloration**, rather than being a separate, mutually exclusive type.

### **12.1.2 Animal Stripes and Camouflage**

- **Tigers:** Stripes help break up the body's visible outline in vegetation, contributing to camouflage.
- **Zebras:** Stripes help reduce biting-fly landings; camouflage is not established as their primary function.

## **12.2 Ecological Tolerance**

**Ecological tolerance** is the ability of an organism to survive and function within a particular range of environmental conditions, such as temperature, salinity, moisture or pH.

### **12.2.1 Shelford's Law of Tolerance**

Proposed by **Victor E. Shelford (1913)**.

The distribution and abundance of organisms are influenced by their tolerance limits for environmental factors.

For each environmental factor, an organism generally has:

- **Minimum tolerance limit:** Lower limit beyond which it cannot survive.
- **Optimum range:** Conditions most favourable for growth, survival and reproduction.
- **Maximum tolerance limit:** Upper limit beyond which it cannot survive.

### **12.2.2 Important Ecological Tolerance Terms**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Eurythermal** | Tolerates a wide range of temperatures |
| **Stenothermal** | Tolerates a narrow range of temperatures |
| **Euryhaline** | Tolerates a wide range of salinities |
| **Stenohaline** | Tolerates a narrow range of salinities |

**Remember:**

- **Eury :** means wide tolerance.
- **Steno :** means narrow tolerance.
- **thermal :** refers to temperature.
- **haline :** refers to salinity.

---

## **12.3 Liebig's Law of the Minimum**

Associated with **Justus von Liebig**.

**Liebig's Law of the Minimum** states that growth is limited by the essential resource that is most deficient relative to an organism's requirements, rather than by the total quantity of available resources.

**Example:** If a plant has adequate sunlight, water and other nutrients but insufficient nitrogen, its growth may be limited by nitrogen availability.

### **12.3.1 Liebig's Law vs Shelford's Law**

| Feature | Liebig's Law of the Minimum | Shelford's Law of Tolerance |
|---|---|---|
| **Associated scientist** | Justus von Liebig | Victor E. Shelford |
| **Main principle** | Growth may be limited by the essential resource in shortest relative supply | Organisms have minimum and maximum tolerance limits for environmental factors |
| **Key emphasis** | Resource deficiency | Both deficiency and excess of environmental factors can be limiting |

**Important distinction:** Liebig's Law emphasizes the limitation caused by insufficient essential resources, whereas Shelford's Law recognizes that environmental conditions can become limiting when they are either too low or too high.
